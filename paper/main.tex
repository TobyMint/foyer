\title{When Does Admission Control Pay?\\
The Memory Tier Sets the Cost of Concurrency in Agentic LLM Serving}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Agentic LLM sessions are long, multi-round jobs whose KV footprint grows every
round, and on a memory-constrained accelerator the pool is the binding
resource. Admission control therefore looks like the obvious lever: a
controller that reads engine telemetry and admits on measured capacity should
beat a static cap. We give a measurement that says how much
room there is, and show what happens when you build against it. Throughput during busy time is
$\overline{R}/\tau$---running requests over per-step cost---so raising
concurrency by a factor $f$ buys $f^{1-\epsilon}$, where
$\epsilon = \mathrm{d}\ln\tau / \mathrm{d}\ln\overline{R}$. Near zero, busy-time throughput keeps
converting; near one it does not. It is a property of the workload \emph{and}
the engine configuration, not of the controller, and what sets it is the
memory tier. The trace bounds reuse at 75.5\% of its prompt tokens, and no
configuration we ran reaches that bound---the best realizes 92.6\% of it.
What the tier decides is how much of the bound survives added concurrency:
device-only, realization falls from 92.6\% to 36.3\% as the running count
rises from 1.00 to 1.84 and the shortfall appears as recomputation (8.6\,M to
20.7\,M tokens); with a host tier it holds at 90.2--92.3\% between 1.25 and
1.73, while the \emph{device} cache misses more. Two configurations at the
same running count and the same pool occupancy realize 78.9\% and 91.1\% of
the bound, so it is not how full the pool is but what happens when it
overflows---though that pair is two runs on two days, which is why we also
vary the pool size alone on one card as a capacity control. Our controllers run on the host-tier configuration, where
$\epsilon$ stays below one across their whole operating range, so the ceiling
we measure on device-only caps is not what bounded them. It bounds what added concurrency is worth, not what a
whole run achieves---idle time and the SLO axis are separate. We measure $\epsilon$ on a
memory-bound agentic replay (200 sessions, one 24\,GB GPU, a pool that holds
four median sessions) and get $0.38$ to $0.61$ depending on which step cost a
system can observe: every 10\% of added concurrency buys 4--6\% of throughput.

The verdict that criterion returns here is negative, and we report it at that
strength. Nine controller variants spanning four routes, plus a
reimplementation of an independent congestion-based controller, none is
above the static Pareto front at the latency threshold interactive serving
cares about---the best misses it by under a point, on the below side of our
tie band, and its repeat misses by 5.6; a plain cap on resident sessions
remains the fastest policy we measured. That is not an argument for admitting everything: with no control at
all the median request waits 1{,}774 seconds for its first token, the prefix
cache's realized hit rate collapses from the trace's planned 75.5\% to
1.6\%, and SLO falls to 3\%.
The question is which selective rule, not whether to be selective. Two
measurable effects, rather than one we can tune away, account for part of why
our variants fail---the arm-by-arm attribution of
\S\ref{sec:results:attribution} covers four of the eleven arms we tested and
leaves seven as residuals we can measure and cannot yet explain---and every
one of those four attributions that names a step-cost cause rests on runs
measured under host co-tenancy, so we flag it rather than count it. They hold
nearly half
the pool idle while sessions queue---their own headroom estimate on the base
configuration is 1.79$\times$ the truth (1.0--4.1$\times$ across all of them)---and the per-step elasticity measured on the
configuration they actually run stays below one across their whole operating
range, so the engine had room they did not take: the ceiling we measure on
device-only caps does not survive on the host-tier configuration the
controllers use. The wall
clock splits into busy and idle time; admission policy moves only the running
count, and $\tau$ rises with it. The idle component (7.4--36.3\% of the
sampled span) comes from gaps the tool waits set---an agent session issues no
request while a tool runs,
and that wait averages 16.85\,s, not the sub-second figure the trace's median
suggests---though how many such gaps a run collects is partly the policy's,
since a policy sets how many sessions stay live. On device-only configurations we can account for the rise token for
token; on host-tier ones part of it is co-tenancy we found late and part we
still cannot attribute, and we say which is which. We also report the six of
our own claims we retracted along the way, and the boundary of the verdict: at
a loose latency threshold one variant does cross the front, by a margin we
cannot resolve against run-to-run noise.
\end{abstract}
