\section{Results: Four Routes, One Outcome}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} places every completed run on the (wall clock, SLO)
plane and draws the upper-left envelope of the static points. Table~\ref{tab:runs}
gives the numbers and each controller's signed distance from that envelope.

The result is unambiguous. Of nine controller variants, none is above the
envelope. The best, a growth-reserve setting of one round instead of the
default three, lands $+0.22$ percentage points above the line. We ran it
again, and the repeat lands at $-4.57$. Same code, same trace, same pool,
guarded the same way: 260.8 minutes against 255.8, and 80.9\% SLO against
84.8\%. \emph{The one arm that reached the front does not reproduce reaching it}, and
the two runs of that single configuration differ in signed distance by
4.8\,pp---one a tie, the other clearly below.

That is not an isolated unlucky pair. Four configurations have been run more
than once, and while their wall clocks are stable across repeats---coefficients
of variation of 0.9\%, 1.0\%, 1.6\% and 1.8\%---their SLO is not: the
ranges are 3.9, 5.1, 1.0 and 0.5\,pp. The two widest belong to configurations
that \emph{decide} something---the leftmost envelope anchor and the best
controller arm---so the front we compare against, and the best result we report
against it, are both uncertain at a scale larger than the $\pm1.0$\,pp band
we judge with. A fifth pair, two runs of \texttt{cap3}+HiCache, we do not use:
one of them exceeded its own cap. The widest
of those belongs to an \emph{envelope anchor}: \texttt{cap4}+HiCache scores
63.4\% on one run and 68.5\% on its repeat. So the front we measure against
is not a sharp line, and our $\pm1.0$\,pp verdict band is narrower than the
uncertainty of the points that define it. Against that, the best controller's
$+0.22$\,pp is not merely inside the noise---it is smaller than the noise of
the anchors by an order of magnitude. We record it as \emph{on} the line
rather than above it, and we state plainly that resolving a margin this small
would need several repeats per configuration, which we did not run.

The same repeat moved the frontier and changed nothing. The leftmost anchor's
second run came out both faster (209.1 against 213.0 minutes) and higher on SLO
(68.5 against 63.4), so it displaces the first as the envelope's leftmost
point. Not one controller verdict moves: every one of them finishes to the
right of the next anchor at 236 minutes, so none of them is judged against the
segment that changed. We report both runs rather than keeping the better one,
because a frontier whose anchors are single measurements is the weakness this
whole subsection is about.

The other end of the front deserves its own sentence, because the rest of this
paper is negative and a reader could close it thinking the answer is to do
nothing. The run with \emph{no} admission control---every session admitted the
moment it arrives---finishes in 288.6 minutes at 3.0\% SLO, $87.5$\,pp below
the envelope and the worst SLO we report. Its median
time-to-first-token is 1{,}774 seconds, its prefix cache hit rate collapses from
the runner's reported planned 75.5\%---the all-steps figure, a different
convention from the round-1+ numbers in \S\ref{sec:related}---to 1.6\%, and its segment SLO is 30\% in the first
tenth of the run and exactly zero in each of the nine after that. Uncontrolled
admission does not degrade gracefully; it destroys the cache and then serves
almost nobody. The question this paper addresses is therefore not whether to
admit selectively, but which selective rule to use.

Two features of the front are worth noting before the negative results.
The envelope is steep between 213 and 236 minutes (0.76\,pp per minute) and
shallow thereafter (0.18\,pp per minute), so the cost of buying SLO by
retreating from the fast end is high. And $W$ is not monotone in concurrency:
five sessions (260.9\,min) is \emph{slower} than four (249.9\,min)---the same
configuration family, no host cache on either side---because the per-step cost
rises faster than the request count does. We return to this
non-monotonicity in \S\ref{sec:model}, where it turns out to be the first
appearance of the mechanism that drives the whole frontier.

\subsection{The controllers leave memory unused}
\label{sec:results:idle-memory}

Before the routes, one measurement that reframes all of them. Our controller
keeps every session's permit for its lifetime, so its pool occupancy is a
policy output, not a constraint. It runs at a median of 49.2\% of the pool on
the base configuration, and the median number of sessions queueing for
admission is 66. The configuration that finishes fastest---the repeat of that same static cap
of four, at 209.1 minutes---runs at 71.9\%, and its first run at 70.2\%. The ordering is the same across every arm
we have: occupancy, then running requests, then wall clock.

The controller believes it is nearly full. Its own decision log records the
projection it admits against---the largest of measured occupancy and the summed
contexts of live sessions, plus a reserve for their growth---at a median of
89{,}073 tokens against a budget of 96{,}360, i.e.\ 7\% free. Its measured
occupancy at the same instants is 49{,}905 tokens: 48.2\% of that budget, or
50.8\% of the pool, is idle. The
projection is 1.79$\times$ the truth. Two terms produce the gap: charging every
live session its full context, when a parked session's KV is evictable and
therefore absent from the engine's count, and the growth reserve, which alone
is 26{,}762 tokens, 28\% of the budget.

Two things follow, and we are careful about the second.

The first is that the controllers under-use the pool, and that this is a
property of their estimates rather than of the hardware: the base
configuration holds a fifth of the pool idle with 66 sessions queued, because
it believes it has 7\% free when it has 46\%. Any account of why it is slow
that does not mention this is incomplete.

The second is that under-use does \emph{not} order the outcomes, so it is not
the whole explanation either. \texttt{horizon}=1 and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache sit
at the same median occupancy (57.8\% of the pool) and the same mean running
count (1.31 against 1.36), and the cap is 19 minutes faster. \texttt{hz0} runs
at 64.1\%, higher than that cap, and is 54 minutes slower. Occupancy buys
running requests reliably---the two columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} track
each other across all twenty arms---but converting running requests into wall
clock is where the per-step cost of \S\ref{sec:model} does its work. Both
effects are real; on this workload the unused-memory one is larger for the arms
that finish worst, and the step-cost one is what separates the arms that finish
close together.

\subsection{What the two mechanisms explain, arm by arm}
\label{sec:results:attribution}

We have offered two mechanisms, and the honest thing is to say what each one
covers. For every controller arm we took the static cap closest to it in wall
clock and compared their occupancy and their step cost. An arm is attributed to
the unused-memory mechanism if it runs meaningfully fewer requests than its
comparison, and to the step-cost mechanism if it runs a comparable number at a
measurably higher cost. Everything else is a residual.

\subsection{Route 1: the accounting base}

Our controller's projection (Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) charges a session for its full
context even while it is parked in a tool wait, when its KV is evictable and
therefore absent from the engine's reported occupancy. We argued this was a
double charge and removed it, admitting on engine telemetry alone.

The charge is not double. Removing it did admit more---engine occupancy rose
from 41.0\% to 52.2\% of the pool---and the round-1+ prefix hit rate held at
80.8\% against 80.6\%, so no eviction spiral occurred. But the run was
4.2 minutes \emph{slower} and lost 6.0\,pp of SLO. Per \S\ref{sec:model}, the
extra occupancy raised both $\overline{R}$ and $\tau$ (31.3\,ms against
23.8\,ms), and the two cancelled.

We note that this claim had already been falsified once by source inspection:
SGLang's \texttt{token\_usage} subtracts evictable blocks, so a parked
session's prefix is genuinely not counted, and charging for it reserves
capacity that really will be needed back. The end-to-end measurement agrees
with the source reading.

\subsection{Route 2: the growth reserve}

Sessions grow by roughly 2.5K tokens per round, so the controller reserves
capacity for the next \texttt{horizon} rounds. We swept the horizon over
$\{0,1,2,3\}$ and the reserve is not a free parameter to be minimised:
Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} shows the response is non-monotone. Removing the
reserve entirely costs 12.6\,pp of SLO, and a reserve of two rounds is
15 minutes \emph{slower} than three. The optimum is at one round, and there it
touches the static front and stops.

\subsection{Route 3: the hard ceiling}

Lowering the budget fraction is the crudest available knob and behaves as the
other routes do: it slides the operating point along the front. We report it
only for completeness, since it adds no mechanism.

\subsection{An independent adaptive controller}
\label{sec:results:aimd}

Every controller above is ours, which invites the objection that the negative
result is a statement about one design rather than about adaptive admission.
We therefore implemented a second, mechanistically different controller: an
AIMD window over an agent-level admission set, following the control law of
the congestion-based system we compare against in \S\ref{sec:related}. The
window grows additively while cache occupancy sits below a low watermark, is
cut multiplicatively when occupancy exceeds a high watermark \emph{and} the
prefix hit rate falls below a threshold, and holds otherwise. Cuts pause the
youngest sessions at their next step boundary; growth resumes paused sessions
first. Every input is a runtime gauge---occupancy and hit rate from the
engine, nothing from the trace---and the window acts through the same named
permits as \sys{}.

It does not beat the static front either, at either parameter setting
(Table~\ref{tab:runs}). At the values reported in that paper's evaluation it
finishes in 364.3 minutes at 81.4\% SLO, $11.2$\,pp below the envelope; the
same arm with its retuned watermarks finishes in 264.6 minutes at 52.8\%,
$33.4$\,pp below. The retuned setting is also one of the most
volatile runs we report: its segment-to-segment SD is 13.2\,pp of SLO, with
segment values swinging between 37 and 72. Three runs are worse (\texttt{cap4}
at 15.8, the all-throttles-off arm at 14.4, and \texttt{breshz1} at 14.2), and
no other controller arm is as bad---which is what a control loop oscillating
around a capacity cliff looks like.

We report two things honestly about this comparison. First, this is our
reimplementation of a published control law, not the authors' system, and a
reimplementation of any controller can underperform its paper. We built it to
the published law, gave it the same permit interface and the same 2\,s cycle
as \sys{}, and did not tune it beyond the two documented settings; but the
caveat stands. Second, both controllers stall. At its paper parameters this one pinned its
window at one session, admitted nothing, and left the engine idle for over an
hour. Our own controller does the same thing more briefly: in 4 of the 11
\sys{} runs an audit found a single window of 150--175\,s in which the engine
was empty, the queue held ~90 sessions, the SLO valve was open, and the
controller issued no permit at all---about 0.9\% of each run's wall clock. The
remaining runs, including the best one, show no such window. Nothing is logged
as an error and the run recovers on its own.

We traced one such window in a repeat of our best configuration and found a
code path that explains it: single-flight admission considered only the head of
the FIFO queue, and when the head was a session too large to fit the remaining
budget it was never admitted, never left the queue, and blocked every smaller
session behind it. We fixed that (scan the queue, still admit at most one per
cycle), but we are explicit that this is \emph{a} path, not a proven cause:
the audit is a log signature, and the same signature can be produced by other
closed gates. What we can say is that the signature is present in a third of
our \sys{} runs and absent from all of our static caps, which have no
admission decision to get stuck in.

That is the honest version of a finding we first reported too strongly. An
intermediate draft of this paper called it a deadlock and said the
configuration that produced our best result was the one that deadlocked on its
repeat. Neither holds: the windows are ~160\,s and self-clearing, and the best
configuration never showed one. The windows are also too small (0.9\%) to move
any verdict we report. We keep them because the mechanism is real and its size
is the point: on a pool that holds four median sessions, a controller's failure
mode is a silent stall, not graceful degradation, and it took a repeat run to
see it.

\subsection{Route 4: execution granularity}
\label{sec:results:gate}

The first three routes all assume that what a controller can set is \emph{how
many sessions are resident}. A static cap sets the same quantity, so it is
unsurprising that they land in the same place. The fourth route attacks the
assumption instead: because a large fraction of live sessions are parked at
any instant, a cap on \emph{resident} sessions leaves the engine idle whenever
all residents happen to be parked. Capping the \emph{running-request} count
instead should keep the engine busy while leaving the resident count high.

The permit design supports this directly: a paused session finishes its
current round and blocks before its next one, and resumes when the controller
re-admits it. We implemented it as a gate on the engine's own
\texttt{num\_running\_reqs} and swept the gate over $\{2,4\}$.

It does not work, and the reason is structural rather than a tuning matter.
Since a live session is running about 45\% of the time, the running count is
approximately $0.45 \times (\text{live count})$. Constraining the live count to
$N$ therefore constrains the running count to $0.45N$, which is below the
gate---so the gate can only throttle, never pin. Both repairs fail: without a
constraint on admission, a paused session is re-admitted on the next cycle and
blocks for at most one 2\,s cycle; with one, the policy is a session cap again.
The obstacle is granularity---\texttt{paused} takes effect at round
boundaries, while pinning an instantaneous count requires control the
permit mechanism does not have.

The measured outcome matches: the gate drove running concurrency from 1.42 to
1.29 and live sessions from 2.78 to 1.91. It tightened rather than pinned, and
a tightening is another cap.
