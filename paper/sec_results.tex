\section{Results: Four Routes, One Outcome}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} places every completed run on the (wall clock, SLO)
plane and draws the upper-left envelope of the static points; Table~\ref{tab:runs}
gives the numbers and each controller's distance from it. The result is
the same for the nine policy variants of the controller we deployed, and all nine
are clean measurements: \emph{none} of their runs is above the envelope at the
$10$\,s threshold. The \emph{best} is \texttt{horizon}=2, run
twice: $-0.30$ and $+0.22$\,pp, either side of the line and both inside our
$\pm1.0$\,pp band. (Best, not closest: \texttt{single-flight off}'s repeat reads
$+0.13$, nearer the line, but its reported run is $-0.55$.) Against the discrete
static points rather than the envelope between them, that arm is \emph{not
dominated}: every static configuration faster than it scores lower on SLO, and
every one that scores higher is slower. We take the envelope as the baseline, and
\S\ref{sec:bg} states what that choice costs us.

Two points in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} are above the line, and they are one arm:
two of its three rows of Table~\ref{tab:runs}, \texttt{scans the queue} and its
repeat, neither of them one of the nine. All three are the same controller
revision and flags as the base's own repeat, with exactly one expression changed:
that revision releases only the \emph{head} of the waiting queue unless single-flight is
explicitly disabled, and this arm scans the whole queue and releases the first
session that fits it. Their logs show
the change to be narrow---one admission per cycle, never two---and the source
differs in that expression plus a comment block; the launcher differs too and we
cannot diff its earlier revision (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).
The three measure $+1.30$, $+2.16$ and $+0.29$\,pp: two clear the band by $0.30$
and $1.16$\,pp, the third does not. Against the $0.5$ to $4.6$\,pp that
separates repeats of a single controller configuration in our set (the spread of
$\Delta$; \texttt{horizon}=2's own two runs are the tightest of the five groups at
$0.5$\,pp) the arm's own three span $1.87$\,pp---more than the band's $1.0$\,pp
half-width and straddling its edge, its first and third $1.01$\,pp apart---so two of
three, not a stable crossing.
The first's wall clock is $26.9$ minutes ($9.2\%$) below the base's repeat at
$1.0$\,pp lower SLO. The other clock, which charges the arm for the permit wait it
imposes, leaves none of the three above the line: $-0.56$, $+0.96$ and
$-1.07$\,pp. Nor is it simply that scanning the queue
wins---one of the nine, \texttt{single-flight off}, scans it too, landing
$0.55$\,pp \emph{below} the line on its first run and $+0.13$\,pp on its repeat.
These establish the reach of the negative result: the readings above the line
come from the \emph{release} rule, not from the budget rule admission acts on.

The base configuration's repeats are tight: four runs spanning 0.5\,pp of SLO
around an 89.5\% mean---though not one build, since one is a later controller
revision and one ran on the other card, so 0.5\,pp is the spread we observed, not
a clean estimate of one revision's noise. Against the 3.5\,pp separating it from \texttt{single-flight
off} (89.8\% against 86.3\%; \S\ref{sec:repro} says which run)---several times its
own noise, and a trade rather than a win: 28 minutes of wall clock for 3.5\,pp of
SLO.

Four of the nine carried the co-tenancy flag of Table~\ref{tab:runs} and were
re-measured alone (\S\ref{sec:repro}); we do not read them as
agreeing---one contamination window, not four independent replications---and even
the largest correction leaves its arm below the line.\footnote{The repeats are uneven, and we do not want the tightest pair to stand
for all of them. Nine configurations have been run more than once, their SLO
spreads spanning 0.5 to 5.1\,pp, and the three widest belong to the three arms
that decide something, so where a small margin matters our repeats are weakest.
The asymmetry runs against the controllers: the envelope takes each anchor's
\emph{better} run where a repeat exists, while most controllers are a single run.
The exposure is bounded---the verdicts are set by the segment to
\texttt{cap2}+HiCache, whose repeats differ by 0.8 and 1.0\,pp, below the
$\pm1.0$\,pp band we judge in.}

The no-control arm is the other end of the front:
every session admitted the moment it arrives finishes in 288.6 minutes at 3.0\%
SLO, $87.6$\,pp below the envelope, with a median time-to-first-token of
1{,}774 seconds.

\subsection{The controllers leave memory unused}
\label{sec:results:idle-memory}

Our controller admits fewer sessions rather than discarding one, so its pool
occupancy is a policy output, not a constraint: the base configuration holds a
median 49.2\% of the pool while 66 sessions queue for admission, and the
configuration that finishes fastest is a poor fast end (\texttt{cap6}+HC, 28.9\%
on SLO). Occupancy and running requests track each other (\texttt{occupancy.csv},
Spearman $\rho=0.97$), but running count does \emph{not} order wall clock:
\texttt{cap3}+HC and \texttt{horizon}$=$1, no hw carry the same mean running
count to two decimals and finish 21 minutes apart.

The controller believes it is nearly full. It admits against a projection
(Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) that runs at a median 7.6\% free of its budget, while
its measured occupancy at the same instants leaves 48.2\% of that budget, or
50.8\% of the pool, idle. The projection is 1.79$\times$ the occupancy the engine
reports, and not a constant of the design: it ranges 1.32 to 1.96 across the
twenty-seven controller runs that finished on this trace. Under-use is a property of the
controller's estimates rather than of the hardware, but it does not order the
outcomes, so it is not the whole explanation either; and removing the
single-flight gate is the same trade every other route makes---it moves that arm
along the front rather than past it.

\subsection{What the two mechanisms explain, arm by arm}
\label{sec:results:attribution}

Two mechanisms have been offered; we say what each one covers. For every
controller arm we took the static cap closest to it in wall
clock, out of every cap we ran, and compared occupancy and step cost: an arm is
attributed to the unused-memory mechanism if it runs fewer than 92\% of its
comparison's requests, and to the step-cost mechanism if it runs a comparable
number---within 8\%---at a more than 8\% higher $\tau_1$; what is left is a residual,
unless no comparable cap confirms the $\tau$ difference, in which case we record it as
\emph{unattributable}. The comparability clause is load-bearing.

Of the thirteen arms, seven are attributed---six to unused memory and one to step
cost (the published-parameter AIMD); two have a $\tau$
no matched comparison can confirm (\texttt{rg4} and all-throttles-off); and four
are residuals---%
\texttt{horizon}$=1$, the base, \texttt{breshz1} and the retuned AIMD, whose
occupancy and step cost both sit close to their comparison cap's, so their
shortfall is real but we cannot say from these measurements which mechanism
produced it. No row carries the co-tenancy flag, because the classification is
made on $\tau_1$, exactly the column co-tenancy inflates, and the arms it would
have flagged have all been re-measured alone. Two limits on what that buys. The
comparison is a static cap matched on \emph{wall clock}, and the arms that finish
near 260 minutes are matched against \texttt{cap5} of the full run table, whose SLO is 27.1\%---so the
classification describes an arm relative to a wall-clock peer, not to the static
\emph{front}. And the six unused-memory rows run fewer requests than their
comparison, which is a description of what they did, not a demonstration that
doing otherwise would have won. The per-arm readings are in
\texttt{attribution.csv}.

\subsection{Why the knobs do not help}
\label{sec:results:gate}

Our controller's projection (Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) charges a session for its full
context even while it is parked in a tool wait, when its KV is evictable and
therefore absent from the engine's reported occupancy. We argued this was a
double charge, removed it, and admitted on engine telemetry alone (\emph{resident
base} in Table~\ref{tab:runs}). The charge is not double. Removing it does what
the argument predicted on the engine side, with no eviction spiral, and finishes
25.4 minutes earlier (265.9 against 291.3). It is nonetheless not a win: the SLO
falls from 89.8\% to 85.9\%, and the static front itself falls 3.81\,pp over
those same 25.4 minutes, so the arm keeps its place relative to the envelope to
within 0.09\,pp while sliding 25 minutes along it, at the front's own slope. At
265.9 minutes the front sits at 87.2\%, so the arm would have had to give up no
more than 2.6\,pp of SLO instead of 3.9, and it did not. We rest nothing on the
0.09\,pp: the base configuration's own four runs sit at $-1.24$, $-1.73$, $-1.49$
and $+0.18$\,pp against this same front, a 1.9\,pp spread.

The other two knobs the mechanism names behave the same way. Removing the growth
reserve entirely costs 10.0\,pp of SLO, and the response is non-monotone in wall
clock: one round is the fastest point, two rounds is 20 minutes \emph{faster} than
three, and none of the four beats the base on the SLO axis. The best is
\texttt{horizon}=2, whose two runs read $-0.30$ and $+0.22$\,pp; the one-round
optimum, which this paper spends the most words on, does not reproduce, its two
repeats landing $5.6$ and $3.2$\,pp below the line. Lowering the budget fraction
slides the point \emph{below} the front rather than along it: budgets of 0.85 and
0.90 finish at 333.9 and 315.9 minutes for 89.9\% and 87.7\% SLO, $-2.24$ and
$-4.22$\,pp from the envelope at
those wall clocks, against $-0.93$ and $-1.24$ for the two nearest arms at the
0.95 the rest of the paper uses. None of the three adds a mechanism.

A fourth variant attacks the assumption the first three share---that what a
controller sets is \emph{how many sessions are resident}. Because a live session
spends most of its time parked, capping \emph{running requests} rather than
resident sessions should keep the engine busy. We implemented it as a gate on
\texttt{num\_running\_reqs} and swept it over $\{2,3,4\}$. A gate of four
tightened both counts rather than pinning the one it aims at, and only that arm ran to 998
of the trace's 999 steps, so this is a single comparison rather than a sweep. Both
repairs fail: without a constraint on admission a paused session is re-admitted on
the next cycle, and with one the policy is a session cap again. The obstacle is
granularity---\texttt{paused} takes effect at round boundaries, while pinning an
instantaneous count needs control the permit mechanism does not have.

\subsection{An independent adaptive controller}
\label{sec:results:aimd}

Every controller above is ours, which invites the objection that the negative
result is a statement about one design rather than about adaptive admission. We
therefore implemented a second, mechanistically different controller: an AIMD
window over an agent-level admission set, following the control law of the
congestion-based system we compare against in \S\ref{sec:related} \cite{concur,jacobson1988},
acting on runtime gauges through the same named permits as \sys{}. It does not
beat the static front either, at either parameter setting
(Table~\ref{tab:runs}): at the values reported in that paper's evaluation it
finishes in 326.8 minutes at 79.4\% SLO, $12.7$\,pp below the envelope, and with
its retuned watermarks in 264.6 minutes at 52.8\%, $34.3$\,pp below. This is our
reimplementation of a published control law, not the authors' system, and a
reimplementation can underperform its paper; we did not tune it beyond the two
documented settings, and its
first two attempts stalled because the launch path that ran it had no supervisor
loop. Our own controller shows a smaller version of the same shape: one of the
seventeen runs of ours in \texttt{stall.csv} has a window in which it admits nobody
for at least 150\,s while the engine has no running request and at least 85
sessions wait, with neither the SLO valve nor the hard stop holding
admission---166.6\,s, 0.93\% of the span it sampled; it cannot arise in our static
caps, which have no admission decision to get stuck in.
