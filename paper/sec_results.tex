\section{Results: Four Routes, One Outcome}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} places every completed run on the (wall clock, SLO)
plane and draws the upper-left envelope of the static points. Table~\ref{tab:runs}
gives the numbers and each controller's signed distance from that envelope.

The result is unambiguous. Of nine controller variants, \emph{none} is above
the envelope---not one, on any run, and the pair we repeated most tightly says
so at a resolution the others do not. The closest arm of all is the one with
single-flight admission disabled, at $0.57$ percentage points \emph{below} the
line---inside our $\pm1.0$\,pp tie band, so we record it as \emph{on} the
line rather than below it, but on the below side of it.

The base configuration is the one whose repeats we can trust, and they are
tight: four runs spanning 0.5\,pp of SLO (89.5\% mean, 279.8 to 293.2 minutes),
against the 3.3\,pp that separates it from the single-flight-off arm. So that
difference is several times the noise of its own configuration rather than
inside it, and it is a trade rather than a win: 28 minutes of wall clock bought
with 3.5\,pp of SLO, moving the arm onto the tie band from below it. Not one
configuration in the table gets above the line, and the tightest pair we have
is what makes us willing to say the near misses are misses. A fifth pair, two runs of \texttt{cap3}+HiCache, \emph{is} used, and an
earlier draft of this paper wrongly set it aside saying one of them exceeded
its own cap. That was a different run: the over-cap one is the device-only
\texttt{cap3} repeat (peak in-flight five against a cap of three; at our
300-point in-flight sample grid, 126 samples sit over the cap, a count that
scales with the grid rather than with the run), while both HiCache runs peak at three with none. The
repeat is also both faster and higher on SLO (230.3 minutes, 81.9\% against
236.4 and 81.1\%), so by the same rule that displaced \texttt{cap4}+HiCache's
first run it displaces this one---and moving that anchor is what puts our
best arm below the line rather than on it.\footnote{The repeats are uneven, and
we do not want the tightest pair to stand for all of them. Five configurations
have been run more than once; their SLO spreads are 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 3.9 and
5.1\,pp (\texttt{replicates.csv}). The two widest belong to the two arms that
decide something---\texttt{horizon}=1 and the leftmost anchor---so where a small
margin matters our repeats are weakest, and where they are strongest (the base
configuration and the \texttt{cap3}+HiCache anchor) the margin is not small. We
report both ends rather than the average.}

The anchor repeats moved the frontier twice and we report both moves. The
leftmost anchor's second run came out both faster (209.1 against 213.0 minutes)
and higher on SLO (68.5 against 63.4), displacing the first; no controller
verdict moves, because every controller finishes to the right of the next
anchor. The middle anchor's repeat moved it the other way for us: it is also
faster and higher, and taking it moves our best arm from \emph{on} the line to
\emph{on the line, below it}. We report every repeat we ran rather than keeping
the better of each pair, because a frontier whose anchors are single
measurements is the weakness this whole subsection is about---and here it is
the weakness biting.

The other end of the front deserves its own sentence, because the rest of this
paper is negative and a reader could close it thinking the answer is to do
nothing. The run with \emph{no} admission control---every session admitted the
moment it arrives---finishes in 288.6 minutes at 3.0\% SLO, $87.6$\,pp below
the envelope and the worst SLO we report. Its median
time-to-first-token is 1{,}774 seconds, its prefix cache hit rate collapses from
the planned 75.5\% the runner prints in its end-of-run summary (the all-steps
convention, a different
one from the round-1+ numbers in \S\ref{sec:related}---for the same trace
\texttt{gen\_table.py} reports 73.8\% on that convention) to 1.6\%, and its segment SLO is 30\% in the first
tenth of the run and exactly zero in each of the nine after that. Uncontrolled
admission does not degrade gracefully; it destroys the cache and then serves
almost nobody. The question this paper addresses is therefore not whether to
admit selectively, but which selective rule to use.

Two features of the front are worth noting before the negative results.
The envelope is steep between 209 and 230 minutes (0.63\,pp per minute) and
shallow thereafter (0.15\,pp per minute), so the cost of buying SLO by
retreating from the fast end is high. And $W$ is not monotone in concurrency:
five sessions (260.9\,min) is \emph{slower} than four (249.9\,min)---the same
configuration family, no host cache on either side---because the per-step cost
rises faster than the request count does. We return to this
non-monotonicity in \S\ref{sec:model}, where it turns out to be the first
appearance of the mechanism that drives the whole frontier.

\subsection{The controllers leave memory unused}
\label{sec:results:idle-memory}

Before the routes, one measurement that reframes all of them. Our controller
keeps every session's permit for its lifetime, so its pool occupancy is a
policy output, not a constraint. It runs at a median of 49.2\% of the pool on
the base configuration, and the median number of sessions queueing for
admission is 66. The configuration that finishes fastest---the repeat of that same static cap
of four, at 209.1 minutes---runs at 71.9\%, and its first run at 70.2\%. Occupancy
and running requests track each other reliably across the arms we have (the two
columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} rank the twenty arms almost identically), but
running count does \emph{not} order wall clock: \texttt{horizon}=2 runs 1.25
requests against the base arm's 1.14 and finishes 15 minutes slower. That gap is
what the next subsection is about.

The controller believes it is nearly full. Its own decision log records the
projection it admits against---the largest of measured occupancy and the summed
contexts of live sessions, plus a reserve for their growth---at a median of
89{,}073 tokens against a budget of 96{,}360, i.e.\ 7\% free. Its measured
occupancy at the same instants is 49{,}875 tokens: 48.2\% of that budget, or
50.8\% of the pool, is idle. The
projection is 1.79$\times$ the truth on this configuration. Across the
controller runs we have projection logs for, the ratio runs from 1.24 to 2.00
with a median of 1.75 on this trace, and from 1.24 to 4.11 with a median of
1.75 once the second trace's controller arm is included (\texttt{projection.csv};
the second trace is the 4.11). So 1.79 is this arm's value, not a constant of
the design, and we name the set because the range depends on it---over every
run in that file, including the flash-crowd family and the 25-session tier, it
is 1.01 to 4.11 with a median of 1.35. Two terms produce the gap: charging every
live session its full context, when a parked session's KV is evictable and
therefore absent from the engine's count, and the growth reserve, which alone
is 26{,}762 tokens, 28\% of the budget.

Two things follow, and we are careful about the second.

The first is that the controllers under-use the pool, and that this is a
property of their estimates rather than of the hardware: the base
configuration holds nearly half the pool idle (48.2\% of its budget, 50.8\% of\
the pool) with 66 sessions queued, because
it believes it has 7\% free when it has 48\%. Any account of why it is slow
that does not mention this is incomplete.

We then asked what actually stops it, by instrumenting the admission decision.
The path is a conjunction: a session is authorized only if the SLO valve is
open, the round gate is not full, and its projected footprint fits what remains
of the budget. Over the base run's 8{,}196 blocked cycles---cycles with a
session waiting and none admitted---the valve accounts for 6.8\%, the round gate
for none, and a projection already above budget for 38.4\%.

The remaining 54.8\% are cycles where the controller selected no candidate at
all, and here we are explicit about the limit of what we can resolve. The
controller is single-flight by default: it authorizes at most one session per
two-second cycle and waits for that session to begin a round before authorizing
the next. Whether that gate held or whether a candidate was tried and did not
fit is \emph{not distinguishable from our logs}---the controller does not record
it---so we do not attribute the bucket to either. We bounded it by running that arm: the same configuration with single-flight
disabled. Two things came out of it, and the second is the one that matters.

The bound first. With the gate off, the residual bucket---which can then only
be \emph{candidates that did not fit}, since the gate cannot be holding---falls
from 54.8\% of blocked cycles to 39.7\%, against 44.0\% that are over budget
outright. So the single-flight path accounts for roughly fifteen points, and
the headroom estimate remains the largest single cause. (Both arms' numbers
are rows of \texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, which now includes the \texttt{sf=0}
arm; an earlier revision quoted 39.9 and 43.5 for the second pair, values that
appeared in no data file.)

The second thing is that disabling it does not make the controller win.
\texttt{sf=0} finishes in 263.3 minutes at 86.3\% SLO against the base run's
291.3 and 89.8\%: it buys 28 minutes for 3.5\,pp, and its signed distance from
the front moves from $-1.25$ to $-0.57$---onto the tie band from below it, not
above it. Its admission rate is unchanged (2.5\% of cycles admit, against
2.3\%). So the gate was a real cost and removing it moves the arm along the
front rather than past it, which is the same trade every other route in this
section makes.

Across the eight \sys{} variants that left controller logs---the set is named in
\texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, and it is eight rather than nine because
\texttt{foyfix} and the two AIMD arms run a different controller and log no
gate state---the projection is over budget in a median 32.2\% of blocked
cycles (19.5\% to 50.8\%) and the valve in a median 32.7\% (6.8\% to 67.9\%).
Both medians move with the set, which is why we name it: over the wider group
of runs in that file they are 37.6\% and 10.5\%. The round gate never binds
except in the two runs that enable it---\texttt{rg2} at 29.3\% and \texttt{rg3}
at 18.0\% of blocked cycles, against 0.6\% or less everywhere else---which is
what one expects of a gate that is off by default.
We also record what this accounting omits, because it changes the picture: the
controller has a starvation guard that bypasses the budget entirely once the
oldest waiter has waited 240\,s, cooling down for 300\,s. On the base run it
accounts for 24.8\% of all admissions (51 of 206), and while the projection is
over budget it is the \emph{only} way in---of 3{,}700 such cycles, nine admitted,
and all nine were forced. It is therefore the guard, not the budget test, that
sets the floor on how fast this controller can admit.

The second is that under-use does \emph{not} order the outcomes, so it is not
the whole explanation either. \texttt{horizon}=1 and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache sit
at the same median occupancy (57.8\% of the pool) and the same mean running
count (1.52 against 1.51 on the busy-time measure of \S\ref{sec:model}), and
the cap is 19 minutes faster. \texttt{hz0} runs
at 64.1\%, higher than that cap, and is 54 minutes slower. Occupancy buys
running requests reliably---the two columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} track
each other across all twenty arms---but converting running requests into wall
clock is not simply the per-step cost of \S\ref{sec:model}. Decomposing that
19 minutes into its idle and busy components, 12.6 of them are idle time---%
65\%---and only 6.8 are busy; the two runs' step costs differ by 1.2\,ms
(29.2 against 28.0), about 4\%. A parked session is one the controller has
authorized but not yet released, so the same policy that sets the running count
also sets a large part of the idle time. Both
effects are real; on this workload the unused-memory one is larger for the arms
that finish worst, and the step-cost one is what separates the arms that finish
close together.

\subsection{What the two mechanisms explain, arm by arm}
\label{sec:results:attribution}

We have offered two mechanisms, and the honest thing is to say what each one
covers. For every controller arm we took the static cap closest to it in wall
clock and compared their occupancy and their step cost. An arm is attributed to
the unused-memory mechanism if it runs fewer than 92\% of its comparison's
requests, and to the step-cost mechanism if it runs a comparable number---%
within 8\%---at a measurably higher cost (more than 8\% higher $\tau_1$).
Everything else is a residual. The rule reads the running count and
$\tau_1$ only; it does not consult occupancy, so the ``unused memory'' label
is licensed by the running count alone---though the two arms it lands on also
sit at the low end of the occupancy column, which is why we let the name
stand. The comparability clause is load-bearing and we
state it because an earlier version of this table did not enforce it, which
classified every arm that ran \emph{more} requests than its comparison as
step-cost purely because $\tau$ rises with the running count.

\subsection{Route 1: the accounting base}

Our controller's projection (Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) charges a session for its full
context even while it is parked in a tool wait, when its KV is evictable and
therefore absent from the engine's reported occupancy. We argued this was a
double charge and removed it, admitting on engine telemetry alone.

The charge is not double. Removing it did admit more---median engine occupancy
rose from 49.2\% to 56.9\% of the pool---and the round-1+ prefix hit rate held at
80.2\% against the 80.6\% of the run that keeps the charge, so no eviction spiral occurred. But the run was
4.2 minutes \emph{slower} and lost 6.0\,pp of SLO. Per \S\ref{sec:model}, the
extra occupancy raised both $\overline{R}$ (1.35 to 1.48) and $\tau_2$
(23.8\,ms to 31.3\,ms), and the two cancelled.

That last sentence is the one place where this route's conclusion depends on a
contaminated measurement, and we mark it. The arm that tests this route
(\texttt{bres}) is one of the four flagged $^\ddagger$ in
Table~\ref{tab:runs}: its 31.3\,ms $\tau_2$ was measured under host
co-tenancy, and the same arm measured clean would plausibly sit near 25\,ms
rather than 31. On that reading the two terms would not cancel and the route
would look better than we report, not worse---so the direction of the
correction is against our own conclusion, which is the safe direction to be
wrong in, but it is still unresolved. The occupancy half of the argument
(49.2\% to 56.9\%) is a volume measurement and is unaffected. We keep the
route in the negative column pending the re-run and say so here rather than
in a footnote.

We note that this claim had already been falsified once by source inspection:
SGLang's \texttt{token\_usage} subtracts evictable blocks, so a parked
session's prefix is genuinely not counted, and charging for it reserves
capacity that really will be needed back. The end-to-end measurement agrees
with the source reading.

\subsection{Route 2: the growth reserve}

Sessions grow by roughly 2.5K tokens per round, so the controller reserves
capacity for the next \texttt{horizon} rounds. We swept the horizon over
$\{0,1,2,3\}$ and the reserve is not a free parameter to be minimised:
Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} shows the response is non-monotone. Removing the
reserve entirely costs 12.6\,pp of SLO, and a reserve of two rounds is
15 minutes \emph{slower} than three. The optimum is at one round, and there it comes closest to the
static front---0.94\,pp below it, inside the tie band---and that single point is
the one this paper spends the most words on, because it does not reproduce: the
repeat of this configuration lands 5.6\,pp below the line
(\S\ref{sec:results}). Read the sweep as a shape, not as a result at its
minimum.

\subsection{Route 3: the hard ceiling}

Lowering the budget fraction is the crudest available knob and behaves as the
other routes do: it slides the operating point along the front. We report it
only for completeness, since it adds no mechanism.

\subsection{An independent adaptive controller}
\label{sec:results:aimd}

Every controller above is ours, which invites the objection that the negative
result is a statement about one design rather than about adaptive admission.
We therefore implemented a second, mechanistically different controller: an
AIMD window over an agent-level admission set, following the control law of
the congestion-based system we compare against in \S\ref{sec:related}. The
window grows additively while cache occupancy sits below a low watermark, is
cut multiplicatively when occupancy exceeds a high watermark \emph{and} the
prefix hit rate falls below a threshold, and holds otherwise. Cuts pause the
youngest sessions at their next step boundary; growth resumes paused sessions
first. Every input is a runtime gauge---occupancy and hit rate from the
engine, nothing from the trace---and the window acts through the same named
permits as \sys{}.

It does not beat the static front either, at either parameter setting
(Table~\ref{tab:runs}). At the values reported in that paper's evaluation it
finishes in 364.3 minutes at 81.4\% SLO, $11.2$\,pp below the envelope; the
same arm with its retuned watermarks finishes in 264.6 minutes at 52.8\%,
$33.4$\,pp below. The retuned setting is also one of the most
volatile runs we report: its segment-to-segment SD is 13.2\,pp of SLO, with
segment values swinging between 37 and 72. Three runs are worse (\texttt{cap4}
at 15.8, the all-throttles-off arm at 14.4, and \texttt{breshz1} at 14.2), and
no other controller arm is as bad---which is what a control loop oscillating
around a capacity cliff looks like.

We report two things honestly about this comparison. First, this is our
reimplementation of a published control law, not the authors' system, and a
reimplementation of any controller can underperform its paper. We built it to
the published law, gave it the same permit interface and the same 2\,s cycle
as \sys{}, and did not tune it beyond the two documented settings; but the
caveat stands. Second, both controllers stall. At its paper parameters this one pinned its
window at one session, admitted nothing, and left the engine idle for over an
hour. Our own controller does the same thing more briefly: in 4 of the 11
\sys{} runs an audit found a single window of 150--175\,s in which the engine
was empty, the queue held ~90 sessions, the SLO valve was open, and the
controller issued no permit at all---about 0.9\% of each run's wall clock. The
remaining runs, including the best one, show no such window. Nothing is logged
as an error and the run recovers on its own.

We traced one such window in a repeat of our best configuration and found a
code path that explains it: single-flight admission considered only the head of
the FIFO queue, and when the head was a session too large to fit the remaining
budget it was never admitted, never left the queue, and blocked every smaller
session behind it. That is the code every run in this paper executed. The fix
in our repository---scan the queue, still admit at most one per cycle---is
\emph{not} in the artifact that produced these results, so the numbers here
predate it and we do not claim them for it. We are also explicit that this is
\emph{a} path, not a proven cause:
the audit is a log signature, and the same signature can be produced by other
closed gates. What we can say is that the signature is present in a third of
our \sys{} runs and absent from all of our static caps, which have no
admission decision to get stuck in.

That is the honest version of a finding we first reported too strongly. An
intermediate draft of this paper called it a deadlock and said the
configuration that produced our best result was the one that deadlocked on its
repeat. Neither holds: the windows are ~160\,s and self-clearing, and the best
configuration never showed one. The windows are also too small (0.9\%) to move
any verdict we report. We keep them because the mechanism is real and its size
is the point: on a pool that holds four median sessions, a controller's failure
mode is a silent stall, not graceful degradation, and it took a repeat run to
see it.

\subsection{Route 4: execution granularity}
\label{sec:results:gate}

The first three routes all assume that what a controller can set is \emph{how
many sessions are resident}. A static cap sets the same quantity, so it is
unsurprising that they land in the same place. The fourth route attacks the
assumption instead: because a large fraction of live sessions are parked at
any instant, a cap on \emph{resident} sessions leaves the engine idle whenever
all residents happen to be parked. Capping the \emph{running-request} count
instead should keep the engine busy while leaving the resident count high.

The permit design supports this directly: a paused session finishes its
current round and blocks before its next one, and resumes when the controller
re-admits it. We implemented it as a gate on the engine's own
\texttt{num\_running\_reqs} and swept the gate over $\{2,4\}$. Only the
gate-of-four arm completed (999 steps); the gate-of-two arm reached 770 and is
not used, so what we report below is a single comparison against the no-gate
arm rather than a sweep.

It does not work, and the measurement says why. Against the no-gate arm, a
gate of four moved mean running requests from 1.33 to 1.30 and mean live
sessions from 3.25 to 3.03: it tightened both, by 2\% and 7\%, rather than
pinning the one it aims at. That is what we
observe, and we state it as the observation it is rather than deriving a bound
we cannot support. A live session spends most of its time parked in a tool wait
(the ratio of mean running requests to mean live sessions is 0.38 to 0.66
across our arms), so pausing live sessions removes capacity that was not
running in the first place---but that ratio is an average over a run, not a
bound on any instant, and the running count can in principle reach the live
count. Both repairs fail: without a
constraint on admission, a paused session is re-admitted on the next cycle and
blocks for at most one 2\,s cycle; with one, the policy is a session cap again.
The obstacle is granularity---\texttt{paused} takes effect at round
boundaries, while pinning an instantaneous count requires control the
permit mechanism does not have.

The measured outcome matches: the gate moved running concurrency from 1.33 to
1.30 and live sessions from 3.25 to 3.03. It tightened rather than pinned, and
a tightening is another cap.
