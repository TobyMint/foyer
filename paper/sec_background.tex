\section{Background and Setup}
\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{Workload}

We replay 200 coding-agent sessions drawn from the {\sc TraceLab} corpus
\cite{zhu2026tracelab}. Each session is a
sequence of rounds; a round presents a prompt of median 23{,}187 tokens
(p90 51{,}064; maximum 89{,}436, which alone is 88\% of the KV pool), and
between rounds the session waits on a tool before returning with a longer
context. Arrivals are Poisson at $\lambda=0.04\,\text{s}^{-1}$ over an
80-minute window, giving 999 steps in total. Two sessions of maximum size
cannot be co-resident: their combined contexts exceed the pool by 76\%.

The tool wait is the load's defining property and the one most easily
misread. Its \emph{median} is 0.35\,s, which invites the conclusion that
sessions never leave the engine. Its \emph{mean} is 16.85\,s and the maximum is 300\,s; 21.8\% of the
tool waits the trace specifies exceed ten seconds, which is 14.4\% of all
round transitions once the zero-wait ones are counted, or 18.6\% on the
measured gaps. We verified that our replay
harness honors the trace faithfully: across 799 round transitions the measured
gap is 16.85\,s mean against a demanded 15.82\,s. On a typical transition the
harness adds nothing---the median of the per-transition measured/demanded
ratios is 1.001---while the ratio of the two means is 1.065, the difference
sitting in the tail. The corpus's own characterization makes the same point
qualitatively---it reports
``diverse and heavily-tailed tool calls'' and calls these gaps
``human-paced''---but gives no numeric mean \cite{zhu2026tracelab}, so the
16.85\,s above is our measurement over the released trace, not a figure we
quote from that paper.

\subsection{Engine and the binding constraint}

We use SGLang v0.5.10 \cite{zheng2024sglang} with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct
\cite{qwen2coder} extended to 96K context,
on one RTX 3090 (24\,GB). KV is paged \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}; at \texttt{mem-fraction-static} 0.85
the pool is 101{,}432 tokens. Hierarchical caching (HiCache), with a host tier of twice the device pool, is
enabled on the configurations marked \emph{+HC} and on every \sys{} run; the
five plain static caps run without it. We keep both because the host tier turns
out to change the mechanism (\S\ref{sec:model}). Every run is guarded: if the pool does not
come out at $101{,}432 \pm 20$---which happens when another tenant holds
memory on the card---the run is aborted rather than recorded, because SGLang
sizes the pool from free memory at startup and a shrunken pool silently
produces incomparable numbers.

The relevant consequence is that the pool holds only about four median
sessions. Concurrency is therefore capacity-bound, not scheduler-bound, and
this is the premise on which everything below rests.

\subsection{The controller under test}

\sys{} is an admission controller that reads engine telemetry and issues
\emph{named} permits rather than a scalar cap: a session is admitted by
appearing in a permit file, and the runner refuses to start a round for a
session that is not listed. This design choice matters for the comparison,
because it is what allows per-session actions at all---including the pause
primitive we exploit in \S\ref{sec:results:gate}. It also means that a session,
once admitted, holds its permit for its entire lifetime, so the controller
never frees capacity by discarding a session's state---it admits fewer sessions
instead. That is a statement about the \emph{policy}, not about the memory: the
engine's radix cache still evicts parked prefixes under pressure, and we
measure that it does and that they are recomputed (\S\ref{sec:model}). We
return to this distinction in \S\ref{sec:discussion}, because it bounds how far
our negative result generalises.

Each control cycle (2\,s) the controller computes
\begin{equation}
  \Pi \;=\; \max(\rho, \textstyle\sum_i c_i) \;+\; \textstyle\sum_i g_i ,
  \label{eq:proj}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the engine's reported occupancy, $c_i$ the context length of
live session $i$, and $g_i$ its forecast growth over the next
\texttt{horizon} rounds. A session is admitted when $\Pi + n \le B$, with $n$
the candidate's estimated need and $B$ the budget. The three terms are
respectively the measured physical occupancy, a reservation for sessions
currently parked in a tool wait (whose KV is evictable and therefore absent
from $\rho$), and a reservation for future growth.

\subsection{Baselines and the verdict criterion}

Baselines are static caps: at most $N$ sessions resident, $N \in \{1,\dots,5\}$,
with and without HiCache. We compare on two axes: end-to-end wall clock
(time from the first submission to the last completion) and SLO attainment
(fraction of requests whose time-to-first-token is under 10\,s).

The verdict criterion deserves care, because ``faster than cap-$N$'' is not
the question. The question is whether a policy reaches a point \emph{above}
the static front. A point that lies merely between two static caps is not a
win: mixing cap-$N$ and cap-$M$ by time slice approximates any point on the
line between them. We flag this segment argument as an argument, not a
measurement---we did not run the time-sliced policies, and carrying queue state
and cache contents across a switch could bend the line. It is the criterion we
apply, and it is the stricter of the two we could have used---stricter than
comparing each controller against the discrete static points alone, because the
line sits above them. We state the direction plainly rather than claim it as a
caution: this choice makes our negative result \emph{easier} to reach, not
harder. Our best arm is $0.94$\,pp below the line and would be $+2.9$\,pp
against the nearest static point below it in wall clock (\texttt{cap3}+HiCache's
first run, 81.1\% at 236.4 minutes). So we take the
upper-left
envelope of the static points and report each controller's signed distance
above or below it. We classify a signed distance of more than $+1.0$\,pp as
above the front, less than $-1.0$\,pp as below it, and the band between the
two as \emph{on} it---a tie, not a win. The band is a convention we fixed
before running anything, and we report it here because every verdict in this
paper depends on it. Our later replicate measurements (\S\ref{sec:results})
bracket it rather than justify it: the SLO spread of configurations we ran
more than once ranges from 0.5\,pp to 5.1\,pp, so for some configurations
the band is generous and for others it is far too narrow. We flag this as a
weakness of the criterion, not a defence of it. One further property of the
envelope deserves flagging, because
four of the nine controller variants finish slower than 295 minutes. Its
right-hand segment runs from \texttt{cap2}+HiCache at 295 minutes to
\texttt{cap1} at 527, and \texttt{cap1} is the only anchor there that runs
\emph{without} HiCache. Those four---\texttt{hz2}, \texttt{bres},
\texttt{breshz1} and \texttt{foyfix}---are therefore judged against an
interpolation between one host-tier point and one device-only point. If the
envelope is drawn from the host-tier anchors alone, that segment does not exist
and those four cannot be adjudicated at all; drawn from device-only anchors
alone, they sit further below. We report the conservative reading and note that
the honest statement about those four is \emph{below the front as we defined
it}, not \emph{below every static configuration}. The front and every
controller's signed distance are computed by one script from the raw per-step
logs; the table generator computes the distance for the four runs that did not
complete all 999 steps---both are named in \S\ref{sec:repro}---and that
script's own first version was wrong: it extrapolated past the end of the
envelope and declared the slowest static cap a winner. We fixed it and report
the episode because it is the failure mode this criterion is most prone to.
