\section{Decomposing the Wall Clock}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two components}

We split the wall clock at every sampled instant according to whether the
engine has any request in flight:
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; W_{\text{busy}} \;+\; W_{\text{idle}} .
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the split for the twenty-eight runs of
Table~\ref{tab:runs}'s families that we have per-step logs for. Two things are
immediately visible and both matter later.

First, idle time is not noise. Across the runs it spans 7.3\% to 41.9\% of
the sampled span---7.3\% for the fastest configuration, 36.3\% for the
slowest, and 41.9\% for the one that idles most (all-throttles-off). The
denominator here
is the span the sampler observed, which runs about six minutes longer than the
request wall clock of Table~\ref{tab:runs}, so the fractions against that
wall clock are 7.6\% and 36.7\%---and it is not spread evenly.
Over 1{,}844 stretches the mean is 20.9\,s and 17.5\% of them exceed
20\,s, accounting for 69.9\% of all idle \emph{time}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). The tail, not the middle, is what a scheduler feels,
and it sits at the scale of the 15.8--16.9\,s mean tool wait (demanded and
measured; \texttt{toolwait.csv}). We report the mean and the share rather
than the median here because the median is not robust to which runs enter the
pool: it is 10.0\,s without \texttt{cap1} and 5.0--5.5\,s with it, since
that one run contributes 411 of the 1{,}844 stretches and is itself the
longest. The mean (18.8--20.9\,s) and the over-20\,s share of idle time
(64.6--70.1\%) move by little across the four poolings we report in
\texttt{idle\_pooled.csv}, so the claim that the tail dominates does not
depend on the choice. The 90th percentile moves with the median
(25.1--30.1\,s) and we give it as a range for the same reason.

Second, the two components move independently and in opposite directions, so
neither is a proxy for the wall clock. The no-control baseline
(\S\ref{sec:results}) cuts idle time by 13.8 minutes against the base
configuration---it admits everything, so nothing queues---and finishes only
2.7 minutes sooner, because its busy time grows by 11.1 minutes; the trade
costs it the SLO column outright, 89.8\% down to 3.0\%. The arm that disables
every throttle moves the other way, and further: idle \emph{rises} by 129
minutes while busy \emph{falls} by 5.4, so the 124 minutes it adds to the wall
clock are almost entirely waiting, bought with 43.7\,pp of SLO. Any account
that treats ``more concurrency'' as one thing will get this wrong in one
direction or the other.

Neither example is one of the damaged runs of \S\ref{sec:repro}; both are
clean in the co-tenancy audit. Neither is a member of the controller family
whose clean step-cost range that section quotes, either---the baseline is not
a controller at all, and its 39.2\,ms comes with the highest running count
among the runs whose step cost we report (1.91), while the throttle-free arm
runs a different controller. (The \texttt{horizon}=1 repeat reads 2.10, but its
$\tau$ columns are the ones we exclude above.) The
charge-free base arm is a third case, whose split we give in
\S\ref{sec:results} with the re-measured numbers.

\subsection{What determines busy time}

During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
running request. Hence
\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
  \label{eq:busy}
\end{equation}
with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and
$\tau$ the per-step cost. Across our runs $\tau$ is not a fit parameter we
tuned; we obtain it two independent ways, and reporting both is what shows
they are measuring different things rather than confirming each other.

\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} /
G$. Two choices in that expression are easy to get wrong and both matter. The
time term must be the \emph{busy} component, not the wall clock---using the
wall clock charges idle time to the steps. And $\overline{R}$ must be the mean
running count \emph{during busy time}, not the mean over the whole run: the
whole-run mean includes the idle samples, where the count is zero, so it equals
$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \times$ (busy fraction). Using it makes $\tau_1$
too low by exactly the busy fraction---so the estimate is short by the
\emph{idle} fraction, 36.3\% for \texttt{cap1}, whose busy share is 63.7\%. We report both columns in the data, and use the
busy-conditional mean throughout; the whole-run mean is what an earlier
revision of this paper used, and it made the two methods agree far better than
they do.

\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} For each step record we compute the
per-request decode interval,
$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$,
and take the median over steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$.

The two do not agree exactly, and the disagreement is informative rather than
embarrassing. $\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every run we accept, by 8.4--39.7\%
(\texttt{decomp.csv}, column \texttt{excess\_pct}, over the 27 runs that remain
once the repeat excluded below is dropped), which in absolute terms is
2.7--8.8\,ms of a 20.6--39.2\,ms step. (The one run we exclude
from the reuse accounting, the \texttt{horizon}=1 repeat, reads 66.3\% here
against its original's 18.5\%. The anomaly sits in the engine's token counters
and in everything derived from them, so that is the scope of the exclusion:
this run's $\tau$ columns, its reuse and recomputation are unusable, while its
wall clock and SLO---which come from the client---are ones we use, and it
appears in Table~\ref{tab:runs} and in the repeat spread of
\S\ref{sec:results} on those two columns alone.) The gap is
\emph{consistent with} engine steps that are prefill and
therefore emit no output token---we can bound it, not identify it, because
subtracting a median from a mean does not isolate a component. All we can form
from these two is their ratio: the excess $\tau_1/\tau_2 - 1$ runs
17.7--39.7\% across the five device-only configurations. Two things follow, and
we separate them. First, it is a ratio of \emph{times}, not of steps, and we
have no measurement of the fraction of step \emph{counts} that are prefill---
chunked prefill makes those two different numbers. Second, even as a time
ratio it is the gap between two measurements, not a decomposition of one: it
is consistent with prefill and non-decode work filling it, but we cannot rule
out that some of the difference is the two estimators disagreeing for other
reasons. So the two are not
two estimates of one number; they are measurements of two different things,
and we do not size a component with their difference. Fig.~\ref{fig:steptime} shows both, run by run. Method 2 remains the one that does not depend on the
quantity it is used to explain, and it is the one we quote when the question
is the cost of a decode step. The engine's own latency counters
(\texttt{itl\_p50}, \texttt{e2e\_p50}, \texttt{ttft\_p50}) are exported as zero
by SGLang v0.5.10 in our configuration, so no directly measured step time
exists in our data; instrumenting this is future work.

\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}

Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is
not. Across the thirteen runs we fitted---the five device-only caps, the three
host-tier caps that span the same range, and the five controller arms we report
individually---$\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and $\tau_2$
from 20.6 to 33.1\,ms, and both rise with the running count: fitting
Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$
($R^2 = 0.89$ for the latter, Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).

The relation is a trend, not a law. Two host-tier configurations with similar
running counts behave differently: \texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at
29.8\,ms while \texttt{F:hz0} runs 1.66 at 35.7\,ms. Running count sets a
floor and a trend, not a value---and on the host-tier family, as we show below,
it does not set the value at all.

This is the crux of the paper, and we state it at the strength the arithmetic
supports. Hold $\tau_1$ at its value for \texttt{cap1}, 28.8\,ms, and busy
time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from 1.00 to 1.84
should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to 184. The
measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes back 50 of
the 156 minutes that the extra concurrency would otherwise have bought---\emph{a
third} of the gain, not all of it and, as an earlier revision of this paper
claimed, not more than half. The other two thirds are real: raising
concurrency does make the engine faster. What it does not do is make it faster
in proportion, and the shortfall compounds with every controller that tries to
exploit the difference.

\paragraph{What the engine is doing when it is not decoding.} SGLang exposes
two counters that answer this directly: tokens evicted from the radix cache,
and prompt tokens that had to be prefilled rather than reused. Across the five
static caps \emph{without} HiCache the two track each other almost exactly.
\texttt{cap1} evicts 9.2\,M tokens and prefills 8.6\,M uncached;
\texttt{cap5} evicts 21.3\,M and prefills 20.7\,M; the gap is 0.6--0.7\,M on
every one of the five. An evicted prefix is recomputed, close to one token for
one, and that recomputation is chunked into decode steps. Those same five runs
give recomputation rising with $\tau$ across four of the five points: 9.2\,M
uncached tokens at 27.4\,ms, 11.5 at 29.7, 15.4 at 32.4, 20.7 at 36.7. The
exception is the first point, \texttt{cap1}, which recomputes least (8.6\,M)
and still costs 28.8\,ms---more than \texttt{cap2} does for more recomputation.
We report the ordering we have rather than rounding it to a monotone one: it is
a mechanism consistent with the $\tau$ trend on four of the five device-only
configurations, and one point it does not order. It is five points on five
different policies, not an
intervention: we did not hold concurrency fixed and vary eviction, so we call
it consistent rather than demonstrated.

\paragraph{Where the mechanism stops.} With HiCache enabled the
correspondence breaks---and it breaks in a way that confirms the mechanism
rather than undermining it. The device cache misses as much as before:
\texttt{cap4\_hc} misses 22.4\,M tokens on the device, more than the
device-only \texttt{cap4} misses. But only 9.1\,M of that is recomputed; the
other 13.3\,M is served from the host tier. Across the nineteen host-tier
runs the host serves 9.5--19.3\,M tokens each, and across the five device-only
runs it serves almost nothing---0.006\% to 0.25\% of the device miss, the rest
of which is recomputed on all five. Recomputation is still what the device cannot
absorb; the host tier is a second absorber in front of it.

\subsection{What tracks reuse is the tier, not the policy}

The device-miss column hides the quantity that matters. Define
\emph{reuse} as the prompt tokens served from anywhere in the memory
hierarchy---device radix cache plus host tier---so that
$\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$, with the prompt fixed at
28.5\,M by the trace.

The bound on reuse has to be defined independently of what we observed,
or the argument is circular. We take it from the trace: summing the planned
reusable prefix (\texttt{prefix\_len}) over all 999 rounds of
\texttt{replay\_pois200\_l04.csv} gives 21{,}540{,}371 tokens against a prompt
total of 28{,}526{,}871, so no policy can reuse more than 75.5\% of the
prompt. That is a property of the workload. It is also a bound rather than a
description: \emph{no configuration we ran reaches it}, and the best realizes
92.5\%, so the gap between ``planned'' and ``achieved'' exists even in the
easiest case. What varies across configurations is how much of the bound is
realized, and that is where the tier enters.

Device-only, realization falls from 92.5\% to 36.4\% and the shortfall is
recomputation: 8.6\,M to 20.7\,M tokens. With a host tier it holds at
90.2--92.4\% on the static points that span the same range, and at
72.8--92.5\% across the nineteen host-tier runs that completed this
trace---the same trace, HiCache on, at least 995 of its 999 steps---including
every controller arm; the lowest is the arm that disables every throttle and
therefore evicts the most (21.1\,M tokens, the largest in the table). The
\emph{device} meanwhile misses more than the device-only family's at every
point (21.6--27.9\,M against 8.6--20.7\,M), because the host serves
9.5--19.3\,M in front of the recomputation instead of letting the device keep
it---so a reader watching only the device hit rate would conclude the host tier
made things worse. The rule is mechanical, and we state the two exclusions that are not, because a
reader applying it will meet them: one run whose engine
prompt-token counter reads $1.41\times$ the trace plan with zero retractions
and no client-side retries, which we could not reconcile, and one superseded
measurement that we re-ran clean (\S\ref{sec:repro}). The step condition is
doing work of its own: two further runs on this trace with a host tier stopped
at 763 and 770 steps, realize 70.7\% and 67.1\%, and are excluded for not
finishing rather than for their values. (Two denominators appear here and we name them: the 75.5\%
is the trace's bound as a share of the prompt, and 92.5\% is the best observed
reuse as a share of \emph{that bound}---\texttt{cap1}, the run that realizes
it, reuses 69.9\% of its prompt. \texttt{reuse.csv} carries \texttt{reuse\_frac} and
\texttt{realized\_frac} side by side so the two cannot be confused.)

This is the same collapse \S\ref{sec:related} reports under a different
normalization. That section quotes the round-1-and-later prefix hit rate---%
steps restricted to rounds $\ge 1$, unweighted mean over steps---which falls
from 80.8\% to 32.8\% across the device-only caps and holds at 80.7\% to
78.7\% with a host tier. The two conventions differ in two ways, not one:
which steps are in the set (round $\ge 1$ against all rounds) and how steps
are combined (unweighted mean against token-weighted sum). We use the
token-weighted all-steps convention here because reuse and recomputation must
add to the prompt, and we name the other whenever we quote it. Both are
\emph{realized} quantities that vary with the policy; neither is the trace's
planned rate, which is fixed at 75.5\% and appears in this section only as
the bound.

Device occupancy does not explain the split. \texttt{cap3\_fixed} and
\texttt{cap3\_hc} run at nearly the same mean device occupancy (55.8\% and
55.5\%) and nearly the same running count (1.53 and 1.51), and they realize
79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound respectively. The host-tier side of that
comparison is not one run: \texttt{cap3}+HiCache has a repeat which sits at the
same occupancy (57.4\%) and the same count (1.53) and realizes 91.1\%, so the
12-point gap is not one lucky run on the host-tier side. The device-only side
is one run, and we say so rather than implying a distribution. What differs is
not how full the
device pool is but what happens when it overflows: with no host tier an
evicted prefix is gone and must be recomputed; with one it is demoted and read
back. The pool is the binding resource; the tier sets the pool; and in these two
runs, which differ in the tier and in nothing we can see on the engine side,
the tier is what separates paying for added concurrency in recomputation from
not paying for it. The
caveat is that this pair is two runs on two different days and two different
GPUs, not a paired experiment; \S\ref{sec:repro} states what we did to rule
out co-tenancy. A capacity-controlled version---same card, same trace, only
the pool size changed---is queued behind the re-runs and we report it in
\S\ref{sec:results} when it lands; we do not use it here.

\paragraph{The second trace agrees on direction and not on level.}
We ran the same accounting on a second workload, reported in full in
\S\ref{sec:xtrace}---117 real Claude agent sessions, 390 rounds, the same pool
and the same engine---and it reproduces the direction: its realization of \emph{its own} bound falls from 74.8\% to 57.5\%
as the running count rises from 1.08 to 1.50, and over that span the whole-range
per-unit slope is $-40.9$ points, against the device-only family's $-66.8$ over
$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00--1.84 (\texttt{slope.csv}). What it does not reproduce is the level, and this is
worth stating because it bounds what we can claim. The lightest cap on that
trace (\texttt{realhr\_cap2}) runs at a \emph{lower} median device occupancy
than the lightest on this one (15.8\% against 22.4\%, \texttt{cap1}) and
realizes \emph{less} (74.8\% against 92.5\%), so realization is not a function
of occupancy alone---and a
statement of the form ``the pool collapses above $x$\% occupancy'' is wrong on
our own data. Occupancy is instantaneous while reuse depends on history; a
session's prefix must survive from one round to the next, and how much
pressure that puts on the pool is not visible in what the pool holds at an
instant. We do not have the measurement that would separate those, which is
why the controlled experiment now queued for \S\ref{sec:results}---it varies
the pool alone, on one card and one trace---is the one that will settle it, and
why we do not lean on the cross-trace comparison here. The claim this section supports is the narrow one: within a
configuration, the host tier preserves realization at a matched running count
and a matched occupancy, and without it realization falls.

That gives the criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} a mechanism rather than a
correlation, and it suggests a prediction we are testing:
if the tier acts by multiplying usable capacity (the host pool is twice the
device pool, so three times in total), then shrinking the device pool alone
should move the collapse point even with no host tier involved. We do not
treat the arithmetic as a result---it assumes KV demand grows linearly in the
running count, which heterogeneous sessions need not satisfy---and the
queued experiment measures the collapse point directly rather than predicting
it, and \S\ref{sec:results} reports it once it has run.

\paragraph{What is left unexplained.} Less than an earlier draft of this
paragraph claimed. Across the nineteen host-tier runs recomputation spans
8.6--12.8\,M tokens and realization 72.8--92.5\%, and $\tau_1$ spans
27.0--36.2\,ms. Two of those runs
(\texttt{F:hz2} 33.6 and \texttt{F:breshz1} 36.2\,ms) were
measured while three other engine instances shared the host, which inflates
per-step cost by 24--41\% and leaves the volume columns untouched
(\S\ref{sec:repro}); they are being re-measured and we do not use their
timing. Two others (the resident base and \texttt{F:hz0}) were re-measured
clean and are in the set above with their new values. Across the remaining
seventeen the $\tau_1$ spread does not look
like a residual: it tracks their recomputation closely (a straight line
through the seventeen leaves every run within $1.6$\,ms, $r = +0.91$). That is a
cross-policy association like every other fit in this section, not an
intervention, and we read it as
the mechanism of this section holding inside the host tier. We report the association and not the line: the coefficients are
fitted to one configuration family and we have no derivation for them. We note that the negative
result does not rest on it either way: the cancellation is measured directly,
not inferred from the mechanism.

One caution about the strength of the account above, since the arithmetic
invites overreading. $\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$ is a
definition, not a finding, so ``the shortfall reappears as recomputation''
follows from how we partitioned the prompt and cannot by itself establish the
mechanism. What is independent of the partition is the pair of engine
counters: evicted tokens and uncached prefill tokens are separate SGLang
counters, and on the device-only runs they track within 0.6--0.7\,M on all
five---which is the observation that licenses reading eviction as the cause
of recomputation. With a host tier the two come apart by design, and the
1.3--8.6\,M gap between them is an upper bound on what the host absorbed out
of the evicted pool: those tokens were evicted and not recomputed, which is
consistent with the host having served them and equally consistent with their
never having been asked for again. We can bound it, not identify it
(\texttt{eviction.csv}); the host serves 9.5--19.3\,M in total, and by the
counter definitions those are tokens the \emph{device} did not have when the
session came back---it had evicted them---and which were not recomputed.

\paragraph{A refutation we had to withdraw.} An earlier revision withdrew this
mechanism outright, on two checks that were both wrong. The first was that
total prefilled tokens equalled the trace's planned prefill to a ratio of 1.00,
so no token could have been computed twice. \texttt{prompt\_tokens\_total}
counts the full submitted prompt \emph{including} its cached portion, so it
equals the sum of prompt lengths by construction and cannot detect
recomputation; the quantity that can is the uncached count used above, which
varies by 2.4$\times$ across these runs. The second was that SGLang reported
no retractions. Radix-cache eviction is not a retraction---retractions count
running requests preempted mid-flight, a different event with its own counter.
We record the episode because it is the same failure mode as the segment
argument of \S\ref{sec:bg}: a check that looks like a measurement but is an
identity.

\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}

The length of an individual idle stretch is a property of the workload, not of
the controller. A session in a
tool wait holds its permit and its KV but issues no request; with only about
two live sessions, the probability that all of them are simultaneously waiting
is substantial. The direct evidence is the duration of the stretches
themselves: they are of the same order as the tool waits, and an order of
magnitude longer than the 2\,s control cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). No
controller can shorten a gap that the tool set.

How much idle a \emph{run} accumulates is a different matter, and there the
controller is not off the hook. Idle accumulates when every live session
happens to be parked at once, and how many sessions are live is precisely what
an admission policy sets. Two configurations with the same mean running count
make this visible: \texttt{horizon}=1 (1.52) and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache (1.51)
idle 36.7 and 24.1 minutes respectively---a 52\% difference at matched
concurrency---and 12.6 of the 19.4-minute wall-clock gap between them is that
idle difference rather than step cost. So the gap durations are the workload's,
and the number of gaps is partly the policy's.

We note one claim we had to drop here. An earlier draft reported that idle
fraction falls monotonically with mean concurrency. With the full data it does
not: on the busy-conditional measure this section uses throughout,
\texttt{F:hz0} at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.66 idles 15.2\% while
\texttt{cap4} at 1.65 idles 10.4\%---more concurrency and \emph{more} idle,
the reverse of the reported trend. (The two arms read 1.41 and 1.48 on the
all-samples mean, where the ordering happens to run the other way;
\texttt{arm\_running.csv} carries both conventions side by side, and we name
the one each claim uses.) The weaker statement that survives is the one we use---%
the stretches are structural and long.

The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always
ready---is what \texttt{bres} does, and the clean measurement of it says the
remedy works and does not pay. \texttt{bres} cuts idle time by 9.5 minutes
against its own baseline, and its busy time falls as well, by 15.5---so the
arm simply finishes 25.4 minutes earlier. What it does not do is improve the
criterion: its SLO falls 3.9\,pp and its distance below the static front is
unchanged (\S\ref{sec:results}). Recovering idle time is not the same act as
buying a better operating point, and here the one does not produce the other.
We are reporting a correction in this paragraph, not just a refinement: an
earlier draft had idle falling while busy rose, leaving the run slower, and
that shape came from a measurement we have since found was taken under host
co-tenancy (\S\ref{sec:repro}). Idle is recoverable; it is recoverable at a
price that does not move the verdict.
The structural remedy would be to make the engine do work during a wait. For
the session that is waiting there is none---its next round does not exist until
its tool returns---but for the \emph{pool} there is: the admission queue holds a
median of 66 sessions waiting to get in, and a policy could run one of those
instead of holding a permit for a parked one. \S\ref{sec:results} measures
what happens when it does: the charge-free base arm cuts total idle from 47.4
to 37.9 minutes, and it does not move the verdict. Idle is recoverable in part;
recovering it is not what pays.
