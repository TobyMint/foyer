\section{Decomposing the Wall Clock}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two components}

We split the wall clock at every sampled instant according to whether the
engine has any request in flight:
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; W_{\text{busy}} \;+\; W_{\text{idle}} .
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the split for every completed run. Two things are
immediately visible and both matter later.

First, idle time is not noise. It ranges from 7.4\% of the wall clock for the
fastest configuration to 36.3\% for the slowest. The stretches are long:
over 1{,}849 of them the median is 5.6\,s, the 90th percentile is 30.1\,s,
17.6\% exceed 20\,s and the longest is 511\,s (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). A
median near the 5\,s sampling floor is expected---most tool calls are quick---
but the tail is what matters for a scheduler, and it sits at the scale of the
16.85\,s mean tool wait.

Second, the two components move independently. \texttt{bres}
(\S\ref{sec:results}) reduces idle from 15.9\% to 10.1\%, recovering about 17
minutes---and is nonetheless \emph{slower} than its own baseline, because its
busy time grows by 21 minutes. Any account that treats ``more concurrency'' as
one thing will get this wrong.

\subsection{What determines busy time}

During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
running request. Hence
\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
  \label{eq:busy}
\end{equation}
with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and
$\tau$ the per-step cost. Across our runs $\tau$ is not a fit parameter we
tuned; we obtain it two ways and check that they agree.

\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} /
G$. Two choices in that expression are easy to get wrong and both matter. The
time term must be the \emph{busy} component, not the wall clock---using the
wall clock charges idle time to the steps. And $\overline{R}$ must be the mean
running count \emph{during busy time}, not the mean over the whole run: the
whole-run mean includes the idle samples, where the count is zero, so it equals
$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \times$ (busy fraction). Using it makes $\tau_1$
too low by exactly the busy fraction---26\% for \texttt{cap1}, whose idle
share is 36.3\%. We report both columns in the data, and use the
busy-conditional mean throughout; the whole-run mean is what an earlier
revision of this paper used, and it made the two methods agree far better than
they do.

\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} For each step record we compute the
per-request decode interval,
$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$,
and take the median over steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$.

The two do not agree exactly, and the disagreement is informative rather than
embarrassing. $\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every run, by 8--40\%, and the gap
is the share of engine steps that are prefill and therefore emit no output
token: $\tau_1$ averages over all steps, $\tau_2$ measures only the decode
interval. That share comes out at 15--28\% across the five device-only
configurations, which is the right order of magnitude for a workload whose
prompts are 23{,}k tokens median against sub-1{,}k outputs. So the two are not
two estimates of one number; they are measurements of two different things,
and we now say so. Method 2 remains the one that does not depend on the
quantity it is used to explain, and it is the one we quote when the question
is the cost of a decode step. The engine's own latency counters
(\texttt{itl\_p50}, \texttt{e2e\_p50}, \texttt{ttft\_p50}) are exported as zero
by SGLang v0.5.10 in our configuration, so no directly measured step time
exists in our data; instrumenting this is future work.

\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}

Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is
not. Across our 13 runs $\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and $\tau_2$
from 20.6 to 33.1\,ms, and both rise with the running count: fitting
Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$
($R^2 = 0.89$ for the latter, Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).

The relation is a trend, not a law. Two host-tier configurations with similar
running counts behave differently: \texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at
29.8\,ms while \texttt{F:hz0} runs 1.66 at 35.7\,ms. Running count sets a
floor and a trend, not a value---and on the host-tier family, as we show below,
it does not set the value at all.

This is the crux of the paper, and we state it at the strength the arithmetic
supports. Hold $\tau_1$ at its value for \texttt{cap1}, 28.8\,ms, and busy
time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from 1.00 to 1.84
should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to 184. The
measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes back 50 of
the 156 minutes that the extra concurrency would otherwise have bought---\emph{a
third} of the gain, not all of it and, as an earlier revision of this paper
claimed, not more than half. The other two thirds are real: raising
concurrency does make the engine faster. What it does not do is make it faster
in proportion, and the shortfall compounds with every controller that tries to
exploit the difference.

\paragraph{What the engine is doing when it is not decoding.} SGLang exposes
two counters that answer this directly: tokens evicted from the radix cache,
and prompt tokens that had to be prefilled rather than reused. Across the five
static caps \emph{without} HiCache the two track each other almost exactly.
\texttt{cap1} evicts 9.2\,M tokens and prefills 8.6\,M uncached;
\texttt{cap5} evicts 21.3\,M and prefills 20.7\,M; the gap is 0.6--0.7\,M on
every one of the five. An evicted prefix is recomputed, close to one token for
one, and that recomputation is chunked into decode steps. Those same five runs
give a monotone relation between recomputation and $\tau$: 8.6\,M uncached
tokens at 18.4\,ms, 9.2 at 22.4, 11.5 at 25.5, 15.4 at 29.0, 20.7 at 32.2.
That is the mechanism, and on the device-only configurations it explains the
$\tau$ trend completely.

\paragraph{Where the mechanism stops.} With HiCache enabled the
correspondence breaks---and it breaks in a way that confirms the mechanism
rather than undermining it. The device cache misses as much as before:
\texttt{cap4\_hc} misses 22.4\,M tokens on the device, more than the
device-only \texttt{cap4} misses. But only 9.1\,M of that is recomputed; the
other 13.3\,M is served from the host tier. Across the eight host-tier runs
the host serves 13--19\,M tokens each, and across the five device-only runs it
serves \emph{exactly zero}---the device miss equals the recomputed count to
within rounding on all five. Recomputation is still what the device cannot
absorb; the host tier is a second absorber in front of it.

That does not resolve the \sys{} family, and we say so plainly. Those runs sit
at a flat 8.6--8.7\,M recomputed tokens while $\tau$ ranges from 22.5 to
30.6\,ms. Whatever moves $\tau$ there is not recomputation, and we do not
know what it is. We flag it as the largest open question in the paper, and note
that the negative result does not rest on it: the cancellation is measured
directly, not inferred from the mechanism.

\paragraph{A refutation we had to withdraw.} An earlier revision withdrew this
mechanism outright, on two checks that were both wrong. The first was that
total prefilled tokens equalled the trace's planned prefill to a ratio of 1.00,
so no token could have been computed twice. \texttt{prompt\_tokens\_total}
counts the full submitted prompt \emph{including} its cached portion, so it
equals the sum of prompt lengths by construction and cannot detect
recomputation; the quantity that can is the uncached count used above, which
varies by 2.4$\times$ across these runs. The second was that SGLang reported
no retractions. Radix-cache eviction is not a retraction---retractions count
running requests preempted mid-flight, a different event with its own counter.
We record the episode because it is the same failure mode as the segment
argument of \S\ref{sec:bg}: a check that looks like a measurement but is an
identity.

\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}

Idle time is a property of the workload, not of the controller. A session in a
tool wait holds its permit and its KV but issues no request; with only about
two live sessions, the probability that all of them are simultaneously waiting
is substantial. The direct evidence is the duration of the stretches
themselves: they are of the same order as the tool waits, and an order of
magnitude longer than the 2\,s control cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). A
controller cannot be blamed for a gap that is set by the tool.

We note one claim we had to drop here. An earlier draft reported that idle
fraction falls monotonically with mean concurrency. With the full data it does
not: \texttt{F:hz0} at 1.41 running requests idles 15.2\% while \texttt{cap4}
at 1.48 idles 10.4\%. The weaker statement that survives is the one we use---%
the stretches are structural and long.

The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always
ready---is what \texttt{bres} does. It works in the narrow sense: \texttt{bres}
recovers about 17 of the minutes that its own baseline spends idle. It is
still slower overall, because the sessions it keeps live raise the step cost
by more than the recovered idle is worth. Idle is not unrecoverable; it is
recoverable at a price that is not worth paying here.
The structural remedy would be to make the engine do work during a wait, but
there is no work to do: the session's next round does not exist until its tool
returns.
