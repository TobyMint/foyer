\section{Decomposing the Wall Clock}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Two components}

We split the wall clock at every sampled instant by whether the engine has any
request in flight:
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; W_{\text{busy}} \;+\; W_{\text{idle}} .
  \label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the split for the seventeen of the thirty-nine runs
of Table~\ref{tab:runs}'s families we have per-step logs for. Idle time is not
noise: it spans 6.3\% to 41.9\% of the sampled span, and 6.4\% to 42.5\% against
the request wall clock of Table~\ref{tab:runs}, and it is not spread evenly---over
2{,}374 stretches the mean is 20.6\,s and 17.7\% of them exceed 20\,s, accounting
for 69.5\% of all idle \emph{time}, the tail a scheduler feels. We report the mean
and the share rather than the median, which is not robust to which runs enter the
pool: it reads 10.0\,s without \texttt{cap1} and 5.0\,s with it. The mean
(18.9--20.6\,s) and the over-20\,s share (66.1--69.5\%) move by little across the
four variants in \texttt{idle\_pooled.csv}---two distinct pools---and the claim
that the tail dominates does not depend on the choice.

The two components move independently and in opposite directions, so neither is a
proxy for the wall clock. The no-control baseline (\S\ref{sec:results}) cuts idle
time by 13.8 minutes against the base configuration---it admits everything, so
nothing queues---and finishes only 2.7 minutes sooner, since its busy time grows
by 11.1 minutes; the trade costs it the SLO column outright, 89.8\% down to 3.0\%.
The arm that disables every throttle moves the other way, and further: idle
\emph{rises} by 129 minutes while busy \emph{falls} by 5.4, so the 124 minutes it
adds are almost entirely waiting, bought with 43.6\,pp of SLO. Neither example is
one of the damaged runs of \S\ref{sec:repro}---both are clean in the co-tenancy
audit---and neither belongs to the controller family whose clean step-cost range
that section quotes.

\subsection{What determines busy time}

During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
running request. Hence \cite{little1961}
\begin{equation}
  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
  \label{eq:busy}
\end{equation}
with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and $\tau$
the per-step cost---not a fit parameter we tuned: we obtain it two independent
ways.

\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} / G$,
with $\overline{R}$ the mean running count \emph{during busy time}---the
busy-conditional mean throughout, since the whole-run mean includes the zero-count
idle samples and would make $\tau_1$ short by the \emph{idle} fraction, 36.3\% for
\texttt{cap1}, whose busy share is 63.7\%.

\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} The median per-request decode interval
$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$ over
steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$: an independent cross-check that does not
depend on the quantity it explains, and the one we quote for the cost of a decode
step. The two disagree---$\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every
run we accept, by 8.4--39.7\% over the 38 left once the repeat excluded below is
dropped. The gap
is \emph{consistent with} engine steps that are prefill and therefore emit no
output token---we can bound it, not identify it, because subtracting a median from
a mean does not isolate a component: it is a ratio of \emph{times}, not of steps,
and we have no measurement of the fraction of step \emph{counts} that are
prefill, and even as a time ratio it is the gap between two measurements, not a
decomposition of one---so we cannot rule out that some of the difference is the two
estimators disagreeing for other reasons. They are therefore not two estimates of one number
but measurements of two different things, and we do not size a component with their
difference.
(The \texttt{horizon}=1 repeat is excluded from the reuse accounting: its token
counters are anomalous, so its $\tau$ columns, reuse and recomputation are
unusable, while its wall clock and SLO---from the client---are ones we use.) The
engine's own latency counters are exported as zero by SGLang v0.5.10.post1, so no
directly measured step time exists in our data, and the step cost we quote is
inferred rather than read \cite{fwagnostic}.

\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}

Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is not:
across the fourteen runs we fitted, $\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and
$\tau_2$ from 20.6 to 31.2\,ms, and both rise with the running count---fitting
Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).
The relation is a trend, not a law:
\texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at 29.8\,ms while \texttt{horizon}$=0$ runs
1.58 at 30.2, so running count sets a floor and a trend, not a value, and on the
host-tier family, as we show below, not the value at all.

This is the crux of the paper. Hold $\tau_1$ at its \texttt{cap1} value of
28.8\,ms and busy time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from
1.00 to 1.84 should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to
184, but the measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes
back 50 of the 156 minutes the extra concurrency would otherwise have
bought---\emph{a third} of the gain, not all of it and, as an earlier revision
claimed, not more than half; the shortfall compounds with every controller that
tries to exploit the difference.

\paragraph{What the engine is doing when it is not decoding.} Across the five
static caps \emph{without} HiCache, tokens evicted from the radix cache
\cite{h2o,scissorhands} and prompt
tokens that had to be prefilled rather than reused track each other almost
exactly---an evicted prefix is recomputed, close to one token for one, and that
recomputation is chunked into decode steps---and recomputation rises with $\tau$
across four of the five points, the exception being \texttt{cap1}. It is five
points on
five policies, not an intervention---we did not hold concurrency fixed and vary
eviction---so we call it consistent, not demonstrated.

\paragraph{Where the mechanism stops.} With HiCache the correspondence breaks in a
way that confirms the mechanism rather than undermining it: \texttt{cap4\_hc}
misses 22.4\,M tokens on the device, more than the device-only \texttt{cap4}, but
only 9.1\,M of that is recomputed and the other 13.3\,M is served from the host
tier, which across the thirty host-tier runs serves 3.1--19.3\,M each.
Recomputation is still what the device cannot absorb; the host tier is a second
absorber in front of it \cite{gao2024cachedattention}.

\subsection{What tracks reuse is the tier, not the policy}

The device-miss column hides the quantity that matters: \emph{reuse} is the prompt
tokens served from anywhere in the memory hierarchy---device radix cache plus host
tier---so $\text{reuse}+\text{recomputed}=\text{prompt}$, fixed at 28.5\,M by the
trace. The bound on reuse has to be defined independently of what we observed, or
the argument is circular: the planned reusable prefix over all 999 rounds of
\texttt{replay\_pois200\_l04.csv} totals 21{,}540{,}371 tokens against a prompt of
28{,}526{,}871, so no policy can reuse more than 75.5\%. It is a bound rather than
a description---no configuration we ran reaches it, the best realizes
92.5\%---and how much of the bound is realized is where the tier enters.

Device-only, realization falls from 92.5\% to 36.4\%, the shortfall being
recomputation; with a host tier it holds at 87.9--92.4\% on the four static
host-tier caps above and at 59.7--92.5\% across the thirty host-tier runs
that completed this trace at a \emph{single} configuration (HiCache on, at least
995 of its 999 steps; the one run that alternates its cap, \S\ref{sec:bg}, is not
one of them), the lowest \texttt{cap8}+HC at 59.7\%. The \emph{device} misses more
where we can pair them (\texttt{cap3}+HC 26.0\,M against \texttt{cap3}'s
11.5\,M) while the host serves 3.1--19.3\,M in front of the recomputation, but the
ordering is \emph{not} universal---at a cap of five the host-tier run's device
misses 19.0\,M against the device-only run's 20.7\,M---and we report the reversal
with no mechanism for it. Five runs meeting the predicate are left out for reasons
that are not mechanical: one whose prompt-token counter reads $1.41\times$ the
trace plan with neither client-side retries nor retractions to account for the
excess, and four superseded measurements we re-ran clean (\S\ref{sec:repro}).
(75.5\% is the trace's bound as a share of the prompt, and 92.5\% a share of
\emph{that bound}; \texttt{reuse.csv} carries both.)

Device occupancy does not explain the split: \texttt{cap3\_fixed} and
\texttt{cap3\_hc} run at nearly the same occupancy (55.8\% and 55.5\%) and running
count (1.53 and 1.51) yet realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound---occupancy is
instantaneous while reuse depends on history, a session's prefix having to survive
from one round to the next, and we do not have the measurement that would separate
those. The device-only side is one run there, but a repeat has since finished: the
gap becomes 9.8 points rather than 12.2 and the side is no longer a single sample,
its two runs differing by 2.4\,pp (2.4299 before rounding, as with every other gap
here). A pre-registered back-to-back pairing of the two tiers on one card, its
criterion written down in advance, has since finished: \emph{it lands at $10.1$
points---a pass by $0.14$}, against a threshold fixed at $10$ points, its runs
differing by 0.8\% in mean occupancy and 3.1\% in running count, both inside the
10\% tolerance the criterion requires before it will read the comparison. The pair
is not a paired experiment---same
harness generation, about a day and two GPUs apart---and pairing the repeat with
the host-tier run removes the card half of the confound, though those two remain
three and a half days and a generation apart (\S\ref{sec:repro} states what we did
to rule out co-tenancy).

A capacity-controlled version---same trace and policy, pool cut to half or three
quarters---does not return a realization figure: a step whose prompt exceeds the
pool is never executed, the engine answers with a single token, and the runner
records that as a success, so the failure counter stays at zero and the loss is
invisible in every counter we have. The rule is exact rather than statistical, in
one direction without exception---every step reporting \emph{one prompt token}
while planning tens of thousands has a prompt larger than the pool (six runs at
four pool sizes, no counterexample), though not the same set as \emph{steps that
generate one token}, which clean runs contain too. The full pool (101{,}432 tokens)
holds the trace's longest prompt (89{,}436), so the control marks a boundary rather
than a point on the curve, and the comparison is bounded by the trace, not by the
system; it shows capacity matters, but capacity is not occupancy, so it does not
separate the two accounts either. The claim this section supports is
the narrow one: within a configuration the host tier preserves realization at a
matched running count and a matched occupancy, and without it realization
falls---a correlation we can name, on a pair confounded in the two ways above. We
do not treat the arithmetic as a result: it assumes KV demand grows linearly in the
running count, which heterogeneous sessions need not satisfy.

\paragraph{What is left unexplained.} Four of the arms above---the resident base,
\texttt{horizon}$=0$, \texttt{horizon}$=2$ and the \texttt{horizon}$=1$ no-hw
arm---were \emph{first} measured while three other engine instances shared the host,
which inflates per-step cost by 12--41\% and leaves the volume columns untouched
(\S\ref{sec:repro}); all four have since been re-measured alone, and the values
above are the clean ones. (The flagged runs are superseded and not in the table;
the labels name the arms.)

\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}

Some idle is structural: a session in a tool wait issues no request, and an
admission policy sets how many sessions are live, so an individual stretch is the
workload's and how much a \emph{run} accumulates is not.
The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always ready---is
what \texttt{bres} does, and the clean measurement of it says the remedy works and
does not pay: what it does not do is improve the criterion (\S\ref{sec:results}).
Idle is recoverable in part; recovering it is not what pays.
