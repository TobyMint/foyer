\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An agentic LLM session is not a request. It is a long-lived job that issues a
round of inference, waits on a tool, and comes back---tens of times---with a
context that grows every round \cite{zhu2026tracelab,continuum,talaria}. On a
memory-constrained accelerator, where every round carries a first-token deadline
\cite{lmjamming}, admission control---which rounds to admit---looks like the
obvious lever: the KV pool is the binding resource
\cite{kwon2023pagedattention,zerowsap,rtswap}, a single large session can occupy
most of it, and sessions are heterogeneous.

We believed this, built a controller that does exactly that, and spent a
considerable amount of GPU time discovering that on our workload and hardware it
is false. That negative result is most of what we have.

\subsection{What we measured}

We serve a replay of 200 real coding-agent sessions on a single RTX 3090
(Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, 96K context, SGLang, a 101{,}432-token KV pool) and compare
admission policies against a sweep of static concurrency caps. Nine variants of
our own controller span three mechanistically distinct ideas, a fourth is tested
by two further arms, and a reimplementation of an independent congestion-based
controller \cite{concur} runs alongside them; an all-throttles-off ablation runs
a different controller and is not counted. None of the nine is above the static
Pareto front---the static admission baseline for the same trace, deadline and
resource budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). Its best, now measured twice, reads
$-0.30$ and $+0.22$\,pp of deadline satisfaction rate (first tokens, timed from
the engine; \S\ref{sec:bg})---either side of the line,
both inside the $\pm1.0$\,pp band we call a tie, so we record it as on the line:
no improvement outside that band over the static baseline. One further arm lands above it
on two of its three runs---the same controller revision, one expression
changed, its third on the line (\S\ref{sec:results}). Four of the nine were measured while other jobs
shared the host; we re-measured all four, which moved them 11.2, 6.5, 6.4 and
4.5 points closer to the front without crossing it.

\subsection{Why}

We decompose the wall clock into \emph{busy} time (the engine has at least one
request in flight) and \emph{idle} time (none).
Busy time is governed by $W_{\text{busy}} \approx
(G/\overline{R})\,\tau$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:busy})---$\overline{R}$ the mean running
requests, $G$ the generated tokens, $\tau$ the per-step cost---and admission
control moves $\overline{R}$ and little else. But $\tau$ is not constant in
$\overline{R}$: across our runs $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.7\,ms, and on the
device-only family raising the busy-time running count from 1.00 to 1.84 absorbs
a third of the busy time the extra concurrency would otherwise have saved
(\S\ref{sec:model}).

The idle component is structural rather than a control failure: a session in a
tool wait issues no request \cite{mori}, so when all live sessions happen to be
waiting the engine is genuinely empty. The stretches are long in the tail---66.1
to 69.5\% of all idle \emph{time} sits in stretches over 20\,s, an order of
magnitude above the 2\,s control cycle---and the demanded median inter-round gap
is 0.34\,s while the \emph{mean} is 15.8--16.9\,s, with 18\% of those
transitions wait more than ten seconds.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify the memory tier as what determines, in our
        measurements, how much of the achievable prefix reuse survives added
        concurrency, separating the \emph{local} return on added concurrency
        from whether a controller wins \emph{end to end}, where idle time and
        the SLO axis also move (\S\ref{sec:xtrace}, \S\ref{sec:criterion});
        a second workload with the two conditions associated with room still
        does not win.
  \item We test four routes by which an adaptive admission controller was
        expected to beat a tuned static cap---two falsified against criteria
        fixed before the runs, one a knob rather than a hypothesis, the fourth
        falsified by an argument about what the permit mechanism cannot
        express---and we do not call the last two falsifications of a stated
        hypothesis, because they were not.
  \item We give a two-part decomposition of the wall clock (\S\ref{sec:model})
        that locates the cancellation, and reduce the question to one measurable
        number (\S\ref{sec:criterion}), the elasticity of per-step \emph{service
        time} with respect to the running count---of the step, not of the
        request---offered as a diagnostic rather than a predictor, and
        \S\ref{sec:criterion} is explicit about the sense in which it is
        circular.
  \item We make the measurements reproducible from the raw per-step logs
        (\S\ref{sec:repro}) and report the seven claims we had to retract.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What we do not claim}

We do not claim admission control is useless in general: we observe no gain beyond
the band \emph{here}---a gain inside it is not excluded---and we show the frontier
measurement that decides that; the
diagnostic of \S\ref{sec:criterion} is a cheaper check an engineer can run before
committing to a sweep, not a verdict on its own. Nor do we claim our hardware and model are
representative---one 7B model on two accelerators of different sizes. On the larger card, where we have since run a
controller and extended the static sweep to meet it, the controller lands
0.1\,pp \emph{above} the static front at its own wall clock, inside the tie band
(\S\ref{sec:a100}). We read that as a second configuration \emph{not
overturning} the negative result, and deliberately not as the result reproducing
at full strength: it is \emph{one} controller run, and our pre-registered criterion
for that tier does not cover it.
