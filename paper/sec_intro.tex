\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An agentic LLM session is not a request. It is a long-lived job that issues a
round of inference, waits on a tool, and comes back---tens of times---with a
context that grows every round. On a memory-constrained accelerator this makes
admission control look like the obvious lever. The KV pool is the binding
resource; a single large session can occupy most of it; and the sessions
themselves are wildly heterogeneous. If a scheduler can see how much of the
pool a candidate will need and how much room is left, surely it should beat a
fixed number.

We believed this, built a controller that does exactly that, and spent a
considerable amount of GPU time discovering that on our workload and hardware
it is false. This paper reports that negative result, and the negative result is
most of what we have. Alongside it we report one measurement---the elasticity of
per-step cost with respect to the running count---that we hoped would say in
advance how much room admission control has. It does bound that room on the
configurations we measured, but when we tested whether it explains our
controllers' failure, on their own configuration and on a second workload, it
did not. We report it as a diagnostic of a workload, not as a predictor of
whether a controller will win.

\subsection{What we measured}

We serve a replay of 200 real coding-agent sessions on a single RTX 3090
(Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, 96K context, SGLang, a 101{,}432-token KV pool) and compare
a family of admission policies against a sweep of static concurrency caps.
Nine controller variants, spanning four mechanistically distinct ideas, all
land below the static Pareto front, and so does a reimplementation of an
independent congestion-based controller from the literature
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). The best of them misses by under a point---inside the
$\pm1.0$\,pp band we call a tie, but on the below side of it---and its repeat
misses by 5.6.

\subsection{Why}

We decompose the wall clock into \emph{busy} time, during which the engine has
at least one request in flight, and \emph{idle} time, during which it has
none. The busy component is governed by $W_{\text{busy}} \approx
(G/\overline{R})\,\tau$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:busy}), where $\overline{R}$ is the mean
number of running requests, $G$ the generated tokens and $\tau$ the per-step
cost. An admission policy moves $\overline{R}$, and on our configurations it
moves little else---\texttt{bres} is the exception, and \S\ref{sec:model}
uses it to show why moving both components at once is still not enough.
The difficulty is that $\tau$ is not constant in $\overline{R}$: across our
runs $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.7\,ms, and on the device-only family, raising the
busy-time running count from 1.00 to 1.84 absorbs a third of what the extra
concurrency buys.

The reason $\tau$ moves is recomputation, and whether recomputation happens is
decided by the memory tier rather than by the policy. A session's prefix is
worth reusing only while it is still resident, so prefix \emph{reuse} is
bounded by the workload---in our trace, 75.5\% of the prompt tokens, a bound
no configuration we ran reaches. How much of that bound is realized is where
the tier enters. With the KV pool on the device alone, realization falls as
concurrency rises---92.6\% of the bound at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00
down to 36.3\% at 1.84---and the shortfall appears as recomputation, 8.6\,M to
20.7\,M tokens. With a host tier in front of it, realization holds across the
range our controllers occupy---90.2\% to 92.3\% between 1.25 and 1.73---even
though the \emph{device} misses more. Two configurations at the same running
count and the same pool occupancy realize 78.9\% and 91.1\% of the bound
respectively (\S\ref{sec:model}); what separates them is not how full the pool
is but what happens when it overflows. That pair also differs in date and in
GPU, so a capacity control that changes the pool size alone on one card is
queued behind the re-runs; \S\ref{sec:results} reports it when it lands.
That is the finding we would carry to another system first, and it is why this
paper's answer to its own title is a property of the deployment rather than of
the controller.

The idle component is structural rather than a control failure. A session in a
tool wait issues no request, so when all live sessions happen to be waiting,
the engine is genuinely empty. The stretches are short in the middle and long
in the tail---mean 20.6\,s---and the tail is what a scheduler feels: 69.6\%
of all idle \emph{time} sits in stretches over 20\,s, an order of magnitude
above the 2\,s control cycle. (The median is not a good summary here: it moves
between 5 and 10\,s depending on which runs are pooled, because one long run
contributes a fifth of the stretches.) Crucially, the tool wait itself is much longer than
the trace's median suggests: the median inter-round gap is 0.35\,s, but the
\emph{mean} is 15.8--16.9\,s with 22\% of gaps over ten seconds. A controller
that
reads only the median concludes, as we initially did, that sessions never
leave and therefore that there is no idle time to recover. There is.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{We identify the memory tier as what decides how much of the
        achievable prefix reuse survives added concurrency, and therefore
        whether admission control has any room.} The trace bounds reuse at
        75.5\% of its prompt tokens, and no configuration we ran reaches that
        bound---the best realizes 92.6\% of it. What the tier decides is how
        much of the bound is realized as concurrency rises: device-only,
        realization falls from 92.6\% to 36.3\% between
        $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00 and 1.84 and the shortfall appears
        as recomputation, 8.6\,M to 20.7\,M tokens; with a host tier it holds
        at 90.2--92.3\% over 1.25 to 1.73, while the \emph{device} misses more
        (\S\ref{sec:model}). Two configurations at the same running count and
        the same pool occupancy realize 78.9\% and 91.1\% of the bound, so it
        is not how full the pool is but what happens when it overflows; that
        pair differs in date and GPU as well, so we also vary the pool alone
        on one card; that run is queued and \S\ref{sec:results} reports it when
        it lands. The per-step elasticity follows the same line:
        it passes one above $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$
        device-only and stays below one across the controllers' whole
        operating range with a host tier (\S\ref{sec:criterion}). Two
        measurements that do not share a code path---client-side step timing
        and engine-side token accounting---give the same answer. This is the
        finding we would carry to another system first, and it is what the
        title's question turns on.
  \item We test four routes by which an adaptive admission controller was
        expected to beat a tuned static cap on this workload, and we are
        precise about what each one rests on. Two are falsified by measurement
        against criteria we fixed before the runs---changing the accounting
        base (\S\ref{sec:results}) and changing the growth reserve. One is a
        knob rather than a hypothesis, and we report it only because it slides
        the operating point along the front and adds no mechanism. The fourth,
        changing the execution granularity from session count to
        running-request count, is falsified by measurement \emph{and} by an
        argument about why the permit mechanism cannot express the control it
        would need. We do not call the last two of these falsifications of a
        stated hypothesis, because they were not.
  \item We give a two-part decomposition of the wall clock (\S\ref{sec:model})
        that locates the cancellation: admission policy moves the
        running-request count, and the per-step cost moves with it. On the
        device-only configurations we can account for that rise token for
        token---evicted prefixes are recomputed almost one for one. On
        host-tier configurations recomputation is flat and the account has to
        come from elsewhere; we report the part of that residual we were able
        to attribute, and the part we were not (\S\ref{sec:model},
        \S\ref{sec:repro}).
  \item We reduce the question to one measurable number, the elasticity of
        per-step cost with respect to the running count (\S\ref{sec:criterion}),
        and measure it at $0.38$ on the model-consistent step cost and $0.61$
        on the per-request one, on the configurations whose mechanism we can
        account for token for token. We offer it as a diagnostic rather than a
        predictor: it says where on the curve a policy operates and how much
        the remaining headroom is worth, and \S\ref{sec:criterion} is explicit
        about the sense in which it is circular and the sense in which it is
        not.
  \item We characterize when adaptivity \emph{could} have paid, and show that
        our workload is not that case: the same throttle configuration that is
        worth $+51\%$ at 25 sessions costs $-42\%$ at 200 (\S\ref{sec:load}).
        Put differently, no single scalar cap is optimal across both, which is
        the regime adaptive control exists for. On the low-load tier, where
        that argument bites hardest, the controller is \emph{not} worse---%
        same-generation measurements put it on the front, $+0.9$\,pp above the
        line and inside the noise. On the high-load tier it is dominated. That
        asymmetry, not a blanket failure, is what this paper measures.
  \item We make the measurements reproducible: the front and each controller's
        signed distance are produced by one script from the raw per-step logs,
        the tables and the sub-thousand-step runs by two others, all named in
        \S\ref{sec:repro}. We report the six claims we had to retract along the
        way, with the checks that caught them.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What we do not claim}

We do not claim that admission control is useless in general. We claim it does
not pay \emph{here}, we show the measurement that decides it, and we argue
(\S\ref{sec:criterion}) that the same measurement can be taken on another
workload before committing to a sweep. We also do not claim our hardware and model are
representative; our results are from one 24\,GB accelerator and one 7B model,
and extending them is future work.
