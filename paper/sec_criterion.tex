\section{A Criterion for When Admission Control Can Pay}
\label{sec:criterion}

The decomposition turns the
question into one number. Throughput during busy time is
$\overline{R}/\tau$, so raising concurrency by a factor $f$ buys a factor
$f^{1-\epsilon}$, where
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln \tau}{\mathrm{d}\ln \overline{R}}
  \label{eq:elasticity}
\end{equation}
is the elasticity of per-step cost with respect to the running count. At
$\epsilon = 0$ the step cost is flat, concurrency converts one for one into
throughput, and an admission controller has room to work. At $\epsilon = 1$
the two cancel exactly and it has none. Everything in between is a
quantitative statement about how much room.

On the five device-only configurations, where \S\ref{sec:model} establishes
that the step cost is driven by recomputation, $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$
runs from 1.00 to 1.84. A least-squares fit of Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} over
those five points gives $\epsilon = 0.61$ on the per-request measurement
($R^2 = 0.89$), or $0.38$ on the model-consistent one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}). The practical reading is that every 10\% of added
concurrency buys roughly 4--6\% of throughput, depending on which step cost you
can measure. That is a statement about what added concurrency is worth on this
workload. It is \emph{not} a proof that a controller must lose---a controller
could still win by raising concurrency at times a fixed cap does not---so we
report it alongside the measurements of \S\ref{sec:results}, not instead of
them. We offer $\epsilon$ as a diagnostic, with a caveat we would rather state
than bury. On our data it is computed from the same wall clock the model then
explains---$\tau_1$ is derived from $W_{\text{busy}}$, so using it to predict
$W_{\text{busy}}$ restates rather than tests---and its five points are five
different policies, so the slope is a cross-policy association, not a
transportable causal derivative. $\tau_2$ is genuinely independent of the wall
clock but measures only decode, so it cannot be dropped into a throughput
equation that includes prefill. What survives is a comparison tool on a single
workload: the elasticity says how quickly per-step cost eats added concurrency
\emph{here}, and it can be computed from two runs before committing to a
sweep. Whether it orders \emph{different} workloads the way it orders
concurrency on this one is untested, and we do not claim it.
It is not, as an earlier draft of this paper claimed, something that decides
the question in minutes before building anything.
