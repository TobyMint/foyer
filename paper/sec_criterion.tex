\section{A Criterion for When Admission Control Can Pay}
\label{sec:criterion}

The decomposition turns the
question into one number. Throughput during busy time is
$\overline{R}/\tau$, so raising concurrency by a factor $f$ buys a factor
$f^{1-\epsilon}$, where
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln \tau}{\mathrm{d}\ln \overline{R}}
  \label{eq:elasticity}
\end{equation}
is the elasticity of per-step cost with respect to the running count. At
$\epsilon = 0$ the step cost is flat, concurrency converts one for one into
throughput, and an admission controller has room to work. At $\epsilon = 1$
the two cancel exactly and it has none. Everything in between is a
quantitative statement about how much room.

On the five device-only configurations, where \S\ref{sec:model} establishes
that the step cost is driven by recomputation, $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$
runs from 1.00 to 1.84. A least-squares fit of Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} over
those five points gives $\epsilon = 0.61$ on the per-request measurement
($R^2 = 0.89$), or $0.38$ on the model-consistent one (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).

That fit is a straight line through a curve, and the curve is what matters. The
segment elasticities are $\epsilon = -0.21$ from \texttt{cap1} to
\texttt{cap2}, $+0.43$ from \texttt{cap2} to \texttt{cap3}, $+1.15$ from
\texttt{cap3} to \texttt{cap4}, and $+1.14$ from \texttt{cap4} to
\texttt{cap5}. Below $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.3$ added
concurrency is nearly free; above it, $\epsilon$ passes one, which means the
engine gets \emph{slower} in wall clock as more requests run. The single
number $0.38$ averages those regimes and describes none of them.

The operating region is the one that matters, and most of it is the expensive
one. Our nine controller arms run at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ between 1.35
and 1.69. Four sit below 1.53, in the $\epsilon = 0.43$ segment where added
concurrency still pays a little; the other five sit at 1.63 or above, in the
$\epsilon \approx 1.15$ segment where it does not pay at all. A controller
that admits more sessions there does not buy throughput; it buys the same work
spread over more steps. This is a sharper statement than the
global elasticity and it points the other way: the criterion is not ``how much
does added concurrency cost on this workload'' but ``where on the curve does
\emph{this} policy operate'', and a policy that cannot move left along the
curve gains nothing from moving right.

We are explicit about what this does and does not establish. It explains why
pushing concurrency does not pay in the region our controllers reach. It does
not by itself explain why they do not instead operate further left with a
better SLO, which is the axis \S\ref{sec:results:idle-memory} and the
queueing measurement of \S\ref{sec:discussion} address. And it is measured on
five different policies rather than by an intervention, so it is a
cross-policy association. We offer $\epsilon$ as a diagnostic, with a caveat we would rather state
than bury. On our data it is computed from the same wall clock the model then
explains---$\tau_1$ is derived from $W_{\text{busy}}$, so using it to predict
$W_{\text{busy}}$ restates rather than tests---and its five points are five
different policies, so the slope is a cross-policy association, not a
transportable causal derivative. $\tau_2$ is genuinely independent of the wall
clock but measures only decode, so it cannot be dropped into a throughput
equation that includes prefill. What survives is a comparison tool on a single
workload: the elasticity says how quickly per-step cost eats added concurrency
\emph{here}, and it can be computed from two runs before committing to a
sweep. Whether it orders \emph{different} workloads the way it orders
concurrency on this one is untested, and we do not claim it.
It is not, as an earlier draft of this paper claimed, something that decides
the question in minutes before building anything.
