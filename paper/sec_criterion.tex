\section{A Criterion for When Admission Control Can Pay}
\label{sec:criterion}

The decomposition turns the
question into one number. Throughput during busy time is
$\overline{R}/\tau$, so raising concurrency by a factor $f$ buys a factor
$f^{1-\epsilon}$, where
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln \tau}{\mathrm{d}\ln \overline{R}}
  \label{eq:elasticity}
\end{equation}
is the elasticity of per-step cost with respect to the running count. At
$\epsilon = 0$ the step cost is flat, concurrency converts one for one into
throughput, and an admission controller has room to work. At $\epsilon = 1$
the two cancel exactly and it has none. Everything in between is a
quantitative statement about how much room.

On the five device-only configurations, where \S\ref{sec:model} establishes
that the step cost is driven by recomputation, $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$
runs from 1.00 to 1.84. A least-squares fit of Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} over
those five points gives $\epsilon = 0.61$ on the per-request measurement
($R^2 = 0.89$), or $0.38$ on the model-consistent one (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).

That fit is a straight line through a curve, and the curve is what matters. The
segment elasticities are $\epsilon = -0.21$ from \texttt{cap1} to
\texttt{cap2}, $+0.43$ from \texttt{cap2} to \texttt{cap3}, $+1.15$ from
\texttt{cap3} to \texttt{cap4}, and $+1.14$ from \texttt{cap4} to
\texttt{cap5}. Below $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$ added
concurrency still buys something; above it, $\epsilon$ passes one, which means
the engine gets \emph{slower} in wall clock as more requests run. The single
number $0.38$ averages those regimes and describes none of them.

The operating region is the one that matters, and it is not the region the
controllers run in. All nine controller arms run with HiCache enabled, which
the five device-only caps above do not. Measuring the same elasticity on the
three HiCache configurations we have gives $\epsilon = +0.19$ from
\texttt{cap2}+HC to \texttt{cap3}+HC and $+0.46$ from \texttt{cap3}+HC to
\texttt{cap4}+HC, over $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.25 to 1.73. That range
covers the whole controller operating region (1.35 to 1.69), and over it
$\epsilon$ never approaches one. At matched running counts the HiCache caps pay
$1.7$\,ms less per step at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$.

This contradicts what an earlier draft of this paper claimed, and it changes
what the criterion is for. The cliff in the device-only curve is a
recomputation effect: \S\ref{sec:model} measures that those runs recompute what
they evict, while under HiCache the host tier absorbs it. The cliff does not
survive on the configuration our controllers actually use. So the criterion
does \emph{not} explain why they lose---on their own configuration they were
not in a region where added concurrency was self-defeating, and the engine had
room they did not take. What the measurement does is rule one explanation out.
The failure is not that this workload denies admission control its lever; it is
that our controllers do not pull the lever they have, which is what
\S\ref{sec:results:idle-memory} and the attribution of
\S\ref{sec:results:attribution} measure directly.

We are explicit about what this does and does not establish. The HiCache curve
rests on three configurations and two segments, one run each, with no
error bars, and it stops at 1.73---we cannot see whether a cliff appears
beyond the controllers' region. A $\pm10\%$ perturbation of $\tau$ puts the
segment elasticities' bounds across one even on the device-only curve, so the
cliff is a description of the fitted points rather than a threshold we have
located precisely. And it is measured on
different policies rather than by an intervention, so it is a
cross-policy association---the two configurations the tier comparison rests on
were run a day apart on different cards, which \S\ref{sec:model} states, and a
same-card pairing is queued. On our data it is also computed from the same wall
clock the model then explains---$\tau_1$ is derived from $W_{\text{busy}}$, so
using it to predict $W_{\text{busy}}$ restates rather than tests---and its
points are different policies, so the slope is a cross-policy association, not
a transportable causal derivative. $\tau_2$ is genuinely independent of the
wall clock but measures only decode, so it cannot be dropped into a throughput
equation that includes prefill.

We tested whether the curve transfers to a second workload. On a real agent
trace (117 sessions, median inter-round
waits of 20.2\,s against 0.34\,s on the synthetic trace,
\texttt{toolwait.csv}, six static caps and one controller arm) the
model-consistent elasticity is negative over the first two segments---$-2.40$
then $-0.28$, at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.08 to 1.29---and then turns
positive above 1.29 ($+1.0$, $+0.18$, $+0.87$ over the three segments that
reach 1.50). The $\varepsilon > 1$ regime is touched and not held there: the
first of those three segments is the only one at or above one, and a $\pm10\%$
perturbation of $\tau_1$ carries every one of their bounds across both zero and
one. The main trace, for contrast, crosses one at 1.53 with $+1.15$ and stays
above it. So the direction transfers and the location does not, and on this
trace we can say no more than that added concurrency stopped paying. The
per-request measure is no steadier on
that trace ($+0.41$, $+0.48$, $+0.45$, $-1.25$, $+0.73$). The controller arm
there runs at 1.22, below every one of the nine arms above and below where the
branch turns expensive on either trace, and still does not beat the static
front at the threshold this paper uses---though at a stricter one it does
(\S\ref{sec:xtrace}). We report this as a boundary:
the criterion identifies a branch, and where we could check it elsewhere the
sign of the branch transferred while its location did not; it never explained
the failure. It is not, as an earlier draft claimed, something that decides the
question in minutes before building anything.
