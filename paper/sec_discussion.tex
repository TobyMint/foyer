\section{Load Dependence}
\label{sec:load}

A negative result about adaptivity invites the obvious objection: adaptivity
pays when conditions \emph{change}, and a single stationary trace is the one
case where a static cap should be near-optimal. We agree. What we test here is
the weaker thing---two separate constant-load tiers---and we also ran the
stronger one, a trace whose load moves mid-run, whose baselines co-tenancy made
unusable; \S\ref{sec:discussion} reports both what this section leaves open and
why that experiment has no result to give.

We run the same controller and the same cap family on a 25-session tier. The
throttle configuration is reversed: with all safety throttles disabled, the
25-session tier runs \emph{51\% faster} (58.4 to 28.8 minutes) at a cost of
only 2.4\,pp of SLO, while on the 200-session tier disabling the same
throttles costs 42\% (291.3 to 415.0 minutes) and 43.7\,pp of SLO. One switch,
two regimes, opposite signs, both around 40--50\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loadtier}). No single scalar setting is optimal across both.

We flag the generation gap rather than let the figure imply more than we ran.
The two 200-session numbers are current; the two 25-session numbers are from an
earlier harness revision, and we never ran the all-throttles-off arm on the
25-session tier under the current one. So the reversal is a direction we
measured across two harness generations, not a same-generation paired
comparison, and a reviewer should read it that way. The same-generation
25-session measurement we do have is in Table~\ref{tab:tier25}, and it does not
contain that arm.

This is the regime in which adaptive control should win, and we now have the
same-generation measurement that earlier drafts of this paper were missing.
Four arms on the 25-session tier, all under the current harness
(Table~\ref{tab:tier25}): static caps of two, three and four finish in 32.4,
24.2 and 23.6 minutes at 92.8\%, 91.2\% and 79.2\% SLO, and \sys{} finishes
in 27.6 minutes at 92.8\%.

The finding is a trade, not a defeat, and it is the opposite of what the
200-session tier shows. Against the same-generation envelope---which runs from
\texttt{cap4} at (23.6, 79.2) through \texttt{cap3} at (24.2, 91.2) to
\texttt{cap2} at (32.4, 92.8)---\sys{} sits $+0.9$\,pp above the line. A
static cap of three is still 12\% faster in wall clock, but it gives up
1.6\,pp of SLO to do it, so neither dominates the other. On this tier the
controller is a different point on the front rather than a dominated one,
which is exactly what the 200-session tier denies it.

We are careful about the size, and about the yardstick. $+0.9$\,pp is
measured against a straight-line interpolation between static points, not
against a switching policy anyone ran, so the defensible statement is that
\sys{} is not dominated by any static point we measured on this tier---not
that it beats what a static mix could achieve. On 125 requests one request is
worth 0.8\,pp, so the margin is about one request. The tier's
segment-to-segment SD (5.1 to 21.4\,pp) is not the right yardstick: segment
volatility is not run-to-run uncertainty. The right one is the replicate spread
we have---which, across the five configurations we repeated, is 0.5, 0.8, 1.0,
3.9 and 5.1\,pp (\texttt{replicates.csv}). Against the two tightest of those $+0.9$\,pp is about a replicate
wide; against the three widest---which belong to the configurations that decide
our headline elsewhere---it is inside one. We quote the tightest pair
because it is the least favourable to us, not because it is the whole
distribution. What we will defend is the weaker claim: on the
low-load tier the controller is not worse, and the same switch that is worth
$+51\%$ there costs $-42\%$ at high load. A controller that is competitive
where load is light and dominated where load is heavy is the pattern this
paper set out to explain, and it is the pattern we measured.

The second finding is the more useful one, and what it establishes is negative.
A backlog signal exists---on \texttt{foyer\_hc}, the 200-session baseline, and on
\texttt{foyer\_push\_25}, the same-generation 25-session arm, the admission queue
is non-empty in nearly every control cycle inside the run (97.7\% and 93.2\%).
What we cannot do is read it as load, and the reason is that these two arms are
not a controlled comparison. The 25-session trace carries no arrival times at
all, so its twenty-five sessions are submitted together: its queue opens at 24
of 25 and never returns to that depth, while the 200-session one peaks at 135 of
200 a quarter of the way in. Normalised by each tier the ordering reverses---the
25-session controller withholds 72\% of its sessions, the 200-session one 33\%,
both at the median cycle---and the medians are not stationary either (200-session
first, then 25): 21 against 21 over the first tenth of the run, 89 against 18 in
the middle, 6 against 0 at the end. The number an earlier draft quoted, ``four
times the backlog'', set a mid-run peak on one trace against an opening instant
on another; we no longer quote a ratio between them. (\texttt{backlog.csv}
carries both windows, both denominators, and the phase medians, so all of this
is recomputable.)

What does survive is the direction of the explanation, and we can point at the
measurement rather than infer it from the queue's depth. At the 25-session tier
the controller holds eighteen sessions waiting at the median cycle; over the 739
cycles in which a session waited and none was admitted, the safety valve
accounts for 3.7\% of them and the round gate for 0.0\%, and the controller's
own occupancy \emph{projection} sits at 87.4\% of its budget while the engine
reports 43.6\%---a factor of two, and the same over-estimate \S\ref{sec:results}
measures at 1.79 on the baseline arm. The queue is what the controller's own
accounting produces. (Depth alone would not have shown this: the 200-session
baseline runs at a mean of 1.137 requests against the 25-session tier's 1.136,
so ``deep queue, one request in flight'' describes both.) Reading backlog
correctly therefore requires combining it with progress through the run, which
is a stateful computation a cap cannot perform. That is the most promising
direction we did not finish, and we are explicit about how far we did not finish
it: we never built or tested a backlog-based load estimator, so we do not claim
to have shown that one cannot work---only that raw queue length does not order
these two tiers and that the queue is the controller's own artifact. The
earlier-harness 25-session arms of \S\ref{sec:load} run shallower still (11--13
over the same window), which is why we compare only the same-generation pair.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Admission and scheduling for agentic serving.} Recent systems
report large gains from treating an agent session, rather than a request, as
the schedulable unit, and from preserving session state across tool calls:
session-aware admission with CPU-restorable KV
\cite{talaria,continuum}, program-level pause/resume with shortest-context-first
restoration \cite{thunderagent}, and congestion-based control over an
agent-level window \cite{concur}. A recurring measurement in this line is that
recomputation, not admission, dominates: \cite{concur} attributes 49.1\% of
end-to-end latency to recomputation, and \cite{mori} preserves state on the
CPU at the cost of a reload. Our results are consistent with this literature
rather than in tension with it, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} explains why: the
mechanism these systems add is one our controller already has.

\paragraph{Engine-side techniques.} A separate line improves the engine
rather than the admission decision. Iteration-level batching and paged KV
\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023pagedattention} are the substrate every policy here runs
on; chunked prefill with stall-free batching \cite{sarathi}, prefill/decode
disaggregation \cite{distserve}, and cache-centric scheduling
\cite{mooncake} are the refinements. Our \S\ref{sec:model} measures 2.6--8.2\,ms of each
27--37\,ms step as work that is not decoding---prefill and non-decode
overhead---which is exactly what this line attacks. We expect these techniques to move the frontier rather than to decide who
sits above it, but we did not run the interaction and so state it as an
expectation.

\paragraph{State retention and preemption.} A third line keeps session state
alive across the gaps we measure in \S\ref{sec:model}: keeping KV on cheaper
storage across turns \cite{yu2025pensieve,gao2024cachedattention}, migrating it
between instances \cite{sun2024llumnix}, and preempting at iteration granularity
\cite{wu2026fastserve}. These systems exercise finer control than our 2\,s
admission cycle, which is the boundary of our negative result: we falsify one
control granularity, not the family. What they share with \sys{} is that the
state is not discarded---which is why, as we argue below, adding admission
intelligence on top buys nothing here.

\paragraph{Application-level dependencies and replay.} Serving an LLM
\emph{application} rather than a request means the scheduler sees dependencies
between calls \cite{lin2024parrot}, and agentic traces have structure that
request-level benchmarks do not capture \cite{wang2026xperf}. Our replay
preserves that structure but fixes it in advance, which is exactly the
limitation these works would press on.

\paragraph{Prefix caching.} The prefix cache is often proposed as the signal
an admission policy should use. What we measure is that the \emph{realized}
reuse is a function of the admission policy and of the host tier, while the
\emph{planned} reuse is a constant of the trace; an earlier draft of this
paper conflated the two and got the direction wrong in both. The trace plans
a round-1-and-later hit rate of 80.9\%. Without HiCache the realized
round-1+ rate follows the plan only at the loosest cap and then collapses:
80.8\%, 78.5\%, 70.6\%, 56.0\%, 32.8\% for caps of one through five. With
HiCache it tracks the plan at every cap we ran: 80.7\%, 79.6\%, 78.7\% for
caps of two, three and four. Our controller sits at 80.6\%.
So the honest statement is not that admission does not move this quantity---%
\texttt{cap4} alone moves it 25 points---but that the host tier is what keeps
it from moving, which is the same mechanism \S\ref{sec:model} and
\S\ref{sec:criterion} keep running into. A configuration without a host tier
that admits four sessions does not have the cache that a host-tier one at the
same concurrency does. All eight numbers in this paragraph are the
\emph{observed} per-step hit rate (\texttt{server\_prefix\_hit\_rate} in the
per-step logs), unweighted mean over rounds $\ge 1$; the planned rate is the
trace constant 80.9\% and does not move with the cap. \S\ref{sec:model} uses
a different convention---all steps, token-weighted, so that reuse and
recomputation partition the prompt---under which the same collapse reads
92.5\%$\to$36.4\% of the trace's bound for the device-only caps and
92.4\%$\to$90.2\% for the static host-tier caps. We state both because the paper's earlier
drafts mixed them. We note the boundary of this claim---we varied admission policy,
not eviction policy, and SGLang exposes alternate eviction policies we did not
test. A characterization of production KV-cache traffic at a large cloud
provider finds that eviction-policy design is ``highly workload-dependent''
and that reuse patterns differ sharply by request category
\cite{wang2025kvcachewild}; that is reason to expect a single trace family to
understate the space, though it is not a policy comparison and we do not
present it as one.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why published gains do not reappear.} The systems cited above win
by keeping session state alive across tool waits and by pausing at agent
granularity rather than request granularity. \sys{} has both properties by
construction: the permit file gives per-session pause and resume, and the
controller never frees capacity by discarding a session---it admits fewer
sessions instead. To that extent the mechanism these systems add is one our
controller already has, which is one reason adding admission intelligence on
top of it buys nothing here.

We are careful not to overstate this, because the distinction is between
\emph{choosing} to discard state and \emph{having} it evicted. Retention here
is the engine's radix cache doing its ordinary job; it is not something the
controller implements, and \S\ref{sec:model} measures that the cache evicts
parked prefixes under pressure and that they are recomputed. What \sys{}
avoids is the deliberate trade of state for capacity. A system that manages the
host tier as an explicit policy---as \cite{mori} and \cite{yu2025pensieve}
do---holds a lever we do not, and our negative result does not cover it.

\paragraph{The threshold decides the verdict, and we report that.} Every SLO
number in this paper counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is
under 10\,s. That constant sets the whole vertical axis, so we re-derived the
frontier at 5\,s and 20\,s as well, from the same per-request logs. The
ordering of the static caps does not change. What changes is where the
controllers sit relative to them, and at the loose end one of them appears,
in one of its two runs, to cross:

  \sys{} base                 & $-2.3$ & $-1.2$ & $+0.6$ \\
  \texttt{bres}               & $-3.6$ & $-1.3$ & $+0.3$ \\
  Concur-style AIMD           & $-14.2$ & $-11.2$ & $-6.5$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two readings, and we give both. The first is that our headline claim is
conditional: at the strict threshold that interactive serving actually cares
about, none of the cleanly measured controllers reaches the static front; at a
loose one, one measurement of one of them does. The
second is that the crossing is not a win, and there are now two independent
reasons to say so. The first we gave an earlier draft of this paragraph: the
margin is not resolvable. $+1.5$\,pp at 20\,s is larger than the replicate
SLO spread of the three tightest pairs we have---0.5\,pp for the base
controller, 0.8\,pp for \texttt{cap3}+HiCache and 1.0\,pp for
\texttt{cap2}+HiCache---but smaller than the two widest, 3.9\,pp for the best
arm's own repeat and 5.1\,pp for \texttt{cap4}+HiCache, and those two are
anchors. It is the anchors, not the controllers, that set the line the margin
is measured against, so the comparison that matters is the one against the
widest anchor---where the margin is not resolvable. The second reason is
harder to argue with, and we found it only when the arm's repeat landed:
\emph{the crossing does not reproduce}. \texttt{horizon}=1 reads $+1.5$\,pp at
$20$\,s and its own repeat reads $-2.3$---not merely inside the band, but below
it, on the other side of the line the first run crossed. Two runs of one
configuration, 3.8\,pp apart on this axis ($+1.5$ against $-2.3$, from 94.4\%
and 91.1\% of requests served under 20\,s), and the sign of the verdict flips
with them.
So the loose-threshold crossing is a single measurement, and the one arm whose
repeat we have on the axis where it matters does not support it. (An earlier revision discarded
the \texttt{cap3}+HiCache pair on the ground that one run had exceeded its
cap. That was wrong: the over-cap run is the \emph{device-only}
\texttt{cap3} repeat, and both HiCache runs peak at three with none over. The
pair is a valid replicate and we use it, including as an envelope anchor.)
A margin that appears only at one threshold
and only at a size we cannot resolve is not evidence that adaptive admission
works; it is evidence that the question needs more runs than we have. We state
the conditional version in the abstract, and we flag this as the second thing
we would re-run with more GPU time.

\paragraph{The SLO metric excludes the controller's own cost, and we checked.}
Every SLO number above counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is
under 10\,s, and the runner measures that from the moment the request is
\emph{submitted to the engine}. A session that waits for a permit has not been
submitted yet, so the wait is invisible---and that wait is precisely the cost
an admission controller imposes. We therefore recomputed the runs we have per-step
logs for on a ready-to-first-token basis, where a round's clock starts when the
session is actually able to run (its arrival for round~0, the previous round's
completion plus its tool wait otherwise). That is nineteen of the twenty-five
200-session runs in Table~\ref{tab:runs}; absent are the no-control baseline,
the two HiCache anchor repeats and three controller repeats, and we do not
extend the claim to them.

The correction is large. Every configuration loses 2.9--19.3\,pp of SLO, and the
loss is systematic: tight caps queue the hardest, so \texttt{cap1} falls from
94.8\% to 76.2\% while \texttt{cap5} falls only from 27.1\% to 24.2\%. A
session admitted last under \texttt{cap1} can wait hours for its turn; its
engine-side TTFT is still 1.6\,s.

What does \emph{not} change is the verdict, within the subset we recomputed.
The static envelope keeps the same four anchors---the two anchor
repeats, which we did not recompute on the ready clock, stand in as their first
runs, and every distance below carries that substitution
(\texttt{arrival\_slo.csv} records the columns)---and of the eleven controller
arms, nine move by no more than
2.5\,pp. Two move more---the all-throttles-off arm improves by 8.2\,pp and the
retuned AIMD arm by 3.2\,pp---and both stay below the front either way. One
crosses the $\pm1.0$\,pp band, and it is the one that decides our headline:
\texttt{horizon}=1 goes from $-0.93$ (inside the band, a tie) to $-1.21$ (below
it), because on the ready clock its own SLO falls from 84.8\% to 68.2\% while
the anchor it is measured against falls less. The ready clock therefore moves
it from a tie to a loss, which is the same direction the paragraph above
reaches from the other side, and it is why we do not report a ready-clock
distance for it as a headline: the two anchor repeats that define the line are
not in the set we could recompute (\S\ref{sec:discussion} says
which runs are), and inventing a line for them would be the same error this
subsection is about.

The subset matters and we state its edge. The two configurations whose repeats
decide how we read the verdict---\texttt{horizon}=1's repeat and the fastest
anchor \texttt{cap4}+HC's---are not in the nineteen, because we did not
recompute their per-step logs on the ready clock; the rebuilt envelope
therefore rests on the original anchors without their repeats, and a
ready-clock frontier that included them could move the line by the 3.9 and
5.1\,pp those repeats differ by. We do not claim the verdict is unchanged under
every SLO definition---only that on the runs we could recompute, moving the
clock to the ready point costs every configuration 3--19\,pp, moves no
configuration from below the line to above it, and changes exactly one
controller verdict: \texttt{horizon}=1 leaves the tie band on the below
side. (One static cap crosses the other way, \texttt{cap3}+HC from $-1.72$ to
$-0.79$, which does not help any controller.)
We report this because a reviewer should ask it,
and because the answer is the useful part: the negative result is not an
artifact of choosing an SLO definition that flatters controllers. If anything
the flattering choice was the one that moved the arm nearest the line from
$-0.9$ to $+1.5$---that is, from just below the line to just above the tie
band's upper edge, by a margin of the same order as that pair's own replicate
spread (3.9\,pp).

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results come from one 24\,GB accelerator and one
7B model. The workload axis we did test once more (\S\ref{sec:xtrace}); the
hardware axis we did not, so generality across devices is untested. Only five configurations
have been repeated---four of them twice and the base configuration four
times---so the replicate spread we report in \S\ref{sec:results} is estimated
from one to three degrees of freedom per configuration; a margin as small as the one that decides our verdict would need several
more repeats per configuration to resolve, and we did not run them. Two specific boundaries
matter. First, the pool holds about four median sessions, so concurrency is
capacity-bound by construction; on a device with a much larger pool the
premise may not hold and the conclusion may invert---we regard reproducing on
a larger device as the single most important next experiment. Second, the
load is stationary, single-tenant and single-SLO. The load-dependence result
of \S\ref{sec:load} is measured on two \emph{separate} constant-load tiers, so
it says what to expect when conditions differ, not what happens while they
change. We did run the experiment that would settle that---a trace whose load
moves mid-run, with a static cap on each tier and a switcher that changes its
cap at the trace's designed switch point---and we cannot report a result from
it, in either direction, because its static baselines were measured under
host co-tenancy: \texttt{var\_cap3} spent 158 of its 266 minutes at four or
more concurrent engine instances, and \texttt{var\_cap2} is flagged as a
watch (\S\ref{sec:repro}). A first look at those runs has the switcher
finishing 15 minutes ahead of the best static cap at an SLO inside the tie
band, which would be the first evidence in this paper that adaptivity pays;
we do not report it as a result, because the baseline it beats is precisely
the one the audit flags, and a 60\%-contaminated baseline is inflated in
exactly the direction that manufactures that finding. Clean re-runs of that
family are in flight, and the honest position until they land is that the
variable-load case is measured and unusable, not unmeasured. Multi-tenant and
heterogeneous-SLO settings are where we would look next.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
\label{sec:repro}
The front and the signed distance of each controller are computed by
\texttt{scripts/lab/frontier.py} from the raw per-step logs; Table~\ref{tab:runs}
is rendered by \texttt{scripts/lab/gen\_table.py} from
\texttt{frontier.py}'s full-precision coordinates
(\texttt{paper/data/runs\_precise.csv}), including the four runs that stopped
short of 999 steps; the live-session column comes from the runner's admission
log via \texttt{run\_summary.py}, and \S\ref{sec:criterion} is computed by
\texttt{gen\_elasticity.py} and \texttt{gen\_elasticity\_hc.py}. Every run is
guarded against a shrunken KV pool.

\emph{Co-tenancy on the measurement host.} Our runs share a host with other
tenants and with each other, and late in the campaign we found that this
matters more than the pool guard catches. An audit
(\texttt{gen\_concurrency.py}, data in \texttt{paper/data/concurrency.csv})
reconstructs, for every run, how many other runs were alive for how long. Runs
that overlapped three or more others for at least an hour are flagged;
fourteen of ours are, two of which are failed runs we do not report. The flag is a risk marker, not a measurement of damage,
and we treat it that way: five of the twelve (\texttt{F:t85}, \texttt{F:t90},
\texttt{var\_foyer}, \texttt{cap1} and the published-parameter AIMD row) show
no aggregate inflation at all, because their overlap was short relative to the
run or did not touch their decode interval. Where the damage is legible it is large---the
per-request decode interval $\tau_2$, measured client-side and never touching
the sampler, sits at 30.9--33.3\,ms for the three affected arms still carrying
the flag in
Table~\ref{tab:runs} against 23.7--25.0\,ms across the seven clean runs of the
same controller family. That is an inflation of 24 to 41\% \emph{across arms},
which compares the extremes of two ranges and is therefore wider than any
single correction; the one arm we have measured both ways, contaminated and
clean, moved 31.3 to 24.8\,ms---a 26\% excess in the contaminated reading
over the clean one, or a 21\% reduction the other way
(\texttt{superseded.csv})---and that arm is now inside the clean range. Two
controls pin the cause to co-tenancy rather than to the configuration, and both
are within-run---the same run, the same policy, the same trace, with the only
change being who else was on the machine. One run (\texttt{cap1}) reads a
per-half-hour median $\tau_2$ of 20.3--20.9\,ms for its first five and a half
hours, and 21.3, 27.1 and 21.1 in the three half-hours after two others start.
Two runs move the other way as neighbours leave: \texttt{F:t85} from 29.8\,ms
in the half-hour before to 22.3--22.6 in the four after, \texttt{F:rl99} from
32.4 to 25.2 and 25.1. We chose the half-hour window because it is the widest
one that resolves the transition and still puts at least twenty steps in every
bucket quoted; the finer series is noisier and does not support a
minute-resolution claim, so we do not make one.
(\texttt{tau2\_windows.csv} carries the series.) Fourteen of our runs fall in the flagged class; three of them are rows of
Table~\ref{tab:runs} (\texttt{cap1}, \texttt{F:breshz1}, and
the published-parameter AIMD row) and
eleven are arms we do not report here. The flag is a window, and only one of
those three is actually damaged---the controller variant whose $\tau_2$
is 30.9--33.3\,ms. \texttt{cap1} and the AIMD row show no aggregate
inflation (20.6 and 24.4\,ms), so we keep their numbers and flag the window.
Two further rows carried the flag and the damage, and have been re-measured
alone under the same one-run-per-GPU discipline: \texttt{bres}, whose clean
$\tau_2$ is 24.8\,ms, and \texttt{horizon}=0 at 25.5\,ms. Their rows in
Table~\ref{tab:runs} now carry the clean numbers, and the conclusions that
rested on them---\texttt{bres}'s step-cost attribution and the route-1
comparison of \S\ref{sec:results}, and \texttt{horizon}=0's move out of the
$\tau$-high column into \texttt{unused mem.} in
\S\ref{sec:results:attribution}---are restated in those sections rather than
left provisional. The remaining two damaged rows are still being re-measured,
and until those runs land we treat their wall-clock, SLO and step-cost
columns as unusable: the signed distances in Table~\ref{tab:runs} for those
two rows and the $\tau$-high attributions of
\S\ref{sec:results:attribution} where it names them. We flag them rather than
quietly dropping the rows so that a reader can see which conclusions are still
standing on which measurements.

Two things we can already say. First, the failure is silent: the engine-side
counters---running count, pool occupancy, evictions, recomputation---are
indistinguishable between the affected runs and the clean ones, so nothing in
the ordinary telemetry would have caught this. Second, the affected runs'
\emph{volume} columns look untouched: the three damaged host-tier arms reuse
0.6791--0.6959 of the prompt against 0.5497--0.6981 across the sixteen
undamaged host-tier arms, so the damaged runs sit inside the clean range
rather than apart from it---suggestive, but not proof, since co-tenancy could
in principle also reorder completions and change eviction order.

The one arm we have both ways settles the second point directly, and we keep
the two runs side by side in \texttt{superseded.csv} because the superseded
one is by construction absent from the tables the rest of this paper reads.
\texttt{bres}
measured under co-tenancy reuses 0.6943 of its prompt; the same policy measured
alone reuses 0.6904, a 0.39-point difference, while its per-request decode
interval moves 31.3 to 24.8\,ms and its wall clock by 10\%. The volume columns
therefore carry no signature of the contamination that we can detect---one arm
is one comparison, and we are not claiming more than that---which is why the
audit had to run on the client-side clock. For the two arms still
outstanding, the re-runs will confirm it policy by policy, and we compare
reuse per round and per session between the pairs rather than only the totals.

One caveat about the controller artifact, which a reader with our code would
otherwise hit. There is no single controller copy behind this paper: the runs
record four different hashes of \texttt{controller\_budget3.py}, so the artifact
evolved during the campaign and a reader cannot reproduce every number from one
revision. Two of the four are the campaign's workhorses---the artifact was
revised mid-campaign and both revisions account for a large share of the runs,
so we do not quote those two counts, which grow as runs land. The other two are
counts we can state without qualification, because nothing has
landed under either since: \texttt{d692ab11f396} on 4 runs and
\texttt{e1dd5ae78019} on 3. (These are snapshot counts too---a future run could
in principle be launched from either copy---but none is planned, whereas the two
workhorse copies are what the re-runs in this revision are using.) None of the four matches the repository copy
(\texttt{a21bd2b63774}); the one divergence we measured and wrote down while
the copies still existed was 23 lines in \texttt{controller\_budget3.py},
a charge-mode flag added on the machine and not in the repository. Two other
files diverged by more (\texttt{controller\_foyer.py} by 108 lines,
\texttt{run\_matrix.py} by 111), so the four copies are not the repository
under another hash. What we can no longer do is say precisely how each of the
four differed: they were read while they existed and only the repository
revision survives on the machine, so that comparison cannot be re-derived from
what we still have.

One coincidence in those two small counts deserves stating rather than leaving
for a reader to find, and it is worse than a coincidence. The arms the
contention audit flags are not a scattered subset: they are \emph{all four} runs
recorded under \texttt{d692ab11f396}, a whole code copy---and that copy has no
other run. So there is no run under it that escaped the window, and the two
explanations, the revision and the co-tenancy, cannot be separated by any
comparison in our data. The other small copy does not break the tie.
\texttt{e1dd5ae78019} was used to launch three arms of which one completed, and
that one, \texttt{horizon}=1, spent only 14 minutes at four lanes against
282--296 for the four flagged runs: below the one-hour threshold the audit itself
uses, so it was never exposed to the condition under test. Its being clean is
what the flag's own definition predicts, whichever copy it ran. We therefore
state the confound rather than a resolution. The re-runs changed the contention
window and the controller revision together, and only the repository revision
survives on the machine, so the campaign copies cannot be diffed after the fact.
The numbers in this paper therefore describe the pre-fix path, with one
partial exception we want to state precisely rather than let a reader assume
the stronger thing. We did run the same configuration with \texttt{sf=0}
(\S\ref{sec:results:idle-memory}), and on the running copy that changes two
things at once: it scans the queue \emph{and} drops the one-per-cycle limit,
because the \texttt{break} that enforces single-flight is inside the same
conditional. That last sentence is a reading of the copy the run actually used,
\texttt{f825bc768188}, taken while it was still on the machine; like the other
three campaign copies it is not in the repository, so the reading cannot be
re-checked from what we still have, and a reader should weigh it accordingly.
The repository's fix keeps the limit and only adds the scan. So our
run is not a test of the fix as written---it is a test of the fix plus a looser
admission rate, and it moves the arm along the front rather than past it. The
narrower experiment, scan-with-the-limit-kept, is the one we would do first with
more GPU time. We report six claims we retracted during
the work, because the retractions
were the most informative events. In order of how much they cost us:

\emph{The denominator of the step cost.} Our first \S\ref{sec:model} divided by
the whole-run mean running count while multiplying by busy time. The two are
not the same quantity---the whole-run mean includes the idle samples---so
$\tau$ came out low by exactly the busy fraction, 36\% for \texttt{cap1}, and
two independent errors cancelled to make our two measurement methods appear to
agree. This is the one that changed a headline number.

\emph{A mechanism refutation that was itself refuted}: we withdrew the
eviction-to-recomputation account on the strength of two checks that were both
identities rather than measurements (\S\ref{sec:model}).

\emph{A median-based reading of the tool wait} that inverted a central
assumption. \emph{A monotone relation between idle fraction and concurrency}
that the full data does not show. \emph{A growth-sensitivity conclusion} that
rested on an arm we had already invalidated. And \emph{the accounting
double-charge} of \S\ref{sec:results}, falsified twice---once by source
inspection and once by the end-to-end measurement here.

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether an adaptive admission controller can beat a tuned static
concurrency cap on a memory-bound agentic LLM workload. On our system we could
not build one that does: nine variants, four routes tested, and of the eight
whose measurements are clean, none above the interpolated static front---which
is the strictest of the two baselines we could have used (\S\ref{sec:bg}),
since it sits above the discrete static points. (Four were measured while
other jobs shared the host; we have re-measured three of them, which moved
6.5, 6.4 and 4.5\,pp closer without crossing, and the last one is still
out.) We expected the reason to be a cancellation---admission policy controls
how many requests are in flight, the per-step cost rises with that number, and
the product is what the wall clock sees---and we built a two-part decomposition
of the wall clock that makes the cancellation measurable. That decomposition
holds, and the cost it measures is real, but it is not the whole reason and on
the configuration our controllers run it is not the binding one: there the
elasticity stays below one across the operating range, so added concurrency was
still cheap---they did not lose where concurrency was expensive, though that is
not the same as showing they could have won elsewhere. What we
can say at the end is narrower than what we set out to say: on this hardware and
this workload, the admission rules we could build and tune did not beat a static
cap, the capacity estimate they act on is 1.79$\times$ the occupancy the engine
reports, and a
structural idle component that no policy we built recovers at a profit remains.
The regime in which adaptivity should have paid---where the same switch is worth
$+51\%$ on the light tier and $-42\%$ on the heavy one, a comparison that spans
two harness generations (\S\ref{sec:load})---is one where the controller is not
dominated by any static
point we measured, and does not dominate one either. The measurement we offer is
a diagnostic an engineer can take before committing to a sweep; whether it
predicts the outcome on another workload is a question we tested once and could
not confirm.

The part we would carry elsewhere is not the negative result but the
mechanism behind it. Prefix reuse is bounded by the workload---75.5\% of our
prompt tokens---and whether that bound survives added concurrency is set by
the memory tier: device-only, realization falls from 92.5\% to 36.4\% as the
running count rises, and the shortfall reappears as recomputation; with a host
tier the static caps hold at 90.2--92.4\% across the range our controllers
occupy, and the worst of the nineteen host-tier runs that completed this
trace---the one that disables every throttle---still realizes 72.8\%, where the
nearest device-only point, the cap
of four at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.65, manages 60.7\%---while
the device cache misses \emph{more}. Two configurations at the same running
count and the same pool occupancy realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound,
which is why we describe the tier rather than the occupancy as the variable.
That claim is measured on one trace family, and the matched pair behind it ran
on two cards a day apart; the controlled version---one card, back to
back---is queued rather than done, and we do not extend the claim past what we
measured---but
it is the finding a system designer can act on, and it is the one we would
test first on different hardware. Two boundaries come with it. On a second
workload both of our measurements survive in direction and neither in level:
that trace realizes less of its own bound at a lower occupancy, and its
elasticity turns positive at a lower running count---reaching 1.0 there where
the main trace reaches 1.15 at 1.53---so neither an occupancy threshold nor a
concurrency threshold transfers. And the variable-load case, which is where a controller should
have its best chance, is measured but not usable---its baselines were taken
under host co-tenancy and we report it as open rather than as a result.
