\section{Load Dependence}
\label{sec:load}

A negative result about adaptivity invites the obvious objection: adaptivity
pays when conditions \emph{change}, and a single stationary trace is the one
case where a static cap should be near-optimal. We agree. What we test here is a weaker thing---two
separate constant-load tiers---and \S\ref{sec:discussion} says what that leaves
open.

We run the same controller and the same cap family on a 25-session tier. The
throttle configuration is reversed: with all safety throttles disabled, the
25-session tier runs \emph{51\% faster} (58.4 to 28.8 minutes) at a cost of
only 2.4\,pp of SLO, while on the 200-session tier disabling the same
throttles costs 42\% (291.3 to 415.0 minutes) and 43.7\,pp of SLO. One switch,
two regimes, opposite signs, both around 40--50\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loadtier}). No single scalar setting is optimal across both.

We flag the generation gap rather than let the figure imply more than we ran.
The two 200-session numbers are current; the two 25-session numbers are from an
earlier harness revision, and we never ran the all-throttles-off arm on the
25-session tier under the current one. So the reversal is a direction we
measured across two harness generations, not a same-generation paired
comparison, and a reviewer should read it that way. The same-generation
25-session measurement we do have is in Table~\ref{tab:tier25}, and it does not
contain that arm.

This is the regime in which adaptive control should win, and we now have the
same-generation measurement that earlier drafts of this paper were missing.
Four arms on the 25-session tier, all under the current harness
(Table~\ref{tab:tier25}): static caps of two, three and four finish in 32.4,
24.2 and 23.6 minutes at 92.8\%, 91.2\% and 79.2\% SLO, and \sys{} finishes
in 27.6 minutes at 92.8\%.

The finding is a trade, not a defeat, and it is the opposite of what the
200-session tier shows. Against the same-generation envelope---which runs from
\texttt{cap4} at (23.6, 79.2) through \texttt{cap3} at (24.2, 91.2) to
\texttt{cap2} at (32.4, 92.8)---\sys{} sits $+0.9$\,pp above the line. A
static cap of three is still 12\% faster in wall clock, but it gives up
1.6\,pp of SLO to do it, so neither dominates the other. On this tier the
controller is a different point on the front rather than a dominated one,
which is exactly what the 200-session tier denies it.

We are careful about the size, and about the yardstick. $+0.9$\,pp is
measured against a straight-line interpolation between static points, not
against a switching policy anyone ran, so the defensible statement is that
\sys{} is not dominated by any static point we measured on this tier---not
that it beats what a static mix could achieve. On 125 requests one request is
worth 0.8\,pp, so the margin is about one request. The tier's
segment-to-segment SD (5.1 to 21.4\,pp) is not the right yardstick: segment
volatility is not run-to-run uncertainty. The right one is the replicate spread
we have, 1.0 and 0.5\,pp of SLO on the two configurations we repeated, and
against that $+0.9$\,pp is about a replicate wide. What we will defend is the weaker claim: on the
low-load tier the controller is not worse, and the same switch that is worth
$+51\%$ there costs $-42\%$ at high load. A controller that is competitive
where load is light and dominated where load is heavy is the pattern this
paper set out to explain, and it is the pattern we measured.

The second finding is the more useful one. The signal an adaptive controller
would need is available---the number of sessions waiting for admission differs
by 6--7$\times$ between the tiers (median 9--11 at 25 sessions against 66 at
200, maximum 135)---but its \emph{interpretation} is not obvious. At the
25-session tier the controller holds eleven sessions back while running
exactly one; that backlog is manufactured by the throttling itself rather than
evidence that demand has slackened. Reading it correctly requires combining
instantaneous backlog with progress through the run, which is a stateful
computation a cap cannot perform. We regard this as the most promising
direction we did not finish, and we are explicit that we did not finish it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Admission and scheduling for agentic serving.} Recent systems
report large gains from treating an agent session, rather than a request, as
the schedulable unit, and from preserving session state across tool calls:
session-aware admission with CPU-restorable KV
\cite{talaria,continuum}, program-level pause/resume with shortest-context-first
restoration \cite{thunderagent}, and congestion-based control over an
agent-level window \cite{concur}. A recurring measurement in this line is that
recomputation, not admission, dominates: \cite{concur} attributes 49.1\% of
end-to-end latency to recomputation, and \cite{mori} preserves state on the
CPU at the cost of a reload. Our results are consistent with this literature
rather than in tension with it, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} explains why: the
mechanism these systems add is one our controller already has.

\paragraph{Engine-side techniques.} A separate line improves the engine
rather than the admission decision. Iteration-level batching and paged KV
\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023pagedattention} are the substrate every policy here runs
on; chunked prefill with stall-free batching \cite{sarathi}, prefill/decode
disaggregation \cite{distserve}, and cache-centric scheduling
\cite{mooncake} are the refinements. Our \S\ref{sec:model} measures 2.6--8.2\,ms of each
27--37\,ms step as work that is not decoding---prefill and non-decode
overhead---which is exactly what this line attacks. We expect these techniques to move the frontier rather than to decide who
sits above it, but we did not run the interaction and so state it as an
expectation.

\paragraph{State retention and preemption.} A third line keeps session state
alive across the gaps we measure in \S\ref{sec:model}: keeping KV on cheaper
storage across turns \cite{yu2025pensieve,gao2024cachedattention}, migrating it
between instances \cite{sun2024llumnix}, and preempting at iteration granularity
\cite{wu2026fastserve}. These systems exercise finer control than our 2\,s
admission cycle, which is the boundary of our negative result: we falsify one
control granularity, not the family. What they share with \sys{} is that the
state is not discarded---which is why, as we argue below, adding admission
intelligence on top buys nothing here.

\paragraph{Application-level dependencies and replay.} Serving an LLM
\emph{application} rather than a request means the scheduler sees dependencies
between calls \cite{lin2024parrot}, and agentic traces have structure that
request-level benchmarks do not capture \cite{wang2026xperf}. Our replay
preserves that structure but fixes it in advance, which is exactly the
limitation these works would press on.

\paragraph{Prefix caching.} The prefix cache is often proposed as the signal
an admission policy should use. In our workload it is not a policy variable:
round-1+ hit rate tracks the trace's own planned value to within a few points
across four different admission policies---80.6\% measured against 80.9\%
planned on the \sys{} baseline, and 78.5\% against the same 80.9\% for the
worst static cap---so admission moves it hardly at all. (Both numbers are the
unweighted mean of the per-step planned hit rate over rounds 1 and later; the
token-weighted figure for the same steps is 86.2\%, and the all-steps figure
is 73.8\%. We use the unweighted round-1+ convention throughout, and flag it
because the paper's earlier drafts mixed conventions.) We note the boundary of this claim---we varied admission policy,
not eviction policy, and SGLang exposes alternate eviction policies we did not
test. A characterization of production KV-cache traffic at a large cloud
provider finds that eviction-policy design is ``highly workload-dependent''
and that reuse patterns differ sharply by request category
\cite{wang2025kvcachewild}; that is reason to expect a single trace family to
understate the space, though it is not a policy comparison and we do not
present it as one.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why published gains do not reappear.} The systems cited above win
by keeping session state alive across tool waits and by pausing at agent
granularity rather than request granularity. \sys{} has both properties by
construction: the permit file gives per-session pause and resume, and the
controller never frees capacity by discarding a session---it admits fewer
sessions instead. To that extent the mechanism these systems add is one our
controller already has, which is one reason adding admission intelligence on
top of it buys nothing here.

We are careful not to overstate this, because the distinction is between
\emph{choosing} to discard state and \emph{having} it evicted. Retention here
is the engine's radix cache doing its ordinary job; it is not something the
controller implements, and \S\ref{sec:model} measures that the cache evicts
parked prefixes under pressure and that they are recomputed. What \sys{}
avoids is the deliberate trade of state for capacity. A system that manages the
host tier as an explicit policy---as \cite{mori} and \cite{yu2025pensieve}
do---holds a lever we do not, and our negative result does not cover it.

\paragraph{The threshold decides the verdict, and we report that.} Every SLO
number in this paper counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is
under 10\,s. That constant sets the whole vertical axis, so we re-derived the
frontier at 5\,s and 20\,s as well, from the same per-request logs. The
ordering of the static caps does not change. What changes is where the
controllers sit relative to them, and at the loose end one of them crosses:

Two readings, and we give both. The first is that our headline claim is
conditional: at the strict threshold that interactive serving actually cares
about, no controller reaches the static front; at a loose one, one does. The
second is that the crossing is still not a win, though the reason is more
awkward than we first wrote. $+2.7$\,pp at 20\,s is larger than the replicate
SLO spread of two of the three controller-side configurations we repeated
(1.0 and 0.5\,pp) but smaller than the best arm's own (3.9\,pp) and smaller
than an envelope anchor's (5.1\,pp)---and it is the
anchors, not the controllers, that set the line the margin is measured
against. A third pair
exists---two runs of \texttt{cap3}+HiCache---but one of them exceeded its own
cap, so we do not use it as a replicate. A margin that appears only at one threshold
and only at a size we cannot resolve is not evidence that adaptive admission
works; it is evidence that the question needs more runs than we have. We state
the conditional version in the abstract, and we flag this as the second thing
we would re-run with more GPU time.

\paragraph{The SLO metric excludes the controller's own cost, and we checked.}
Every SLO number above counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is
under 10\,s, and the runner measures that from the moment the request is
\emph{submitted to the engine}. A session that waits for a permit has not been
submitted yet, so the wait is invisible---and that wait is precisely the cost
an admission controller imposes. We therefore recomputed the runs we have per-step
logs for on a ready-to-first-token basis, where a round's clock starts when the
session is actually able to run (its arrival for round~0, the previous round's
completion plus its tool wait otherwise). That is nineteen of the twenty-two
runs in Table~\ref{tab:runs}; the no-control baseline and the two runs whose
per-step logs we did not recompute are absent, and we do not extend the claim
to them.

The correction is large. Every configuration loses 3--19\,pp of SLO, and the
loss is systematic: tight caps queue the hardest, so \texttt{cap1} falls from
94.8\% to 76.2\% while \texttt{cap5} falls only from 27.1\% to 24.2\%. A
session admitted last under \texttt{cap1} can wait hours for its turn; its
engine-side TTFT is still 1.6\,s.

What does \emph{not} change is the verdict, within the subset we recomputed.
The static envelope keeps the same four anchors, and of the eleven controller
arms, nine move by no more than
2.7\,pp and none crosses the $\pm1.0$\,pp band into a new verdict. Two move
more: the all-throttles-off arm improves by 8.3\,pp and the retuned AIMD arm by
2.8\,pp, both of which stay below the front either way. The one that decides our headline moves the wrong way for
the controller---\texttt{horizon}=1 goes from $+0.22$ to $-0.68$\,pp, still a
tie but now on the below side.

The subset matters and we state its edge. The two configurations whose repeats
decide how we read the verdict---\texttt{horizon}=1's repeat and the fastest
anchor \texttt{cap4}+HC's---are not in the nineteen, because we did not
recompute their per-step logs on the ready clock; the rebuilt envelope
therefore rests on the original anchors without their repeats, and a
ready-clock frontier that included them could move the line by the 3.9 and
5.1\,pp those repeats differ by. We do not claim the verdict is unchanged under
every SLO definition---only that on the runs we could recompute, moving the
clock to the ready point costs every configuration 3--19\,pp and changes no
verdict in that set.
We report this because a reviewer should ask it,
and because the answer is the useful part: the negative result is not an
artifact of choosing an SLO definition that flatters controllers. If anything
the flattering choice was the one that let our best arm sit above the line.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results come from one 24\,GB accelerator, one 7B
model and one trace family; generality is untested. Only four configurations
have been repeated---twice, twice, twice and three times---so the replicate
spread we report in \S\ref{sec:results} is estimated from one to two degrees of
freedom per configuration; a margin as small as the one that decides our verdict would need several
more repeats per configuration to resolve, and we did not run them. Two specific boundaries
matter. First, the pool holds about four median sessions, so concurrency is
capacity-bound by construction; on a device with a much larger pool the
premise may not hold and the conclusion may invert---we regard reproducing on
a larger device as the single most important next experiment. Second, the
load is stationary, single-tenant and single-SLO. The load-dependence result
of \S\ref{sec:load} is measured on two \emph{separate} constant-load tiers, so
it says what to expect when conditions differ, not what happens while they
change; a trace whose load moves during the run is the experiment that would
settle it, and we did not run one. Multi-tenant and heterogeneous-SLO settings
are where we would look next.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
\label{sec:repro}
The front and the signed distance of each controller are computed by
\texttt{scripts/lab/frontier.py} from the raw per-step logs; the run tables and
the four runs shorter than 999 steps are computed by
\texttt{scripts/lab/gen\_table.py} and \texttt{scripts/lab/run\_summary.py}
respectively, and \S\ref{sec:criterion} is computed by
\texttt{gen\_elasticity.py} and \texttt{gen\_elasticity\_hc.py}. Every run is
guarded against a shrunken KV pool. We report six claims we retracted during
the work, because the retractions
were the most informative events. In order of how much they cost us:

\emph{The denominator of the step cost.} Our first \S\ref{sec:model} divided by
the whole-run mean running count while multiplying by busy time. The two are
not the same quantity---the whole-run mean includes the idle samples---so
$\tau$ came out low by exactly the busy fraction, 36\% for \texttt{cap1}, and
two independent errors cancelled to make our two measurement methods appear to
agree. This is the one that changed a headline number.

\emph{A mechanism refutation that was itself refuted}: we withdrew the
eviction-to-recomputation account on the strength of two checks that were both
identities rather than measurements (\S\ref{sec:model}).

\emph{A median-based reading of the tool wait} that inverted a central
assumption. \emph{A monotone relation between idle fraction and concurrency}
that the full data does not show. \emph{A growth-sensitivity conclusion} that
rested on an arm we had already invalidated. And \emph{the accounting
double-charge} of \S\ref{sec:results}, falsified twice---once by source
inspection and once by the end-to-end measurement here.

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether an adaptive admission controller can beat a tuned static
concurrency cap on a memory-bound agentic LLM workload, and found that on our
system it cannot: nine variants, four routes tested, none above the static
front. We expected the reason to be a cancellation---admission policy controls
how many requests are in flight, the per-step cost rises with that number, and
the product is what the wall clock sees---and we built a two-part decomposition
of the wall clock that makes the cancellation measurable. That decomposition
holds, and the cost it measures is real, but it is not the whole reason and on
the configuration our controllers run it is not the binding one: there the
elasticity stays below one across the operating range, so added concurrency was
still cheap and the engine had room. Our controllers did not take it. What we
can say at the end is narrower than what we set out to say: on this hardware and
this workload, the admission rules we could build and tune did not beat a static
cap, the capacity estimate they act on is 1.79$\times$ the truth, and a
structural idle component that no policy we built recovers at a profit remains.
The regime in which adaptivity should have paid---where the same switch is worth
$+51\%$ and $-42\%$---is one where the controller is not dominated by any static
point we measured, and does not dominate one either. The measurement we offer is
a diagnostic an engineer can take before committing to a sweep; whether it
predicts the outcome on another workload is a question we tested once and could
not confirm.
