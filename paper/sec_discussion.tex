\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Related work.} Recent systems report large gains from treating an agent session rather than a request as the schedulable unit and preserving session state across tool calls
\cite{talaria,continuum,thunderagent,concur,mori}; one measures what losing that state costs---recomputation at 49.1\% of end-to-end latency \cite{concur}---and we find the same pressure in our runs, whose mechanism our controller already has (\S\ref{sec:discussion}). A second line improves
the engine rather than the admission decision
\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023pagedattention,sarathi,distserve,mooncake,flashattention,splitwise}: our
own measurements bound, without identifying, 3.3--8.8\,ms of each
20.6--39.2\,ms step---the overhead that line
attacks; we expect it to move the frontier rather than decide who sits above it,
but we did not run the interaction. A third keeps session state alive across the
gaps we measure
\cite{yu2025pensieve,gao2024cachedattention,sun2024llumnix,wu2026fastserve} at
finer control than our 2\,s admission cycle, which bounds our negative result: we
falsify one control granularity, not the family. Serving an \emph{application}
also exposes dependencies between calls \cite{lin2024parrot}
and trace structure that request-level benchmarks miss \cite{wang2026xperf},
a structure our replay preserves but fixes in advance \cite{genprofiling}---the
limitation these works would press on.

\paragraph{Where the pressure comes from.} A parallel literature regulates the
resource our mechanism turns on rather than the admissions that consume it:
bandwidth reservation \cite{memguard,yun2016,mempol}, its dynamic and per-bank
variants \cite{dynamo,perbank24,etm2,perbank26}; on accelerators, memory extension
across tiers \cite{rtswap,zerowsap} and interference-aware scheduling
\cite{xiang2019,tpdnndpu,aiheat}. None is a control we deploy.

\emph{Realized} prefix reuse is a function of the admission policy and of the
host tier, \emph{planned} reuse a constant of the trace (80.9\%); conflating the
two got the direction wrong. The honest statement is not that
admission cannot move this quantity---\texttt{cap4} alone moves it 25 points---but
that the host tier keeps it from moving while the cap stays inside what the
pool can hold: we varied admission policy, and eviction policy too---one \texttt{slru}
run reads 80.5\% against 80.6\% for two runs of SGLang's \cite{zheng2024sglang}
default and 80.87\% planned, though it differs from both in card and launcher and
from one in controller revision, so it cannot isolate the eviction policy's
effect. A characterization of
production KV-cache traffic finds eviction-policy design ``highly
workload-dependent'' \cite{wang2025kvcachewild}---reason to expect one trace
family to understate the space, though it is not a policy comparison and we do
not present it as one.

\paragraph{Why published gains do not reappear.} The systems cited above win by keeping session state alive across tool waits and pausing at agent granularity rather than request granularity; \sys{} has both by construction (\S\ref{sec:bg}), so admission intelligence on top adds nothing we can resolve here; the arm above the front is a one-expression change to the \emph{release} rule, above the line on two of its three runs
(\S\ref{sec:results}). The distinction we must not blur is between \emph{choosing} to discard state and \emph{having} it evicted: retention here is the engine's radix cache doing its ordinary job, not something the controller implements, and \S\ref{sec:model} measures that the cache evicts parked prefixes
under pressure and that they are recomputed. A system managing the host tier as an explicit policy---as \cite{mori}, \cite{flexgen} and \cite{yu2025pensieve} do---holds a lever we do not, and our negative result does not cover it.

\paragraph{The threshold decides the verdict, and we report that.} Every SLO number
here counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is under 10\,s---the whole
vertical axis---so we re-derived the frontier at 5\,s and 20\,s from the same logs. The ordering of the static caps does not change, but where the
controllers sit does: at the loose end six cross, \texttt{horizon}$=1$ by $+1.5$,
\texttt{horizon}$=2$ by $+1.0$, the queue-scanning arm by $+1.4$, \emph{its repeat
by} $+2.4$ \emph{and its third run by} $+1.2$, and \texttt{single-flight off}'s
second run by $+2.0$. Our
headline claim is conditional, and this is where the condition bites, in the
direction that costs us most. At the strict threshold every other number here is
judged at, the queue-scanning arm of \S\ref{sec:results} is above the line on
two of its three runs, by $+1.30$ and $+2.16$\,pp, the third inside it at
$+0.29$\,pp, against the $0.5$ to $4.6$\,pp that separates repeats of a single
controller configuration; it is the only arm above the line at all three
thresholds and the only one whose repeat confirms it---its third run does not. Three of the six that cross
at the loose end are contradicted by their own repeats. What this supports is neither a verdict on
admission control nor a single-run anomaly: a one-expression change
to the \emph{release} rule is above the static envelope on two of its three runs, while
every arm that changes the \emph{admission} decision is not.

\paragraph{The SLO metric excludes the controller's own cost, and we checked.}
The runner measures time-to-first-token from the moment the request is
\emph{submitted to the engine}, so a permit wait is invisible---and that wait is
precisely the cost an admission controller imposes. We recomputed all
thirty-seven 200-session runs we measured on this trace on a
ready-to-first-token basis, including the two anchor repeats that define the line. The correction is
large---every run that gates admission loses 0.4--19.5\,pp of SLO, the tightest
caps queueing the hardest---but the direction of the verdict does \emph{not}
change: the seven that cross the $\pm1.0$\,pp band all move toward the negative,
and on this clock \emph{no} run is above the line, the arm that clears
it on the engine clock falling outside it on one of its three runs. We do not claim the verdict
is unchanged under every SLO definition---only that moving the clock moves none
from below the line to above it. We report the engine clock as primary because every other number in
this paper uses it; the ready clock is the stricter of the two.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results come from one 7B model: we did test the
workload axis once more (\S\ref{sec:xtrace}) and the hardware axis on a second
accelerator (\S\ref{sec:a100})---a static sweep plus one controller run, where the
controller ties rather than crosses, and one row there we could not get clean---so
the negative result \emph{across the nine \sys{} variants} is still measured on
one card. Only nine configurations have been repeated, so the replicate spread in
\S\ref{sec:results} rests on one to three degrees of freedom per configuration,
and a margin as small as ours would need several more repeats; we did not run
them. The front every verdict is measured against is a piecewise interpolation
between measured static points, not a measured policy (\S\ref{sec:bg})---it is
the stricter of the
two comparisons, but \emph{stricter} is not the same as \emph{valid}: we have
since run that policy once, landing $0.89$\,pp \emph{below} the line at its own
wall clock inside the tie band (\S\ref{sec:bg}, \texttt{slice23.csv}), and one
point is not the whole segment, so we cannot exclude that it overstates what a
static mix could deliver elsewhere on it; if it does, ``no controller is above the
line'' would not by itself establish that there is no room. That is the single
assumption this paper's verdict rests on, and it is an assumption. The pool
holds about four median sessions, so concurrency is capacity-bound by
construction, and on a device with a much larger pool the premise may not hold and
the conclusion may invert. Both completed attempts at that baseline were taken
under co-tenancy, so the one comparison we have there is indicative only, and the
controller's edge over the no-control arm is the presence of \emph{a} cap, not the
quality of the choice. The load is stationary, single-tenant and single-SLO, so we
cannot say what happens while they change, and the experiment that would settle
that gives us no result---\emph{the switcher never fired}, so what the runs meant to test it
measure is the run-to-run spread of one configuration and not evidence that
adaptivity pays, and the baselines are unusable as controls. Multi-tenant and
heterogeneous-SLO settings are where we would look next.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Front and signed distances are recomputed from the raw per-step logs over a
full-precision run table; \S\ref{sec:criterion} from its own elasticity
generator. Every number has a row in one of our data files, and five quantities
this paper turns on are re-derived by five checks sharing no code with the
generators, each reproducing the table it checks. The per-run readings, the full run
table, the audit forensics and the controller revision history are collected in
one supplementary document; \emph{no verdict in this paper depends on it.}

\emph{Co-tenancy on the measurement host.} An audit flags every run that
overlapped three or more others for at least an hour: fourteen are, and the
incident that created those windows killed four runs outright, none of which
appears in our tables. The flag is a risk marker, not a measurement of damage: the
engine-side counters are indistinguishable between affected and clean runs, which
is why the audit runs on the client-side decode interval $\tau_2$
\cite{yun2016}---where the damage is legible it is large, with $\tau_2$ at 33.3\,ms on the worst-affected arm
when flagged against 24.8\,ms on re-measurement, and 23.8--27.5\,ms across seventeen
clean runs of the same controller family. Four of the fourteen were damaged, have
all been re-measured alone, and moved 4.5--11.2\,pp without crossing the line they
were measured against; their rows carry the clean numbers and no damaged row
remains, and we keep the flag on the one undamaged row rather than quietly
dropping it.

\emph{The controller artifact.} The runs record four different hashes of our
controller script, none of them the repository copy, so no single
revision reproduces every number; \emph{all four} runs recorded under one of those
copies are flagged by the audit, so the revision and the co-tenancy
cannot be separated by any comparison in our data. We state the confound rather
than a resolution: the recorded parameters and build do match the repository, but
the copy most of our arms ran under diverges in two places, so
\S\ref{sec:results:idle-memory} tests the fix plus a looser admission rate, not
the fix as written, and the launcher hashes are ones we can neither diff nor
certify.

\emph{Seven claims were retracted during this work}, and we list them because the
retractions were the most informative events: an audit whose valve predicate was
inverted, so the ``windows'' we first reported were the valve doing its job; the
denominator of the step cost, which divided by the whole-run mean running count
while multiplying by busy time, making $\tau$ come out low by exactly the idle
fraction (36\% for \texttt{cap1}); a median-based reading of the tool wait that
inverted a central assumption; an oracle-monotonicity claim that rested on an
arm later shown invalid; a double-charging argument; a monotone idle-rate claim;
and a refutation that was itself refuted. Each is reported with its evidence in
\texttt{claims\_audit.md}.

\section{Conclusion}

We asked whether an adaptive admission controller can beat a tuned static
concurrency cap on a memory-bound agentic LLM workload. On our system none of the
nine variants we built beats it beyond the tie band, against the interpolated
static front---the strictest of the two baselines we could have used
(\S\ref{sec:bg}), since it sits above the discrete static points. One arm's
readings are above the band on two of its three runs, and we say which: the same
controller with one expression changed, by $1.3$ and
$2.2$\,pp---a change to the \emph{release} rule rather than to the admission
decision. We expected the reason to be a cancellation---admission policy sets how
many requests are in flight, the per-step cost rises with that number, and the
product is the wall clock---and our two-part decomposition makes it measurable: it
holds, but on our configuration it is not the binding one, the elasticity staying
below one across the operating range. They
did not lose where concurrency was expensive, though that is not the same as
showing they could have won elsewhere. What we can say is narrower than what we
set out to say: on this hardware and workload the admission rules we could build
and tune did not beat a static cap, the capacity estimate they act on is
1.79$\times$ the occupancy the engine reports, and a structural idle component
that no policy we built recovers at a profit remains.

The part we would carry elsewhere is not the negative result but the mechanism
behind it: prefix reuse is bounded by the workload, and whether that bound
survives added concurrency is set by the memory tier---an association across every
configuration we ran rather than an intervention (\S\ref{sec:model}). That claim
is measured on one trace family, on a pair that ran on two cards a day apart; a
same-card pairing returns the same verdict over a smaller gap, and the capacity
control returns no realization figure at all. Two boundaries come with it: on a
second workload both measurements survive in direction and neither in level, so
neither an occupancy nor a concurrency threshold transfers; and the variable-load
case, where a controller should have its best chance, is measured but not usable,
its baselines having been taken under host co-tenancy---we report it as open
rather than as a result.
