   147	\label{sec:results:idle-memory}

   150	keeps every session's permit for its lifetime, so its pool occupancy is a
   151	policy output, not a constraint. It runs at a median of 49.2\% of the pool on
   152	the base configuration, and the median number of sessions queueing for
   153	admission is 66. The configuration that finishes fastest---the repeat of that same static cap
   154	of four, at 209.1 minutes---runs at 71.9\%, and its first run at 70.2\%. Occupancy
   155	and running requests track each other reliably across the arms we have (the two
   156	columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} rank the twenty arms almost identically), but
   157	running count does \emph{not} order wall clock: \texttt{horizon}=2 runs 1.25
   158	requests against the base arm's 1.14 and finishes 15 minutes slower. That gap is
   159	what the next subsection is about.
   160	
   161	The controller believes it is nearly full. Its own decision log records the
   162	projection it admits against---the largest of measured occupancy and the summed
   163	contexts of live sessions, plus a reserve for their growth---at a median of
   164	89{,}073 tokens against a budget of 96{,}360, i.e.\ 7\% free. Its measured
   165	occupancy at the same instants is 49{,}875 tokens: 48.2\% of that budget, or
   166	50.8\% of the pool, is idle. The
   167	projection is 1.79$\times$ the truth on this configuration. Across the
   168	seventeen controller runs we have projection logs for, the ratio runs from 1.24
   169	to 4.11 with a median of 1.59 (\texttt{projection.csv}), so 1.79 is this arm's

   172	live session its full context, when a parked session's KV is evictable and

   191	
   192	The remaining 54.8\% are cycles where the controller selected no candidate at
   193	all, and here we are explicit about the limit of what we can resolve. The
   194	controller is single-flight by default: it authorizes at most one session per
   195	two-second cycle and waits for that session to begin a round before authorizing
   196	the next. Whether that gate held or whether a candidate was tried and did not
   197	fit is \emph{not distinguishable from our logs}---the controller does not record
   198	it---so we do not attribute the bucket to either. We bounded it by running that arm: the same configuration with single-flight
   199	disabled. Two things came out of it, and the second is the one that matters.
   200	
   201	The bound first. With the gate off, the residual bucket---which can then only
   202	be \emph{candidates that did not fit}, since the gate cannot be holding---falls
   203	from 54.8\% of blocked cycles to 39.7\%, against 44.0\% that are over budget
   204	outright. So the single-flight path accounts for roughly fifteen points, and
   205	the headroom estimate remains the largest single cause. (Both arms' numbers
   206	are rows of \texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, which now includes the \texttt{sf=0}
   207	arm; an earlier revision quoted 39.9 and 43.5 for the second pair, values that
   208	appeared in no data file.)
   209	
   210	The second thing is that disabling it does not make the controller win.
   211	\texttt{sf=0} finishes in 263.3 minutes at 86.3\% SLO against the base run's
   212	291.3 and 89.8\%: it buys 28 minutes for 3.5\,pp, and its signed distance from
   213	the front moves from $-1.25$ to $-0.57$---onto the tie band from below it, not
   214	above it. Its admission rate is unchanged (2.5\% of cycles admit, against
   215	2.3\%). So the gate was a real cost and removing it moves the arm along the
   216	front rather than past it, which is the same trade every other route in this
   217	section makes.
   218	
   219	Across the eight \sys{} variants that left controller logs---the set is named in
   220	\texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, and it is eight rather than nine because
   221	\texttt{foyfix} and the two AIMD arms run a different controller and log no
   222	gate state---the projection is over budget in a median 32.2\% of blocked
   223	cycles (19.5\% to 50.8\%) and the valve in a median 32.7\% (6.8\% to 67.9\%).
   224	Both medians move with the set, which is why we name it: over the wider group
   225	of runs in that file they are 37.6\% and 10.5\%. The round gate never binds
   226	except in the two runs that enable it---\texttt{rg2} at 29.3\% and \texttt{rg3}
   227	at 18.0\% of blocked cycles, against 0.6\% or less everywhere else---which is
   228	what one expects of a gate that is off by default.
   229	We also record what this accounting omits, because it changes the picture: the
   230	controller has a starvation guard that bypasses the budget entirely once the
   231	oldest waiter has waited 240\,s, cooling down for 300\,s. On the base run it
   232	accounts for 24.8\% of all admissions (51 of 206), and while the projection is
   233	over budget it is the \emph{only} way in---of 3{,}700 such cycles, nine admitted,
   234	and all nine were forced. It is therefore the guard, not the budget test, that
   235	sets the floor on how fast this controller can admit.
   236	
   237	The second is that under-use does \emph{not} order the outcomes, so it is not
   238	the whole explanation either. \texttt{horizon}=1 and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache sit
   239	at the same median occupancy (57.8\% of the pool) and the same mean running
   240	count (1.52 against 1.51 on the busy-time measure of \S\ref{sec:model}), and
   241	the cap is 19 minutes faster. \texttt{hz0} runs
   242	at 64.1\%, higher than that cap, and is 54 minutes slower. Occupancy buys
   243	running requests reliably---the two columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} track
   244	each other across all twenty arms---but converting running requests into wall
   245	clock is not simply the per-step cost of \S\ref{sec:model}. Decomposing that
   246	19 minutes into its idle and busy components, 12.6 of them are idle time---%
   247	65\%---and only 6.8 are busy; the two runs' step costs differ by 1.2\,ms
   248	(29.2 against 28.0), about 4\%. A parked session is one the controller has
   249	authorized but not yet released, so the same policy that sets the running count
   250	also sets a large part of the idle time. Both
   251	effects are real; on this workload the unused-memory one is larger for the arms
   252	that finish worst, and the step-cost one is what separates the arms that finish
   253	close together.

   325	\subsection{Route 1: the accounting base}
   326	
   327	Our controller's projection (Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) charges a session for its full
   328	context even while it is parked in a tool wait, when its KV is evictable and
   329	therefore absent from the engine's reported occupancy. We argued this was a
   330	double charge and removed it, admitting on engine telemetry alone.
   331	
   332	The charge is not double. Removing it did admit more---median engine occupancy
   333	rose from 49.2\% to 56.9\% of the pool---and the round-1+ prefix hit rate held at
   334	80.2\% against the 80.6\% of the run that keeps the charge, so no eviction spiral occurred. But the run was
   335	4.2 minutes \emph{slower} and lost 6.0\,pp of SLO. Per \S\ref{sec:model}, the
   336	extra occupancy raised both $\overline{R}$ (1.35 to 1.48) and $\tau_2$
   337	(23.8\,ms to 31.3\,ms), and the two cancelled.
   338	
   339	That last sentence is the one place where this route's conclusion depends on a
   340	contaminated measurement, and we mark it. The arm that tests this route
   341	(\texttt{bres}) is one of the four flagged $^\ddagger$ in
   342	Table~\ref{tab:runs}: its 31.3\,ms $\tau_2$ was measured under host
   343	co-tenancy, and the same arm measured clean would plausibly sit near 25\,ms
   344	rather than 31. On that reading the two terms would not cancel and the route
   345	would look better than we report, not worse---so the direction of the
   346	correction is against our own conclusion, which is the safe direction to be
   347	wrong in, but it is still unresolved. The occupancy half of the argument
   348	(49.2\% to 56.9\%) is a volume measurement and is unaffected. We keep the
   349	route in the negative column pending the re-run and say so here rather than
   350	in a footnote.
   351	
   352	We note that this claim had already been falsified once by source inspection:
   353	SGLang's \texttt{token\_usage} subtracts evictable blocks, so a parked
   354	session's prefix is genuinely not counted, and charging for it reserves
   355	capacity that really will be needed back. The end-to-end measurement agrees
   356	with the source reading.

   358	\subsection{Route 2: the growth reserve}
   359	
   360	Sessions grow by roughly 2.5K tokens per round, so the controller reserves
   361	capacity for the next \texttt{horizon} rounds. We swept the horizon over
   362	$\{0,1,2,3\}$ and the reserve is not a free parameter to be minimised:
   363	Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} shows the response is non-monotone. Removing the
   364	reserve entirely costs 12.6\,pp of SLO, and a reserve of two rounds is
   365	15 minutes \emph{slower} than three. The optimum is at one round, and there it comes closest to the
   366	static front---0.94\,pp below it, inside the tie band---and that single point is
   367	the one this paper spends the most words on, because it does not reproduce: the
   368	repeat of this configuration lands 5.6\,pp below the line
   369	(\S\ref{sec:results}). Read the sweep as a shape, not as a result at its
   370	minimum.

   378	\subsection{An independent adaptive controller}
   379	\label{sec:results:aimd}
   380	
   381	Every controller above is ours, which invites the objection that the negative
   382	result is a statement about one design rather than about adaptive admission.
   383	We therefore implemented a second, mechanistically different controller: an
   384	AIMD window over an agent-level admission set, following the control law of
   385	the congestion-based system we compare against in \S\ref{sec:related}. The
   386	window grows additively while cache occupancy sits below a low watermark, is
   387	cut multiplicatively when occupancy exceeds a high watermark \emph{and} the
   388	prefix hit rate falls below a threshold, and holds otherwise. Cuts pause the
   389	youngest sessions at their next step boundary; growth resumes paused sessions
   390	first. Every input is a runtime gauge---occupancy and hit rate from the
   391	engine, nothing from the trace---and the window acts through the same named
   392	permits as \sys{}.
   393	
   394	It does not beat the static front either, at either parameter setting
   395	(Table~\ref{tab:runs}). At the values reported in that paper's evaluation it
   396	finishes in 364.3 minutes at 81.4\% SLO, $11.2$\,pp below the envelope; the
   397	same arm with its retuned watermarks finishes in 264.6 minutes at 52.8\%,
   398	$33.4$\,pp below. The retuned setting is also one of the most
   399	volatile runs we report: its segment-to-segment SD is 13.2\,pp of SLO, with
   400	segment values swinging between 37 and 72. Three runs are worse (\texttt{cap4}
   401	at 15.8, the all-throttles-off arm at 14.4, and \texttt{breshz1} at 14.2), and
   402	no other controller arm is as bad---which is what a control loop oscillating
   403	around a capacity cliff looks like.
   404	
   405	We report two things honestly about this comparison. First, this is our
   406	reimplementation of a published control law, not the authors' system, and a
   407	reimplementation of any controller can underperform its paper. We built it to
   408	the published law, gave it the same permit interface and the same 2\,s cycle
   409	as \sys{}, and did not tune it beyond the two documented settings; but the
   410	caveat stands. Second, both controllers stall. At its paper parameters this one pinned its
   411	window at one session, admitted nothing, and left the engine idle for over an
   412	hour. Our own controller does the same thing more briefly: in 4 of the 11
   413	\sys{} runs an audit found a single window of 150--175\,s in which the engine
   414	was empty, the queue held ~90 sessions, the SLO valve was open, and the
   415	controller issued no permit at all---about 0.9\% of each run's wall clock. The
   416	remaining runs, including the best one, show no such window. Nothing is logged
   417	as an error and the run recovers on its own.
   418	
   419	We traced one such window in a repeat of our best configuration and found a
   420	code path that explains it: single-flight admission considered only the head of
   421	the FIFO queue, and when the head was a session too large to fit the remaining
   422	budget it was never admitted, never left the queue, and blocked every smaller
   423	session behind it. That is the code every run in this paper executed. The fix
   424	in our repository---scan the queue, still admit at most one per cycle---is
   425	\emph{not} in the artifact that produced these results, so the numbers here
   426	predate it and we do not claim them for it. We are also explicit that this is
   427	\emph{a} path, not a proven cause:
   428	the audit is a log signature, and the same signature can be produced by other
   429	closed gates. What we can say is that the signature is present in a third of
   430	our \sys{} runs and absent from all of our static caps, which have no
   431	admission decision to get stuck in.
   432	
   433	That is the honest version of a finding we first reported too strongly. An
   434	intermediate draft of this paper called it a deadlock and said the
   435	configuration that produced our best result was the one that deadlocked on its
   436	repeat. Neither holds: the windows are ~160\,s and self-clearing, and the best
   437	configuration never showed one. The windows are also too small (0.9\%) to move
   438	any verdict we report. We keep them because the mechanism is real and its size
   439	is the point: on a pool that holds four median sessions, a controller's failure
   440	mode is a silent stall, not graceful degradation, and it took a repeat run to
   441	see it.

   443	\subsection{Route 4: execution granularity}
   444	\label{sec:results:gate}
   445	
   446	The first three routes all assume that what a controller can set is \emph{how
   447	many sessions are resident}. A static cap sets the same quantity, so it is
   448	unsurprising that they land in the same place. The fourth route attacks the
   449	assumption instead: because a large fraction of live sessions are parked at
   450	any instant, a cap on \emph{resident} sessions leaves the engine idle whenever
   451	all residents happen to be parked. Capping the \emph{running-request} count
   452	instead should keep the engine busy while leaving the resident count high.
   453	
   454	The permit design supports this directly: a paused session finishes its
   455	current round and blocks before its next one, and resumes when the controller
   456	re-admits it. We implemented it as a gate on the engine's own
   457	\texttt{num\_running\_reqs} and swept the gate over $\{2,4\}$. Only the
   458	gate-of-four arm completed (999 steps); the gate-of-two arm reached 770 and is
   459	not used, so what we report below is a single comparison against the no-gate
   460	arm rather than a sweep.
   461	
   462	It does not work, and the measurement says why. Against the no-gate arm, a
   463	gate of four moved mean running requests from 1.33 to 1.30 and mean live
   464	sessions from 3.25 to 3.03: it tightened both, by 2\% and 7\%, rather than
   465	pinning the one it aims at. That is what we
   466	observe, and we state it as the observation it is rather than deriving a bound
   467	we cannot support. A live session spends most of its time parked in a tool wait
   468	(the ratio of mean running requests to mean live sessions is 0.38 to 0.66
   469	across our arms), so pausing live sessions removes capacity that was not
   470	running in the first place---but that ratio is an average over a run, not a
   471	bound on any instant, and the running count can in principle reach the live
   472	count. Both repairs fail: without a
   473	constraint on admission, a paused session is re-admitted on the next cycle and
   474	blocks for at most one 2\,s cycle; with one, the policy is a session cap again.
   475	The obstacle is granularity---\texttt{paused} takes effect at round
   476	boundaries, while pinning an instantaneous count requires control the
   477	permit mechanism does not have.
   478	
   479	The measured outcome matches: the gate moved running concurrency from 1.33 to
   480	1.30 and live sessions from 3.25 to 3.03. It tightened rather than pinned, and
   481	a tightening is another cap.

    54	\subsection{What determines busy time}
    55	
    56	During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
    57	running request. Hence
    58	\begin{equation}
    59	  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
    60	  \label{eq:busy}
    61	\end{equation}
    62	with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and
    63	$\tau$ the per-step cost. Across our runs $\tau$ is not a fit parameter we
    64	tuned; we obtain it two independent ways, and reporting both is what shows
    65	they are measuring different things rather than confirming each other.
    66	
    67	\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} /
    68	G$. Two choices in that expression are easy to get wrong and both matter. The
    69	time term must be the \emph{busy} component, not the wall clock---using the
    70	wall clock charges idle time to the steps. And $\overline{R}$ must be the mean
    71	running count \emph{during busy time}, not the mean over the whole run: the
    72	whole-run mean includes the idle samples, where the count is zero, so it equals
    73	$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \times$ (busy fraction). Using it makes $\tau_1$
    74	too low by exactly the busy fraction---36.3\% for \texttt{cap1}, whose idle
    75	share is 36.3\%. We report both columns in the data, and use the
    76	busy-conditional mean throughout; the whole-run mean is what an earlier
    77	revision of this paper used, and it made the two methods agree far better than
    78	they do.
    79	
    80	\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} For each step record we compute the
    81	per-request decode interval,
    82	$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$,
    83	and take the median over steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$.
    84	
    85	The two do not agree exactly, and the disagreement is informative rather than
    86	embarrassing. $\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every run, by 7.9--39.8\%, which in
    87	absolute terms is 2.6--8.2\,ms of a 27--37\,ms step. The gap is
    88	\emph{consistent with} engine steps that are prefill and
    89	therefore emit no output token---we can bound it, not identify it, because
    90	subtracting a median from a mean does not isolate a component. All we can form
    91	from these two is their ratio: the excess $\tau_1/\tau_2 - 1$ runs
    92	17.6--39.8\% across the five device-only configurations. Two things follow, and
    93	we separate them. First, it is a ratio of \emph{times}, not of steps, and we
    94	have no measurement of the fraction of step \emph{counts} that are prefill---
    95	chunked prefill makes those two different numbers. Second, even as a time
    96	ratio it is the gap between two measurements, not a decomposition of one: it
    97	is consistent with prefill and non-decode work filling it, but we cannot rule
    98	out that some of the difference is the two estimators disagreeing for other
    99	reasons. So the two are not
   100	two estimates of one number; they are measurements of two different things,
   101	and we do not size a component with their difference. Fig.~\ref{fig:steptime} shows both, run by run. Method 2 remains the one that does not depend on the
   102	quantity it is used to explain, and it is the one we quote when the question
   103	is the cost of a decode step. The engine's own latency counters

   120	\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}
   121	
   122	Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is
   123	not. Across our 13 runs $\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and $\tau_2$
   124	from 20.6 to 33.1\,ms, and both rise with the running count: fitting
   125	Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
   126	$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$
   127	($R^2 = 0.89$ for the latter, Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).
   128	
   129	The relation is a trend, not a law. Two host-tier configurations with similar
   130	running counts behave differently: \texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at
   131	29.8\,ms while \texttt{F:hz0} runs 1.66 at 35.7\,ms. Running count sets a
   132	floor and a trend, not a value---and on the host-tier family, as we show below,
   133	it does not set the value at all.
   134	
   135	This is the crux of the paper, and we state it at the strength the arithmetic
   136	supports. Hold $\tau_1$ at its value for \texttt{cap1}, 28.8\,ms, and busy
   137	time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from 1.00 to 1.84
   138	should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to 184. The
   139	measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes back 50 of
   140	the 156 minutes that the extra concurrency would otherwise have bought---\emph{a
   141	third} of the gain, not all of it and, as an earlier revision of this paper
   142	claimed, not more than half. The other two thirds are real: raising
   143	concurrency does make the engine faster. What it does not do is make it faster
   144	in proportion, and the shortfall compounds with every controller that tries to
   145	exploit the difference.
   146	
   147	\paragraph{What the engine is doing when it is not decoding.} SGLang exposes
   148	two counters that answer this directly: tokens evicted from the radix cache,
   149	and prompt tokens that had to be prefilled rather than reused. Across the five
   150	static caps \emph{without} HiCache the two track each other almost exactly.
   151	\texttt{cap1} evicts 9.2\,M tokens and prefills 8.6\,M uncached;
   152	\texttt{cap5} evicts 21.3\,M and prefills 20.7\,M; the gap is 0.6--0.7\,M on
   153	every one of the five. An evicted prefix is recomputed, close to one token for
   154	one, and that recomputation is chunked into decode steps. Those same five runs
   155	give recomputation rising with $\tau$ across four of the five points: 9.2\,M
   156	uncached tokens at 27.4\,ms, 11.5 at 29.7, 15.4 at 32.4, 20.7 at 36.7. The
   157	exception is the first point, \texttt{cap1}, which recomputes least (8.6\,M)
   158	and still costs 28.8\,ms---more than \texttt{cap2} does for more recomputation.
   159	We report the ordering we have rather than rounding it to a monotone one: it is
   160	a mechanism consistent with the $\tau$ trend on four of the five device-only
   161	configurations, and one point it does not order. It is five points on five
   162	different policies, not an
   163	intervention: we did not hold concurrency fixed and vary eviction, so we call
   164	it consistent rather than demonstrated.

   185	\paragraph{Where the mechanism stops.} With HiCache enabled the
   186	correspondence breaks---and it breaks in a way that confirms the mechanism
   187	rather than undermining it. The device cache misses as much as before:
   188	\texttt{cap4\_hc} misses 22.4\,M tokens on the device, more than the
   189	device-only \texttt{cap4} misses. But only 9.1\,M of that is recomputed; the
   190	other 13.3\,M is served from the host tier. Across the ten host-tier runs
   191	the host serves 12--19\,M tokens each, and across the five device-only runs it
   192	serves \emph{exactly zero}---the device miss equals the recomputed count to
   193	within rounding on all five. Recomputation is still what the device cannot
   194	absorb; the host tier is a second absorber in front of it.
   195	
   196	\subsection{The tier, not the policy, decides whether reuse survives}
   197	
   198	The device-miss column hides the quantity that matters. Define
   199	\emph{reuse} as the prompt tokens served from anywhere in the memory
   200	hierarchy---device radix cache plus host tier---so that
   201	$\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$, with the prompt fixed at
   202	28.5\,M by the trace.
   203	
   204	The bound on reuse has to be defined independently of what we observed,
   205	or the argument is circular. We take it from the trace: summing the planned
   206	reusable prefix (\texttt{prefix\_len}) over all 999 rounds of
   207	\texttt{replay\_pois200\_l04.csv} gives 21{,}540{,}371 tokens against a prompt
   208	total of 28{,}526{,}871, so no policy can reuse more than 75.5\% of the
   209	prompt. That is a property of the workload. It is also a bound rather than a
   210	description: \emph{no configuration we ran reaches it}, and the best realizes
   211	92.6\%, so the gap between ``planned'' and ``achieved'' exists even in the
   212	easiest case. What varies across configurations is how much of the bound is
   213	realized, and that is where the tier enters.

   229	Device-only, realization falls from 92.6\% to 36.3\% and the shortfall is
   230	recomputation: 8.6\,M to 20.7\,M tokens. With a host tier it holds at
   231	90.2--92.3\% on the static points that span the same range, and at
   232	83.9--92.3\% across the ten host-tier runs including the controller arms---the
   233	lowest of those being the arm that disables its own admission gate and
   234	therefore runs the pool hardest. and it holds
   235	while the \emph{device} misses more than the device-only family's at every
   236	point (22.4--27.9\,M against 8.6--20.7\,M), because the host serves
   237	13--19\,M in front of the recomputation instead of letting the device keep
   238	it. A reader watching only the device hit rate would conclude the host tier
   239	made things worse. (The 92.6\% ceiling-on-prompt and the 75.5\% bound are not
   240	in competition: the first is what we measured, the second is what the trace
   241	permits; \texttt{reuse.csv} carries both columns and the raw counts.)
   242	
   243	This is the same collapse \S\ref{sec:related} reports under a different
   244	normalization. That section quotes the round-1-and-later prefix hit rate---%
   245	steps restricted to rounds $\ge 1$, unweighted mean over steps---which falls
   246	from 80.8\% to 32.8\% across the device-only caps and holds at 80.7\% to
   247	78.7\% with a host tier. The two conventions differ in two ways, not one:
   248	which steps are in the set (round $\ge 1$ against all rounds) and how steps
   249	are combined (unweighted mean against token-weighted sum). We use the
   250	token-weighted all-steps convention here because reuse and recomputation must
   251	add to the prompt, and we name the other whenever we quote it. Both are
   252	\emph{realized} quantities that vary with the policy; neither is the trace's
   253	planned rate, which is fixed at 75.5\% and appears in this section only as
   254	the bound.
   255	
   256	Device occupancy does not explain the split. \texttt{cap3\_fixed} and
   257	\texttt{cap3\_hc} run at nearly the same mean device occupancy (55.8\% and
   258	55.5\%) and nearly the same running count (1.53 and 1.51), and they realize
   259	78.9\% and 91.1\% of the bound respectively. What differs is not how full the
   260	device pool is but what happens when it overflows: with no host tier an
   261	evicted prefix is gone and must be recomputed; with one it is demoted and read
   262	back. The pool is the binding resource, the tier sets the pool, and the tier
   263	therefore sets whether added concurrency is paid for in recomputation. The
   264	caveat is that this pair is two runs on two different days and two different
   265	GPUs, not a paired experiment; \S\ref{sec:repro} states what we did to rule
   266	out co-tenancy. A capacity-controlled version---same card, same trace, only
   267	the pool size changed---is queued behind the re-runs and we report it in
   268	\S\ref{sec:results} when it lands; we do not use it here.
   269	
   270	\paragraph{The second trace agrees on direction and not on level.}
   271	We ran the same accounting on a second workload, reported in full in
   272	\S\ref{sec:xtrace}---117 real Claude agent sessions, 390 rounds, the same pool
   273	and the same engine---and it reproduces the direction: its realization of \emph{its own} bound falls from 74.8\% to 67.5\%
   274	as the running count rises from 1.08 to 1.29, a steeper per-unit drop than the
   275	device-only family's. What it does not reproduce is the level, and this is
   276	worth stating because it bounds what we can claim. That trace runs at a
   277	\emph{lower} median device occupancy (15.8\% against 22.4\%) and realizes
   278	\emph{less}, so realization is not a function of occupancy alone---and a
   279	statement of the form ``the pool collapses above $x$\% occupancy'' is wrong on
   280	our own data. Occupancy is instantaneous while reuse depends on history; a
   281	session's prefix must survive from one round to the next, and how much
   282	pressure that puts on the pool is not visible in what the pool holds at an
   283	instant. We do not have the measurement that would separate those, which is
   284	why the controlled experiment we report in \S\ref{sec:results} varies the
   285	pool alone, on one card and one trace, rather than leaning on the cross-trace
   286	comparison. The claim this section supports is the narrow one: within a
   287	configuration, the host tier preserves realization at a matched running count
   288	and a matched occupancy, and without it realization falls.
   289	
   290	That gives the criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} a mechanism rather than a
   291	correlation, and it suggests a prediction we are testing:
   292	if the tier acts by multiplying usable capacity (the host pool is twice the
   293	device pool, so three times in total), then shrinking the device pool alone
   294	should move the collapse point even with no host tier involved. We do not
   295	treat the arithmetic as a result---it assumes KV demand grows linearly in the
   296	running count, which heterogeneous sessions need not satisfy---and the
   297	queued experiment measures the collapse point directly rather than predicting
   298	it, and \S\ref{sec:results} reports it once it has run.
   299	
   300	\paragraph{What is left unexplained.} Not everything here is accounted for.
   301	The host-tier runs sit at a narrow band of recomputed tokens (8.6--10.4\,M)
   302	\emph{and} of realization (83.9--92.3\%), yet $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.2\,ms
   303	across them.
   304	Part of that is now explained and part is not. Three of those runs
   305	(\texttt{F:hz2} 33.6, \texttt{F:bres} 34.1, \texttt{F:hz0} 35.7\,ms) were
   306	measured while three other engine instances shared the host, which inflates
   307	per-step cost by 25--40\% and leaves the volume columns untouched
   308	(\S\ref{sec:repro}); they are being re-measured and we do not use their
   309	timing. Excluding them the remaining six span 27.0--31.5\,ms at flat
   310	recomputation. That residual is small but it is not zero, and we do not
   311	attribute it. We note that the negative result does not rest on it: the
   312	cancellation is measured directly, not inferred from the mechanism.
   313	
   314	One caution about the strength of the account above, since the arithmetic
   315	invites overreading. $\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$ is a
   316	definition, not a finding, so ``the shortfall reappears as recomputation''
   317	follows from how we partitioned the prompt and cannot by itself establish the
   318	mechanism. What is independent of the partition is the pair of engine
   319	counters: evicted tokens and uncached prefill tokens are separate SGLang
   320	counters, and on the device-only runs they track within 0.6--0.7\,M on all
   321	five---which is the observation that licenses reading eviction as the cause
   322	of recomputation. With a host tier the two come apart by design, and the
   323	13--19\,M gap between them is the host's contribution.
   324	
   325	\paragraph{A refutation we had to withdraw.} An earlier revision withdrew this
   326	mechanism outright, on two checks that were both wrong. The first was that
   327	total prefilled tokens equalled the trace's planned prefill to a ratio of 1.00,
   328	so no token could have been computed twice. \texttt{prompt\_tokens\_total}
   329	counts the full submitted prompt \emph{including} its cached portion, so it
   330	equals the sum of prompt lengths by construction and cannot detect
   331	recomputation; the quantity that can is the uncached count used above, which
   332	varies by 2.4$\times$ across these runs. The second was that SGLang reported
   333	no retractions. Radix-cache eviction is not a retraction---retractions count
   334	running requests preempted mid-flight, a different event with its own counter.
   335	We record the episode because it is the same failure mode as the segment
   336	argument of \S\ref{sec:bg}: a check that looks like a measurement but is an
   337	identity.

   339	\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}
   340	
   341	The length of an individual idle stretch is a property of the workload, not of
   342	the controller. A session in a
   343	tool wait holds its permit and its KV but issues no request; with only about
   344	two live sessions, the probability that all of them are simultaneously waiting
   345	is substantial. The direct evidence is the duration of the stretches
   346	themselves: they are of the same order as the tool waits, and an order of
   347	magnitude longer than the 2\,s control cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). No
   348	controller can shorten a gap that the tool set.
   349	
   350	How much idle a \emph{run} accumulates is a different matter, and there the
   351	controller is not off the hook. Idle accumulates when every live session
   352	happens to be parked at once, and how many sessions are live is precisely what
   353	an admission policy sets. Two configurations with the same mean running count
   354	make this visible: \texttt{horizon}=1 (1.52) and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache (1.51)
   355	idle 36.7 and 24.1 minutes respectively---a 52\% difference at matched
   356	concurrency---and 12.6 of the 19.4-minute wall-clock gap between them is that
   357	idle difference rather than step cost. So the gap durations are the workload's,
   358	and the number of gaps is partly the policy's.
   359	
   360	We note one claim we had to drop here. An earlier draft reported that idle
   361	fraction falls monotonically with mean concurrency. With the full data it does
   362	not: \texttt{F:hz0} at 1.41 running requests idles 15.2\% while \texttt{cap4}
   363	at 1.48 idles 10.4\%. The weaker statement that survives is the one we use---%
   364	the stretches are structural and long.
   365	
   366	The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always
   367	ready---is what \texttt{bres} does. It works in the narrow sense: \texttt{bres}
   368	recovers about 17 of the minutes that its own baseline spends idle. It is
   369	still slower overall, because the sessions it keeps live raise the step cost
   370	by more than the recovered idle is worth. Idle is not unrecoverable; it is
   371	recoverable at a price that is not worth paying here.
   372	The structural remedy would be to make the engine do work during a wait, but
   373	there is no work to do: the session's next round does not exist until its tool
   374	returns.

     1	\section{Introduction}
     2	\label{sec:intro}
     3	
     4	An agentic LLM session is not a request. It is a long-lived job that issues a
     5	round of inference, waits on a tool, and comes back---tens of times---with a
     6	context that grows every round. On a memory-constrained accelerator this makes
     7	admission control look like the obvious lever. The KV pool is the binding
     8	resource; a single large session can occupy most of it; and the sessions
     9	themselves are wildly heterogeneous. If a scheduler can see how much of the
    10	pool a candidate will need and how much room is left, surely it should beat a
    11	fixed number.
    12	
    13	We believed this, built a controller that does exactly that, and spent a
    14	considerable amount of GPU time discovering that on our workload and hardware
    15	it is false. This paper reports that negative result, and the negative result is
    16	most of what we have. Alongside it we report one measurement---the elasticity of
    17	per-step cost with respect to the running count---that we hoped would say in
    18	advance how much room admission control has. It does bound that room on the
    19	configurations we measured, but when we tested whether it explains our
    20	controllers' failure, on their own configuration and on a second workload, it
    21	did not. We report it as a diagnostic of a workload, not as a predictor of
    22	whether a controller will win.

    56	\subsection{Why}
    57	
    58	We decompose the wall clock into \emph{busy} time, during which the engine has
    59	at least one request in flight, and \emph{idle} time, during which it has
    60	none. The busy component is governed by $W_{\text{busy}} \approx
    61	(G/\overline{R})\,\tau$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:busy}), where $\overline{R}$ is the mean
    62	number of running requests, $G$ the generated tokens and $\tau$ the per-step
    63	cost. An admission policy moves $\overline{R}$, and on our configurations it
    64	moves little else---\texttt{bres} is the exception, and \S\ref{sec:model}
    65	uses it to show why moving both components at once is still not enough.
    66	The difficulty is that $\tau$ is not constant in $\overline{R}$: across our
    67	runs $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.7\,ms, and on the device-only family, raising the
    68	busy-time running count from 1.00 to 1.84 absorbs a third of what the extra
    69	concurrency buys.
    70	
    71	The reason $\tau$ moves is recomputation, and whether recomputation happens is
    72	decided by the memory tier rather than by the policy. A session's prefix is
    73	worth reusing only while it is still resident, so prefix \emph{reuse} is
    74	bounded by the workload---in our trace, 75.5\% of the prompt tokens, a bound
    75	no configuration we ran reaches. How much of that bound is realized is where
    76	the tier enters. With the KV pool on the device alone, realization falls as
    77	concurrency rises---92.6\% of the bound at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00
    78	down to 36.3\% at 1.84---and the shortfall appears as recomputation, 8.6\,M to
    79	20.7\,M tokens. With a host tier in front of it, realization holds across the
    80	range our controllers occupy---90.2\% to 92.3\% between 1.25 and 1.73---even
    81	though the \emph{device} misses more. Two configurations at the same running
    82	count and the same pool occupancy realize 78.9\% and 91.1\% of the bound
    83	respectively (\S\ref{sec:model}); what separates them is not how full the pool
    84	is but what happens when it overflows. That pair also differs in date and in
    85	GPU, so we also run a version that changes the pool size alone on one card;
    86	it is queued behind the re-runs and \S\ref{sec:results} reports it when it
    87	lands.
    88	That is the finding we would carry to another system first, and it is why this
    89	paper's answer to its own title is a property of the deployment rather than of
    90	the controller.
    91	
    92	The idle component is structural rather than a control failure. A session in a
    93	tool wait issues no request, so when all live sessions happen to be waiting,
    94	the engine is genuinely empty. The stretches are short in the middle and long
    95	in the tail---median 5.6\,s, mean 20.6\,s---and the tail is what a scheduler
    96	feels: 69.6\% of all idle \emph{time} sits in stretches over 20\,s, an order
    97	of magnitude above the 2\,s control cycle. Crucially, the tool wait itself is much longer than
    98	the trace's median suggests: the median inter-round gap is 0.35\,s, but the
    99	\emph{mean} is 15.8--16.9\,s with 22\% of gaps over ten seconds. A controller
   100	that
   101	reads only the median concludes, as we initially did, that sessions never
   102	leave and therefore that there is no idle time to recover. There is.

   104	\subsection{Contributions}
   105	
   106	\begin{itemize}
   107	  \item \textbf{We identify the memory tier as what decides how much of the

   113	        realization falls from 92.6\% to 36.3\% between

   115	        as recomputation, 8.6\,M to 20.7\,M tokens; with a host tier it holds
   116	        at 90.2--92.3\% over 1.25 to 1.73, while the \emph{device} misses more
   117	        (\S\ref{sec:model}). Two configurations at the same running count and
   118	        the same pool occupancy realize 78.9\% and 91.1\% of the bound, so it
   119	        is not how full the pool is but what happens when it overflows; that
   120	        pair differs in date and GPU as well, so we also vary the pool alone

# 发现：复用兑现率跨 trace **不能**用一个占用阈值概括

**日期** 2026-09-24 · **数据** `paper/data/reuse.csv` + realhr 四条 run 的原始日志
**检验** 方向 B 的主线主张「容量决定复用兑现率」能不能迁移到第二条 trace

## 怎么发现的

做方向 B 时去查：realhr（第二条 trace，device-only，与我们主 trace 同池子 101,432）
的静态点能不能复现主 trace 的兑现率曲线。四条 run 的日志齐全，不用等新实验。

用与 `gen_reuse.py` 完全相同的定义算（上界从 trace 的 `prefix_len` 求和独立得到，
runner 的 `summary.json` 里也有同一个数，两处一致）：

| run | R̄_busy | 占用 p50 | 计划上界 | 实测复用 | **兑现率** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `realhr_cap2` | 1.076 | **15.8%** | 86.27% | 64.51% | **74.8%** |
| `realhr_cap3` | 1.160 | 18.4% | 86.27% | 62.24% | **72.2%** |
| `realhr_cap4` | 1.287 | 19.8% | 86.27% | 58.23% | **67.5%** |
| `realhr_foyer` | 1.222 | 19.4% | 86.27% | 59.84% | **69.4%** |

对照主 trace 的 device-only 五条：

| run | R̄_busy | 占用 p50 | 计划上界 | 兑现率 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cap1` | 1.000 | **22.4%** | 75.51% | **92.6%** |
| `cap2` | 1.271 | 41.6% | 75.51% | 89.6% |
| `cap3_fixed` | 1.534 | 59.0% | 75.51% | 78.9% |
| `cap4` | 1.650 | 66.4% | 75.51% | 60.6% |
| `cap5` | 1.842 | 74.3% | 75.51% | 36.3% |

## 结论一（支持）：两条 trace 上都出现兑现率随并发下降

这是好的：**「加并发 → 兑现率掉 → 重算补上」不是主 trace 的孤例。**
realhr 从 74.8% 掉到 67.5%（R̄ 1.076→1.287），direction 一致。

按 R̄ 计的斜率，realhr 甚至更陡：
- 主 trace：−3.0pp / ΔR̄ 0.27 = **−11.1 pp/单位**
- realhr：−7.3pp / ΔR̄ 0.21 = **−34.5 pp/单位**

## 结论二（**不支持**）：兑现率不能用占用率概括，跨 trace 更不行

主 trace 在占用 **22.4%** 时兑现 92.6%；realhr 在占用 **15.8%**（更低！）时只兑现
74.8%。**占用更低而兑现更差，方向是反的。**

所以「池子占到 X% 就开始塌」这种说法**不成立**，跨 trace 尤其不成立。可能的原因
（都**没有**测过，不许当结论写）：
- 占用率是**瞬时**量，而复用取决于**历史**：realhr 的 117 个会话在两小时内轮流，
  每个会话的上一轮前缀要活到下一轮才谈得上复用，而这一段的淘汰压力不体现在
  瞬时占用上；
- 两条 trace 的会话大小、轮次间隔、到达统计都不同（realhr 每步 prompt 18.6K，
  主 trace 28.6K）。

## 对论文的影响

1. **§III 那段「梯队决定兑现」在【同一配置内 + host tier 对照】上仍然成立**，
   那个对照是匹配占用的（cap3_fixed 55.8% vs cap3_hc 55.5%），不受本条影响。
2. **但不能写成「容量/占用决定兑现率」这种带可迁移阈值的说法。** 摘要与引言里
   「the tier decides how much of the bound survives」这个措辞仍然安全（它讲的是
   梯队这个**变量**起作用），但**任何暗示「占用率是那个自变量」的句子都要去掉**。
3. 这与论文既有的立场一致：§IV 已经承认弹性判据是「工作负载 + 配置的诊断，
   不是预测器」。现在复用兑现率有同样的性质，**应该一起说**，而不是假装指数
   干净。
4. **这让半池实验变得更重要**：同一张卡、同一条 trace、同一个模型，只动池子大小，
   是目前唯一能把「容量」这一个变量单独拎出来的实验。跨 trace 做不到。

## 待办

- 半池实验（`lane_pool50.sh`，已排队）落地后，用同一套定义算兑现率，
  看它是否随池子大小单调移动。**这是唯一能定因果的那个实验。**
- realhr 的 cap5/cap6/cap8（正在跑）会告诉我们兑现率在更高 R̄ 处掉到哪里 ——
  如果它在占用仍低的情况下继续掉，那「占用不是自变量」这条就被进一步坐实。

    13	$\epsilon = 0$ the step cost is flat, concurrency converts one for one into
    14	throughput, and an admission controller has room to work. At $\epsilon = 1$
    15	the two cancel exactly and it has none. Everything in between is a
    16	quantitative statement about how much room.

    60	none. The busy component is governed by $W_{\text{busy}} \approx
    61	(G/\overline{R})\,\tau$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:busy}), where $\overline{R}$ is the mean
    62	number of running requests, $G$ the generated tokens and $\tau$ the per-step
    63	cost. An admission policy moves $\overline{R}$, and on our configurations it
    64	moves little else---\texttt{bres} is the exception, and \S\ref{sec:model}
    65	uses it to show why moving both components at once is still not enough.
    66	The difficulty is that $\tau$ is not constant in $\overline{R}$: across our
    67	runs $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.7\,ms, and on the device-only family, raising the
    68	busy-time running count from 1.00 to 1.84 absorbs a third of what the extra
    69	concurrency buys.
    70	
    71	The reason $\tau$ moves is recomputation, and whether recomputation happens is
    72	decided by the memory tier rather than by the policy. A session's prefix is
    73	worth reusing only while it is still resident, so prefix \emph{reuse} is
    74	bounded by the workload---in our trace, 75.5\% of the prompt tokens, a bound
    75	no configuration we ran reaches. How much of that bound is realized is where
    76	the tier enters. With the KV pool on the device alone, realization falls as
    77	concurrency rises---92.6\% of the bound at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00
    78	down to 36.3\% at 1.84---and the shortfall appears as recomputation, 8.6\,M to

    91

   129	def front_slo(env, wall):
   130	    """包络在给定墙钟处的 SLO 上界。范围外返回 None，不做端点外推。"""
   131	    if not env or wall < env[0][0] or wall > env[-1][0]:
   132	        return None

   150	def seg_lookup(sigma, tau_a, tau_b):
   151	    """相邻两个静态 cap 之间的分段弹性。tau_a/tau_b 给出 τ₁ 与 τ₂ 两种口径。
   152	
   153	    ⚠ 取整不是无关紧要的，而这不是小事：gen_elasticity.py 读的是 decomp.csv，
   154	    那里的 running 只留 2 位、step_back_ms 只留 1 位。ε 是两个对数之比，分母
   155	    ln(R̄1/R̄0) 在 R̄≈1.5 时只有 ~0.076，2 位取整能把它挪动 3%，于是 cap3→cap4
   156	    从 1.15 变成 1.20。所以本函数算【三个】口径，并且主列 eps1 严格复刻
   157	    gen_elasticity.py 的输入精度（τ₁ 1 位、R̄ 2 位），这样主 trace 那一列能和
   158	    论文 §IV 的四个数逐位对上；eps1f 是未取整的，eps2 用独立的 τ₂。
   159	    """
   160	    out = []
   161	    for i in range(len(sigma) - 1):
   162	        r0, r1 = sigma[i][1], sigma[i + 1][1]
   163	        r0r, r1r = round(r0, 2), round(r1, 2)          # 与 decomp.csv 同精度
   164	        a, b = tau_a[i], tau_a[i + 1]
   165	        c, d = tau_b[i], tau_b[i + 1]
   166	        ar, br, cr, dr = round(a, 1), round(b, 1), round(c, 1), round(d, 1)
   167	        e1 = math.log(br / ar) / math.log(r1r / r0r) if a and b else None
   168	        e1f = math.log(b / a) / math.log(r1 / r0) if a and b else None
   169	        e2 = math.log(dr / cr) / math.log(r1r / r0r) if c and d else None
   170	        # 噪声带：ε 是两个对数之比，分母 ln(R̄1/R̄0) 在 R̄≈1.2 时只有 ~0.07，
   171	        # 所以 τ 的百分之几就能把分子放倒。这里把 τ₁a、τ₁b 各自 ±p 独立扰动，
   172	        # 给出 ε 的范围。p 取 5% / 10%，参照本仓 repeat 实测的墙钟极差
   173	        # （同 policy 同 trace 的重复对：1.2% ~ 22.4%，见 xtrace_validation.md）。
   174	        lo5 = hi5 = lo10 = hi10 = None
   175	        if a and b:
   176	            for p, lo_ref, hi_ref in ((0.05, "lo5", "hi5"), (0.10, "lo10", "hi10")):
   177	                d_ = math.log(r1r / r0r)
   178	                lo = math.log((1 - p) * br / ((1 + p) * ar)) / d_
   179	                hi = math.log((1 + p) * br / ((1 - p) * ar)) / d_
   180	                if lo_ref == "lo5":
   181	                    lo5, hi5 = lo, hi
   182	                else:
   183	                    lo10, hi10 = lo, hi
   184	        out.append(dict(from_=sigma[i][0], to=sigma[i + 1][0], r0=r0, r1=r1,
   185	                        tau1_a=ar, tau1_b=br, eps1=e1, eps1f=e1f, eps2=e2,
   186	                        lo5=lo5, hi5=hi5, lo10=lo10, hi10=hi10))
   187	    return out
