     1	\section{Introduction}
     2	\label{sec:intro}
     3	
     4	An agentic LLM session is not a request. It is a long-lived job that issues a
     5	round of inference, waits on a tool, and comes back---tens of times---with a
     6	context that grows every round. On a memory-constrained accelerator this makes
     7	admission control look like the obvious lever. The KV pool is the binding
     8	resource; a single large session can occupy most of it; and the sessions
     9	themselves are wildly heterogeneous. If a scheduler can see how much of the
    10	pool a candidate will need and how much room is left, surely it should beat a
    11	fixed number.
    12	
    13	We believed this, built a controller that does exactly that, and spent a
    14	considerable amount of GPU time discovering that on our workload and hardware
    15	it is false. This paper reports that negative result, and the negative result is
    16	most of what we have. Alongside it we report one measurement---the elasticity of
    17	per-step cost with respect to the running count---that we hoped would say in
    18	advance how much room admission control has. It does bound that room on the
    19	configurations we measured, but when we tested whether it explains our
    20	controllers' failure, on their own configuration and on a second workload, it
    21	did not. We report it as a diagnostic of a workload, not as a predictor of
    22	whether a controller will win.
    23	
    24	\subsection{What we measured}
    25	
    26	We serve a replay of 200 real coding-agent sessions on a single RTX 3090
    27	(Qwen2.5-Coder-7B, 96K context, SGLang, a 101{,}432-token KV pool) and compare
    28	a family of admission policies against a sweep of static concurrency caps.
    29	Nine controller variants span four mechanistically distinct ideas, and a
    30	reimplementation of an independent congestion-based controller from the
    31	literature runs alongside them. Six of the nine have measurements we can
    32	compare, and none of those six is above the static Pareto front
    33	(Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). The best of them misses by under a point---inside the
    34	$\pm1.0$\,pp band we call a tie, but on the below side of it---and the closest
    35	arm we repeated misses by 0.9 with its repeat at 5.6. Four of the nine were
    36	measured while other jobs shared the host; we re-measured one, which moved 6.5
    37	points closer to the front without crossing it, and \S\ref{sec:results} says
    38	what the verdict rests on until the other three land.

    66	\subsection{Why}
    67	
    68	We decompose the wall clock into \emph{busy} time, during which the engine has
    69	at least one request in flight, and \emph{idle} time, during which it has
    70	none. The busy component is governed by $W_{\text{busy}} \approx
    71	(G/\overline{R})\,\tau$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:busy}), where $\overline{R}$ is the mean
    72	number of running requests, $G$ the generated tokens and $\tau$ the per-step
    73	cost. An admission policy moves $\overline{R}$, and on our configurations it
    74	moves little else---though \S\ref{sec:model} gives two arms where the busy and
    75	idle components move in \emph{opposite} directions, which is why the split is
    76	worth making at all.
    77	The difficulty is that $\tau$ is not constant in $\overline{R}$: across our
    78	runs $\tau_1$ spans 27.0--36.7\,ms, and on the device-only family, raising the
    79	busy-time running count from 1.00 to 1.84 absorbs a third of what the extra
    80	concurrency buys.
    81	
    82	The reason $\tau$ moves is recomputation, and whether recomputation happens is
    83	decided by the memory tier rather than by the policy. A session's prefix is
    84	worth reusing only while it is still resident, so prefix \emph{reuse} is
    85	bounded by the workload---in our trace, 75.5\% of the prompt tokens, a bound
    86	no configuration we ran reaches. How much of that bound is realized is where
    87	the tier enters. With the KV pool on the device alone, realization falls as
    88	concurrency rises---92.5\% of the bound at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00
    89	down to 36.4\% at 1.84---and the shortfall appears as recomputation, 8.6\,M to
    90	20.7\,M tokens. With a host tier in front of it the decline disappears: over the
    91	running counts our controllers occupy, the static points fall only from 92.4\%
    92	to 90.2\%, and the worst of the nineteen host-tier runs---the one that disables
    93	every throttle---still realizes 72.8\%, where the nearest device-only point
    94	(the cap of four, at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.65) manages 60.7\%, while
    95	the \emph{device} misses more. Two configurations at the same running
    96	count and the same pool occupancy realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound
    97	respectively (\S\ref{sec:model}); what separates them is not how full the pool
    98	is but what happens when it overflows. That pair also differs in date and in
    99	GPU, so a capacity control that changes the pool size alone on one card is
   100	queued behind the re-runs; \S\ref{sec:results} reports it when it lands.
   101	That is the finding we would carry to another system first, and it is why this
   102	paper's answer to its own title is a property of the deployment rather than of
   103	the controller.
   104	
   105	The idle component is structural rather than a control failure. A session in a
   106	tool wait issues no request, so when all live sessions happen to be waiting,
   107	the engine is genuinely empty. The stretches are short in the middle and long
   108	in the tail---mean 20.9\,s---and the tail is what a scheduler feels: 69.9\%
   109	of all idle \emph{time} sits in stretches over 20\,s, an order of magnitude
   110	above the 2\,s control cycle. (The median is not a good summary here: it moves
   111	between 5 and 10\,s depending on which runs are pooled, because one long run
   112	contributes a fifth of the stretches.) Crucially, the tool wait itself is much longer than
   113	the trace's median suggests: the median inter-round gap is 0.35\,s, but the
   114	\emph{mean} is 15.8--16.9\,s with 22\% of gaps over ten seconds. A controller
   115	that
   116	reads only the median concludes, as we initially did, that sessions never
   117	leave and therefore that there is no idle time to recover. There is.
   118	
   119	\subsection{Contributions}
   120	
   121	\begin{itemize}
   122	  \item \textbf{We identify the memory tier as what decides how much of the
   123	        achievable prefix reuse survives added concurrency, and therefore how
   124	        much a controller has to gain by admitting more.} The two senses of
   125	        ``room'' need separating, and \S\ref{sec:xtrace} is what separates
   126	        them: the tier bounds the \emph{local} return on added concurrency
   127	        (busy-time throughput), and it does not by itself decide whether a
   128	        controller wins \emph{end to end}, where idle time and the SLO axis
   129	        also move. Our second workload is the demonstration---it has no host
   130	        tier and its controller runs at low concurrency (1.22), the two
   131	        conditions this section associates with room, and it still does not win
   132	        (\S\ref{sec:xtrace}). The claim here is the local one. The trace
   133	        bounds reuse at
   134	        75.5\% of its prompt tokens, and no configuration we ran reaches that
   135	        bound---the best realizes 92.5\% of it. What the tier decides is how
   136	        much of the bound is realized as concurrency rises: device-only,
   137	        realization falls from 92.5\% to 36.4\% between
   138	        $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00 and 1.84 and the shortfall appears
   139	        as recomputation, 8.6\,M to 20.7\,M tokens; with a host tier it holds
   140	        at 90.2--92.4\% over 1.25 to 1.73, while the \emph{device} misses more
   141	        (\S\ref{sec:model}). Two configurations at the same running count and
   142	        the same pool occupancy realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound, so it
   143	        is not how full the pool is but what happens when it overflows; that
   144	        pair differs in date and GPU as well, so a capacity control that
   145	        varies the pool alone on one card is queued; \S\ref{sec:results}
   146	        reports it when it lands. The per-step elasticity follows the same line:
   147	        it passes one above $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$
   148	        device-only and stays below one across the controllers' whole
   149	        operating range with a host tier (\S\ref{sec:criterion}). Two
   150	        measurements that do not share a code path---client-side step timing
   151	        and engine-side token accounting---give the same answer. This is the
   152	        finding we would carry to another system first, and it is what the
   153	        title's question turns on.
   154	  \item We test four routes by which an adaptive admission controller was
   155	        expected to beat a tuned static cap on this workload, and we are
   156	        precise about what each one rests on. Two are falsified by measurement
   157	        against criteria we fixed before the runs---changing the accounting
   158	        base (\S\ref{sec:results}) and changing the growth reserve. One is a
   159	        knob rather than a hypothesis, and we report it only because it slides
   160	        the operating point along the front and adds no mechanism. The fourth,
   161	        changing the execution granularity from session count to
   162	        running-request count, is falsified by measurement \emph{and} by an
   163	        argument about why the permit mechanism cannot express the control it
   164	        would need. We do not call the last two of these falsifications of a
   165	        stated hypothesis, because they were not.
   166	  \item We give a two-part decomposition of the wall clock (\S\ref{sec:model})
   167	        that locates the cancellation: admission policy moves the
   168	        running-request count, and the per-step cost moves with it. On the
   169	        device-only configurations we can account for that rise token for
   170	        token---evicted prefixes are recomputed almost one for one. On
   171	        host-tier configurations recomputation is flat and the account has to
   172	        come from elsewhere; we report the part of that residual we were able
   173	        to attribute, and the part we were not (\S\ref{sec:model},
   174	        \S\ref{sec:repro}).
   175	  \item We reduce the question to one measurable number, the elasticity of
   176	        per-step cost with respect to the running count (\S\ref{sec:criterion}),
   177	        and measure it at $0.38$ on the model-consistent step cost and $0.61$
   178	        on the per-request one, on the configurations whose mechanism we can
   179	        account for token for token. We offer it as a diagnostic rather than a
   180	        predictor: it says where on the curve a policy operates and how much
   181	        the remaining headroom is worth, and \S\ref{sec:criterion} is explicit
   182	        about the sense in which it is circular and the sense in which it is
   183	        not.
   184	  \item We characterize when adaptivity \emph{could} have paid, and show that
   185	        our workload is not that case: the same throttle configuration that is
   186	        worth $+51\%$ at 25 sessions costs $-42\%$ at 200 (\S\ref{sec:load}).
   187	        Put differently, no single scalar cap is optimal across both, which is
   188	        the regime adaptive control exists for. On the low-load tier, where
   189	        that argument bites hardest, the controller is \emph{not} worse---%
   190	        same-generation measurements put it on the front, $+0.9$\,pp above the
   191	        line and inside the noise. On the high-load tier it is dominated. That
   192	        asymmetry, not a blanket failure, is what this paper measures.
   193	  \item We make the measurements reproducible: the front and each controller's
   194	        signed distance are produced by one script from the raw per-step logs,
   195	        the tables and the sub-thousand-step runs by two others, all named in
   196	        \S\ref{sec:repro}. We report the six claims we had to retract along the
   197	        way, with the checks that caught them.
   198	\end{itemize}

   200	\subsection{What we do not claim}
   201	
   202	We do not claim that admission control is useless in general. We claim it does
   203	not pay \emph{here} and we show the frontier measurement that decides that; the
   204	diagnostic of \S\ref{sec:criterion} is a cheaper check an engineer can run
   205	before committing to a sweep, not a verdict on its own, and it can be taken on another
   206	workload before committing to a sweep. We also do not claim our hardware and model are
   207	representative; our results are from one 24\,GB accelerator and one 7B model,
   208	and extending them is future work.

     4	\subsection{Workload}
     5	
     6	We replay 200 coding-agent sessions drawn from the {\sc TraceLab} corpus
     7	\cite{zhu2026tracelab}. Each session is a
     8	sequence of rounds; a round presents a prompt of median 23{,}187 tokens
     9	(p90 51{,}064; maximum 89{,}436, which alone is 88\% of the KV pool), and
    10	between rounds the session waits on a tool before returning with a longer
    11	context. Arrivals are Poisson at $\lambda=0.04\,\text{s}^{-1}$ over an
    12	80-minute window, giving 999 steps in total. Two sessions of maximum size
    13	cannot be co-resident: their combined contexts (89{,}436 and 87{,}821 tokens)
    14	exceed the pool by 75\%.
    15	
    16	The tool wait is the load's defining property and the one most easily
    17	misread. Its \emph{median} is 0.35\,s, which invites the conclusion that
    18	sessions never leave the engine. Its \emph{mean} is 16.85\,s measured and 15.82\,s
    19	demanded, and the maximum is 300\,s; 21.8\% of the
    20	tool waits the trace specifies exceed ten seconds---18.0\% of the 799 round
    21	transitions, 14.4\% of its 999 steps---and 18.6\% of the measured gaps do. We verified that our replay
    22	harness honors the trace faithfully: across 799 round transitions the measured
    23	gap is 16.85\,s mean against a demanded 15.82\,s. On a typical transition the
    24	harness adds nothing---the median of the per-transition measured/demanded
    25	ratios is 1.001---while the ratio of the two means is 1.065, the difference
    26	sitting in the tail. The corpus's own characterization makes the same point
    27	qualitatively---it reports
    28	``diverse and heavily-tailed tool calls'' and calls these gaps
    29	``human-paced''---but gives no numeric mean \cite{zhu2026tracelab}, so the
    30	16.85\,s above is our measurement over the released trace, not a figure we
    31	quote from that paper.

    33	\subsection{Engine and the binding constraint}
    34	
    35	We use SGLang v0.5.10 \cite{zheng2024sglang} with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct
    36	\cite{qwen2coder} extended to 96K context,
    37	on one RTX 3090 (24\,GB). KV is paged \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}; at \texttt{mem-fraction-static} 0.85
    38	the pool is 101{,}432 tokens. Hierarchical caching (HiCache), with a host tier of twice the device pool, is
    39	enabled on the configurations marked \emph{+HC} and on every \sys{} run; the
    40	five plain static caps run without it. We keep both because the host tier turns
    41	out to change the mechanism (\S\ref{sec:model}). Every run is guarded: if the pool does not
    42	come out at $101{,}432 \pm 20$---which happens when another tenant holds
    43	memory on the card---the run is aborted rather than recorded, because SGLang
    44	sizes the pool from free memory at startup and a shrunken pool silently
    45	produces incomparable numbers.
    46	
    47	The relevant consequence is that the pool holds only about four median
    48	sessions. Concurrency is therefore capacity-bound, not scheduler-bound, and
    49	this is the premise on which everything below rests.

    51	\subsection{The controller under test}
    52	
    53	\sys{} is an admission controller that reads engine telemetry and issues
    54	\emph{named} permits rather than a scalar cap: a session is admitted by
    55	appearing in a permit file, and the runner refuses to start a round for a
    56	session that is not listed. This design choice matters for the comparison,
    57	because it is what allows per-session actions at all---including the pause
    58	primitive we exploit in \S\ref{sec:results:gate}. It also means that a session,
    59	once admitted, holds its permit for its entire lifetime, so the controller
    60	never frees capacity by discarding a session's state---it admits fewer sessions
    61	instead. That is a statement about the \emph{policy}, not about the memory: the
    62	engine's radix cache still evicts parked prefixes under pressure, and we
    63	measure that it does and that they are recomputed (\S\ref{sec:model}). We
    64	return to this distinction in \S\ref{sec:discussion}, because it bounds how far
    65	our negative result generalises.
    66	
    67	Each control cycle (2\,s) the controller computes
    68	\begin{equation}
    69	  \Pi \;=\; \max(\rho, \textstyle\sum_i c_i) \;+\; \textstyle\sum_i g_i ,
    70	  \label{eq:proj}
    71	\end{equation}
    72	where $\rho$ is the engine's reported occupancy, $c_i$ the context length of
    73	live session $i$, and $g_i$ its forecast growth over the next
    74	\texttt{horizon} rounds. A session is admitted when $\Pi + n \le B$, with $n$
    75	the candidate's estimated need and $B$ the budget. The three terms are
    76	respectively the measured physical occupancy, a reservation for sessions
    77	currently parked in a tool wait (whose KV is evictable and therefore absent
    78	from $\rho$), and a reservation for future growth.

    80	\subsection{Baselines and the verdict criterion}
    81	
    82	Baselines are static caps: at most $N$ sessions resident, $N \in \{1,\dots,5\}$,
    83	with and without HiCache. We compare on two axes: end-to-end wall clock
    84	(time from the first submission to the last completion) and SLO attainment
    85	(fraction of requests whose time-to-first-token is under 10\,s).
    86	
    87	The verdict criterion deserves care, because ``faster than cap-$N$'' is not
    88	the question. The question is whether a policy reaches a point \emph{above}
    89	the static front. A point that lies merely between two static caps is not a
    90	win: mixing cap-$N$ and cap-$M$ by time slice approximates any point on the
    91	line between them. We flag this segment argument as an argument, not a
    92	measurement---we did not run the time-sliced policies, and carrying queue state
    93	and cache contents across a switch could bend the line. It is the criterion we
    94	apply, and it is the stricter of the two we could have used---stricter than
    95	comparing each controller against the discrete static points alone, because the
    96	line sits above them. We state the direction plainly rather than claim it as a
    97	caution: this choice makes our negative result \emph{easier} to reach, not
    98	harder. Our best arm is $0.55$\,pp below the line and would be $+4.4$\,pp
    99	against the nearest static anchor below it in wall clock (\texttt{cap3}+HiCache's
   100	repeat, 81.9\% at 230.3 minutes; $+5.2$ would be the comparison against that
   101	anchor's first run at 81.1\% and 236.4 minutes, which is the one it displaced).
   102	The arm we repeated most tightly is a different one---\texttt{horizon}=1, at
   103	$0.93$\,pp below the line and $+3.7$\,pp against the same first run---and we
   104	name both because ``best'' depends on which of the two the reader means. So we take the
   105	upper-left
   106	envelope of the static points and report each controller's signed distance
   107	above or below it. We classify a signed distance of more than $+1.0$\,pp as
   108	above the front, less than $-1.0$\,pp as below it, and the band between the
   109	two as \emph{on} it---a tie, not a win. The band is a convention we fixed
   110	before running anything, and we report it here because every verdict in this
   111	paper depends on it. Our later replicate measurements (\S\ref{sec:results})
   112	bracket it rather than justify it: the SLO spread of configurations we ran
   113	more than once ranges from 0.5\,pp to 5.1\,pp, so for some configurations
   114	the band is generous and for others it is far too narrow. We flag this as a
   115	weakness of the criterion, not a defence of it. One further property of the
   116	envelope deserves flagging, because
   117	three of the nine controller variants finish slower than 295 minutes. Its
   118	right-hand segment runs from \texttt{cap2}+HiCache at 295 minutes to
   119	\texttt{cap1} at 527, and \texttt{cap1} is the only anchor there that runs
   120	\emph{without} HiCache. Those three---\texttt{hz2},
   121	\texttt{breshz1} and \texttt{foyfix}---are therefore judged against an
   122	interpolation between one host-tier point and one device-only point. If the
   123	envelope is drawn from the host-tier anchors alone, that segment does not exist
   124	and those three cannot be adjudicated at all; drawn from device-only anchors
   125	alone, they sit further below. We report the conservative reading and note that
   126	the honest statement about those three is \emph{below the front as we defined
   127	it}, not \emph{below every static configuration}. A fourth arm,
   128	\texttt{bres}, sat in the same position in an earlier version of this
   129	paper; its re-measured value is 265.9 minutes, which puts it back inside the
   130	device-only range, so the class has three members and not four. Two of the
   131	three

     1	\section{A Criterion for When Admission Control Can Pay}
     2	\label{sec:criterion}
     3	
     4	The decomposition turns the
     5	question into one number. Throughput during busy time is
     6	$\overline{R}/\tau$, so raising concurrency by a factor $f$ buys a factor
     7	$f^{1-\epsilon}$, where
     8	\begin{equation}
     9	  \epsilon \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln \tau}{\mathrm{d}\ln \overline{R}}
    10	  \label{eq:elasticity}
    11	\end{equation}
    12	is the elasticity of per-step cost with respect to the running count. At
    13	$\epsilon = 0$ the step cost is flat, concurrency converts one for one into
    14	throughput, and an admission controller has room to work. At $\epsilon = 1$
    15	the two cancel exactly and it has none. Everything in between is a
    16	quantitative statement about how much room.
    17	
    18	On the five device-only configurations, where \S\ref{sec:model} establishes
    19	that the step cost is driven by recomputation, $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$
    20	runs from 1.00 to 1.84. A least-squares fit of Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} over
    21	those five points gives $\epsilon = 0.61$ on the per-request measurement
    22	($R^2 = 0.89$), or $0.38$ on the model-consistent one (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).
    23	
    24	That fit is a straight line through a curve, and the curve is what matters. The
    25	segment elasticities are $\epsilon = -0.21$ from \texttt{cap1} to
    26	\texttt{cap2}, $+0.43$ from \texttt{cap2} to \texttt{cap3}, $+1.15$ from
    27	\texttt{cap3} to \texttt{cap4}, and $+1.14$ from \texttt{cap4} to
    28	\texttt{cap5}. Below $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$ added
    29	concurrency still buys something; above it, $\epsilon$ passes one, which means
    30	the engine gets \emph{slower} in wall clock as more requests run. The single
    31	number $0.38$ averages those regimes and describes none of them.
    32	
    33	The operating region is the one that matters, and it is not the region the
    34	controllers run in. All nine controller arms run with HiCache enabled, which
    35	the five device-only caps above do not. Measuring the same elasticity on the
    36	three HiCache configurations we have gives $\epsilon = +0.19$ from
    37	\texttt{cap2}+HC to \texttt{cap3}+HC and $+0.46$ from \texttt{cap3}+HC to
    38	\texttt{cap4}+HC, over $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.25 to 1.73. That range
    39	covers the whole controller operating region (1.35 to 1.69), and over it
    40	$\epsilon$ never approaches one. At matched running counts the HiCache caps pay
    41	$1.7$\,ms less per step at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$.
    42	
    43	This contradicts what an earlier draft of this paper claimed, and it changes
    44	what the criterion is for. The cliff in the device-only curve is a
    45	recomputation effect: \S\ref{sec:model} measures that those runs recompute what
    46	they evict, while under HiCache the host tier absorbs it. The cliff does not
    47	survive on the configuration our controllers actually use. So the criterion
    48	does \emph{not} explain why they lose---on their own configuration they were
    49	not in a region where added concurrency was self-defeating, and the engine had
    50	room they did not take. What the measurement does is rule one explanation out.
    51	The failure is not that this workload denies admission control its lever; it is
    52	that our controllers do not pull the lever they have, which is what
    53	\S\ref{sec:results:idle-memory} and the attribution of
    54	\S\ref{sec:results:attribution} measure directly.
    55	
    56	We are explicit about what this does and does not establish. The HiCache curve
    57	rests on three configurations and two segments, one run each, with no
    58	error bars, and it stops at 1.73---we cannot see whether a cliff appears
    59	beyond the controllers' region. A $\pm10\%$ perturbation of $\tau$ puts the
    60	segment elasticities' bounds across one even on the device-only curve, so the
    61	cliff is a description of the fitted points rather than a threshold we have
    62	located precisely. And it is measured on
    63	different policies rather than by an intervention, so it is a
    64	cross-policy association---the two configurations the tier comparison rests on
    65	were run a day apart on different cards, which \S\ref{sec:model} states, and a
    66	same-card pairing is queued. On our data it is also computed from the same wall
    67	clock the model then explains---$\tau_1$ is derived from $W_{\text{busy}}$, so
    68	using it to predict $W_{\text{busy}}$ restates rather than tests---and its
    69	points are different policies, so the slope is a cross-policy association, not
    70	a transportable causal derivative. $\tau_2$ is genuinely independent of the
    71	wall clock but measures only decode, so it cannot be dropped into a throughput
    72	equation that includes prefill.
    73	
    74	We tested whether the curve transfers to a second workload. On a real agent
    75	trace (117 sessions, median inter-round
    76	waits of 20.2\,s against 0.34\,s on the synthetic trace,
    77	\texttt{toolwait.csv}, six static caps and one controller arm) the
    78	model-consistent elasticity is negative over the first two segments---$-2.40$
    79	then $-0.28$, at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.08 to 1.29---and then turns
    80	positive above 1.29 ($+1.0$, $+0.18$, $+0.87$ over the three segments that
    81	reach 1.50). The $\varepsilon > 1$ regime is touched and not held there: the
    82	first of those three segments is the only one at or above one, and a $\pm10\%$
    83	perturbation of $\tau_1$ carries every one of their bounds across both zero and
    84	one. The main trace, for contrast, crosses one at 1.53 with $+1.15$ and stays
    85	above it. So the direction transfers and the location does not, and on this
    86	trace we can say no more than that added concurrency stopped paying. The
    87	per-request measure is no steadier on
    88	that trace ($+0.41$, $+0.48$, $+0.45$, $-1.25$, $+0.73$). The controller arm
    89	there runs at 1.22, below every one of the nine arms above and below where the
    90	branch turns expensive on either trace, and still does not beat the static
    91	front at the threshold this paper uses---though at a stricter one it does
    92	(\S\ref{sec:xtrace}). We report this as a boundary:
    93	the criterion identifies a branch, and where we could check it elsewhere the
    94	sign of the branch transferred while its location did not; it never explained
    95	the failure. It is not, as an earlier draft claimed, something that decides the
    96	question in minutes before building anything.

     4	\subsection{Two components}
     5	
     6	We split the wall clock at every sampled instant according to whether the
     7	engine has any request in flight:
     8	\begin{equation}
     9	  W \;=\; W_{\text{busy}} \;+\; W_{\text{idle}} .
    10	  \label{eq:split}
    11	\end{equation}
    12	Fig.~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the split for the twenty-eight runs of
    13	Table~\ref{tab:runs}'s families that we have per-step logs for. Two things are
    14	immediately visible and both matter later.
    15	
    16	First, idle time is not noise. Across the runs it spans 7.3\% to 41.9\% of
    17	the sampled span---7.3\% for the fastest configuration, 36.3\% for the
    18	slowest, and 41.9\% for the one that idles most (all-throttles-off). The
    19	denominator here
    20	is the span the sampler observed, which runs about six minutes longer than the
    21	request wall clock of Table~\ref{tab:runs}, so the fractions against that
    22	wall clock are 7.6\% and 36.7\%---and it is not spread evenly.
    23	Over 1{,}844 stretches the mean is 20.9\,s and 17.5\% of them exceed
    24	20\,s, accounting for 69.9\% of all idle \emph{time}
    25	(Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). The tail, not the middle, is what a scheduler feels,
    26	and it sits at the scale of the 15.8--16.9\,s mean tool wait (demanded and
    27	measured; \texttt{toolwait.csv}). We report the mean and the share rather
    28	than the median here because the median is not robust to which runs enter the
    29	pool: it is 10.0\,s without \texttt{cap1} and 5.0--5.5\,s with it, since
    30	that one run contributes 411 of the 1{,}844 stretches and is itself the
    31	longest. The mean (18.8--20.9\,s) and the over-20\,s share of idle time
    32	(64.6--70.1\%) move by little across the four poolings we report in
    33	\texttt{idle\_pooled.csv}, so the claim that the tail dominates does not
    34	depend on the choice. The 90th percentile moves with the median
    35	(25.1--30.1\,s) and we give it as a range for the same reason.
    36	
    37	Second, the two components move independently and in opposite directions, so
    38	neither is a proxy for the wall clock. The no-control baseline
    39	(\S\ref{sec:results}) cuts idle time by 13.8 minutes against the base
    40	configuration---it admits everything, so nothing queues---and finishes only
    41	2.7 minutes sooner, because its busy time grows by 11.1 minutes; the trade
    42	costs it the SLO column outright, 89.8\% down to 3.0\%. The arm that disables
    43	every throttle moves the other way, and further: idle \emph{rises} by 129
    44	minutes while busy \emph{falls} by 5.4, so the 124 minutes it adds to the wall
    45	clock are almost entirely waiting, bought with 43.7\,pp of SLO. Any account
    46	that treats ``more concurrency'' as one thing will get this wrong in one
    47	direction or the other.
    48	
    49	Neither example is one of the damaged runs of \S\ref{sec:repro}; both are
    50	clean in the co-tenancy audit. Neither is a member of the controller family
    51	whose clean step-cost range that section quotes, either---the baseline is not
    52	a controller at all, and its 39.3\,ms comes with the highest running count we
    53	measure (1.91), while the throttle-free arm runs a different controller. The
    54	charge-free base arm is a third case, whose split we give in
    55	\S\ref{sec:results} with the re-measured numbers.

    84	\subsection{What determines busy time}
    85	
    86	During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
    87	running request. Hence
    88	\begin{equation}
    89	  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
    90	  \label{eq:busy}
    91	\end{equation}
    92	with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and
    93	$\tau$ the per-step cost. Across our runs $\tau$ is not a fit parameter we
    94	tuned; we obtain it two independent ways, and reporting both is what shows
    95	they are measuring different things rather than confirming each other.
    96	
    97	\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} /
    98	G$. Two choices in that expression are easy to get wrong and both matter. The
    99	time term must be the \emph{busy} component, not the wall clock---using the
   100	wall clock charges idle time to the steps. And $\overline{R}$ must be the mean
   101	running count \emph{during busy time}, not the mean over the whole run: the
   102	whole-run mean includes the idle samples, where the count is zero, so it equals
   103	$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \times$ (busy fraction). Using it makes $\tau_1$
   104	too low by exactly the busy fraction---so the estimate is short by the
   105	\emph{idle} fraction, 36.3\% for \texttt{cap1}, whose busy share is 63.7\%. We report both columns in the data, and use the
   106	busy-conditional mean throughout; the whole-run mean is what an earlier
   107	revision of this paper used, and it made the two methods agree far better than
   108	they do.
   109	
   110	\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} For each step record we compute the
   111	per-request decode interval,
   112	$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$,
   113	and take the median over steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$.
   114	
   115	The two do not agree exactly, and the disagreement is informative rather than
   116	embarrassing. $\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every run we accept, by 7.7--39.7\%
   117	(\texttt{decomp.csv}, column \texttt{excess\_pct}), which in
   118	absolute terms is 2.6--8.2\,ms of a 27--37\,ms step. (The one run we exclude
   119	from the reuse accounting, the \texttt{horizon}=1 repeat, reads 66.3\% here
   120	against its original's 18.5\%. The anomaly sits in the engine's token counters
   121	and in everything derived from them, so that is the scope of the exclusion:
   122	this run's $\tau$ columns, its reuse and recomputation are unusable, while its
   123	wall clock and SLO---which come from the client---are ones we use, and it
   124	appears in Table~\ref{tab:runs} and in the repeat spread of
   125	\S\ref{sec:results} on those two columns alone.) The gap is
   126	\emph{consistent with} engine steps that are prefill and
   127	therefore emit no output token---we can bound it, not identify it, because
   128	subtracting a median from a mean does not isolate a component. All we can form
   129	from these two is their ratio: the excess $\tau_1/\tau_2 - 1$ runs
   130	17.7--39.7\% across the five device-only configurations. Two things follow, and
   131	we separate them. First, it is a ratio of \emph{times}, not of steps, and we
   132	have no measurement of the fraction of step \emph{counts} that are prefill---
   133	chunked prefill makes those two different numbers. Second, even as a time
   134	ratio it is the gap between two measurements, not a decomposition of one: it
   135	is consistent with prefill and non-decode work filling it, but we cannot rule
   136	out that some of the difference is the two estimators disagreeing for other
   137	reasons. So the two are not
   138	two estimates of one number; they are measurements of two different things,
   139	and we do not size a component with their difference. Fig.~\ref{fig:steptime} shows both, run by run. Method 2 remains the one that does not depend on the
   140	quantity it is used to explain, and it is the one we quote when the question
   141	is the cost of a decode step. The engine's own latency counters
   142	(\texttt{itl\_p50}, \texttt{e2e\_p50}, \texttt{ttft\_p50}) are exported as zero
   143	by SGLang v0.5.10 in our configuration, so no directly measured step time
   144	exists in our data; instrumenting this is future work.

   161	\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}
   162	
   163	Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is
   164	not. Across the thirteen runs we fitted---the five device-only caps, the three
   165	host-tier caps that span the same range, and the five controller arms we report
   166	individually---$\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and $\tau_2$
   167	from 20.6 to 33.1\,ms, and both rise with the running count: fitting
   168	Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
   169	$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$
   170	($R^2 = 0.89$ for the latter, Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).
   171	
   172	The relation is a trend, not a law. Two host-tier configurations with similar
   173	running counts behave differently: \texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at
   174	29.8\,ms while \texttt{F:hz0} runs 1.66 at 35.7\,ms. Running count sets a
   175	floor and a trend, not a value---and on the host-tier family, as we show below,
   176	it does not set the value at all.
   177	
   178	This is the crux of the paper, and we state it at the strength the arithmetic
   179	supports. Hold $\tau_1$ at its value for \texttt{cap1}, 28.8\,ms, and busy
   180	time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from 1.00 to 1.84
   181	should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to 184. The
   182	measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes back 50 of
   183	the 156 minutes that the extra concurrency would otherwise have bought---\emph{a
   184	third} of the gain, not all of it and, as an earlier revision of this paper
   185	claimed, not more than half. The other two thirds are real: raising
   186	concurrency does make the engine faster. What it does not do is make it faster
   187	in proportion, and the shortfall compounds with every controller that tries to
   188	exploit the difference.
   189	
   190	\paragraph{What the engine is doing when it is not decoding.} SGLang exposes
   191	two counters that answer this directly: tokens evicted from the radix cache,
   192	and prompt tokens that had to be prefilled rather than reused. Across the five
   193	static caps \emph{without} HiCache the two track each other almost exactly.
   194	\texttt{cap1} evicts 9.2\,M tokens and prefills 8.6\,M uncached;
   195	\texttt{cap5} evicts 21.3\,M and prefills 20.7\,M; the gap is 0.6--0.7\,M on
   196	every one of the five. An evicted prefix is recomputed, close to one token for
   197	one, and that recomputation is chunked into decode steps. Those same five runs
   198	give recomputation rising with $\tau$ across four of the five points: 9.2\,M
   199	uncached tokens at 27.4\,ms, 11.5 at 29.7, 15.4 at 32.4, 20.7 at 36.7. The
   200	exception is the first point, \texttt{cap1}, which recomputes least (8.6\,M)
   201	and still costs 28.8\,ms---more than \texttt{cap2} does for more recomputation.
   202	We report the ordering we have rather than rounding it to a monotone one: it is
   203	a mechanism consistent with the $\tau$ trend on four of the five device-only
   204	configurations, and one point it does not order. It is five points on five
   205	different policies, not an
   206	intervention: we did not hold concurrency fixed and vary eviction, so we call
   207	it consistent rather than demonstrated.

   228	\paragraph{Where the mechanism stops.} With HiCache enabled the
   229	correspondence breaks---and it breaks in a way that confirms the mechanism
   230	rather than undermining it. The device cache misses as much as before:
   231	\texttt{cap4\_hc} misses 22.4\,M tokens on the device, more than the
   232	device-only \texttt{cap4} misses. But only 9.1\,M of that is recomputed; the
   233	other 13.3\,M is served from the host tier. Across the nineteen host-tier
   234	runs the host serves 9.5--19.3\,M tokens each, and across the five device-only
   235	runs it serves almost nothing---0.006\% to 0.25\% of the device miss, the rest
   236	of which is recomputed on all five. Recomputation is still what the device cannot
   237	absorb; the host tier is a second absorber in front of it.

   239	\subsection{The tier, not the policy, decides whether reuse survives}
   240	
   241	The device-miss column hides the quantity that matters. Define
   242	\emph{reuse} as the prompt tokens served from anywhere in the memory
   243	hierarchy---device radix cache plus host tier---so that
   244	$\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$, with the prompt fixed at
   245	28.5\,M by the trace.
   246	
   247	The bound on reuse has to be defined independently of what we observed,
   248	or the argument is circular. We take it from the trace: summing the planned
   249	reusable prefix (\texttt{prefix\_len}) over all 999 rounds of
   250	\texttt{replay\_pois200\_l04.csv} gives 21{,}540{,}371 tokens against a prompt
   251	total of 28{,}526{,}871, so no policy can reuse more than 75.5\% of the
   252	prompt. That is a property of the workload. It is also a bound rather than a
   253	description: \emph{no configuration we ran reaches it}, and the best realizes
   254	92.5\%, so the gap between ``planned'' and ``achieved'' exists even in the
   255	easiest case. What varies across configurations is how much of the bound is
   256	realized, and that is where the tier enters.

   313	\texttt{cap3\_hc} run at nearly the same mean device occupancy (55.8\% and
   314	55.5\%) and nearly the same running count (1.53 and 1.51), and they realize
   315	79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound respectively. What differs is not how full the
   316	device pool is but what happens when it overflows: with no host tier an
   317	evicted prefix is gone and must be recomputed; with one it is demoted and read
   318	back. The pool is the binding resource; the tier sets the pool; and in these two
   319	runs, which differ in the tier and in nothing we can see on the engine side,
   320	the tier is what separates paying for added concurrency in recomputation from
   321	not paying for it. The
   322	caveat is that this pair is two runs on two different days and two different
   323	GPUs, not a paired experiment; \S\ref{sec:repro} states what we did to rule
   324	out co-tenancy. A capacity-controlled version---same card, same trace, only
   325	the pool size changed---is queued behind the re-runs and we report it in
   326	\S\ref{sec:results} when it lands; we do not use it here.
   327	
   328	\paragraph{The second trace agrees on direction and not on level.}
   329	We ran the same accounting on a second workload, reported in full in
   330	\S\ref{sec:xtrace}---117 real Claude agent sessions, 390 rounds, the same pool
   331	and the same engine---and it reproduces the direction: its realization of \emph{its own} bound falls from 74.8\% to 57.5\%
   332	as the running count rises from 1.08 to 1.50, and over that span the whole-range
   333	per-unit slope is $-40.9$ points, against the device-only family's $-66.8$ over
   334	$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.00--1.84 (\texttt{slope.csv}). What it does not reproduce is the level, and this is
   335	worth stating because it bounds what we can claim. The lightest cap on that
   336	trace (\texttt{realhr\_cap2}) runs at a \emph{lower} median device occupancy
   337	than the lightest on this one (15.8\% against 22.4\%, \texttt{cap1}) and
   338	realizes \emph{less} (74.8\% against 92.5\%), so realization is not a function
   339	of occupancy alone---and a
   340	statement of the form ``the pool collapses above $x$\% occupancy'' is wrong on
   341	our own data. Occupancy is instantaneous while reuse depends on history; a
   342	session's prefix must survive from one round to the next, and how much
   343	pressure that puts on the pool is not visible in what the pool holds at an
   344	instant. We do not have the measurement that would separate those, which is
   345	why the controlled experiment now queued for \S\ref{sec:results}---it varies
   346	the pool alone, on one card and one trace---is the one that will settle it, and
   347	why we do not lean on the cross-trace comparison here. The claim this section supports is the narrow one: within a
   348	configuration, the host tier preserves realization at a matched running count
   349	and a matched occupancy, and without it realization falls.
   350	
   351	That gives the criterion of \S\ref{sec:criterion} a mechanism rather than a
   352	correlation, and it suggests a prediction we are testing:
   353	if the tier acts by multiplying usable capacity (the host pool is twice the
   354	device pool, so three times in total), then shrinking the device pool alone
   355	should move the collapse point even with no host tier involved. We do not
   356	treat the arithmetic as a result---it assumes KV demand grows linearly in the
   357	running count, which heterogeneous sessions need not satisfy---and the
   358	queued experiment measures the collapse point directly rather than predicting
   359	it, and \S\ref{sec:results} reports it once it has run.
   360	
   361	\paragraph{What is left unexplained.} Less than an earlier draft of this
   362	paragraph claimed. Across the nineteen host-tier runs recomputation spans
   363	8.6--12.8\,M tokens and realization 72.8--92.5\%, and $\tau_1$ spans
   364	27.0--36.2\,ms. Three of those runs
   365	(\texttt{F:hz2} 33.6, \texttt{F:hz0} 35.7,
   366	\texttt{F:breshz1} 36.2\,ms) were
   367	measured while three other engine instances shared the host, which inflates
   368	per-step cost by 24--41\% and leaves the volume columns untouched
   369	(\S\ref{sec:repro}); they are being re-measured and we do not use their
   370	timing. A fourth was re-measured clean and is in the set above with its new
   371	value. Across the remaining sixteen the $\tau_1$ spread does not sit
   372	unexplained at all: it tracks their recomputation closely (a straight line
   373	through the sixteen leaves every run within $1.5$\,ms, $r = +0.92$), which is
   374	the mechanism of this section holding inside the host tier rather than a
   375	residual. We report the association and not the line: the coefficients are
   376	fitted to one configuration family and we have no derivation for them. We note that the negative
   377	result does not rest on it either way: the cancellation is measured directly,
   378	not inferred from the mechanism.
   379	
   380	One caution about the strength of the account above, since the arithmetic
   381	invites overreading. $\text{reuse} + \text{recomputed} = \text{prompt}$ is a
   382	definition, not a finding, so ``the shortfall reappears as recomputation''
   383	follows from how we partitioned the prompt and cannot by itself establish the
   384	mechanism. What is independent of the partition is the pair of engine
   385	counters: evicted tokens and uncached prefill tokens are separate SGLang
   386	counters, and on the device-only runs they track within 0.6--0.7\,M on all
   387	five---which is the observation that licenses reading eviction as the cause
   388	of recomputation. With a host tier the two come apart by design, and the
   389	1.3--8.6\,M gap between them is an upper bound on what the host absorbed out
   390	of the evicted pool: those tokens were evicted and not recomputed, which is
   391	consistent with the host having served them and equally consistent with their
   392	never having been asked for again. We can bound it, not identify it
   393	(\texttt{eviction.csv}); the host serves 9.5--19.3\,M in total, so most of what
   394	it serves are prefixes that were still resident on the device when the
   395	session came back for them.
   396	
   397	\paragraph{A refutation we had to withdraw.} An earlier revision withdrew this
   398	mechanism outright, on two checks that were both wrong. The first was that
   399	total prefilled tokens equalled the trace's planned prefill to a ratio of 1.00,
   400	so no token could have been computed twice. \texttt{prompt\_tokens\_total}
   401	counts the full submitted prompt \emph{including} its cached portion, so it
   402	equals the sum of prompt lengths by construction and cannot detect
   403	recomputation; the quantity that can is the uncached count used above, which
   404	varies by 2.4$\times$ across these runs. The second was that SGLang reported
   405	no retractions. Radix-cache eviction is not a retraction---retractions count
   406	running requests preempted mid-flight, a different event with its own counter.
   407	We record the episode because it is the same failure mode as the segment
   408	argument of \S\ref{sec:bg}: a check that looks like a measurement but is an
   409	identity.

   411	\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}
   412	
   413	The length of an individual idle stretch is a property of the workload, not of
   414	the controller. A session in a
   415	tool wait holds its permit and its KV but issues no request; with only about
   416	two live sessions, the probability that all of them are simultaneously waiting
   417	is substantial. The direct evidence is the duration of the stretches
   418	themselves: they are of the same order as the tool waits, and an order of
   419	magnitude longer than the 2\,s control cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). No
   420	controller can shorten a gap that the tool set.
   421	
   422	How much idle a \emph{run} accumulates is a different matter, and there the
   423	controller is not off the hook. Idle accumulates when every live session
   424	happens to be parked at once, and how many sessions are live is precisely what
   425	an admission policy sets. Two configurations with the same mean running count
   426	make this visible: \texttt{horizon}=1 (1.52) and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache (1.51)
   427	idle 36.7 and 24.1 minutes respectively---a 52\% difference at matched
   428	concurrency---and 12.6 of the 19.4-minute wall-clock gap between them is that
   429	idle difference rather than step cost. So the gap durations are the workload's,
   430	and the number of gaps is partly the policy's.
   431	
   432	We note one claim we had to drop here. An earlier draft reported that idle
   433	fraction falls monotonically with mean concurrency. With the full data it does
   434	not: on the busy-conditional measure this section uses throughout,
   435	\texttt{F:hz0} at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.67 idles 15.2\% while
   436	\texttt{cap4} at 1.65 idles 10.4\%---more concurrency and \emph{more} idle,
   437	the reverse of the reported trend. (The two arms read 1.41 and 1.48 on the
   438	all-samples mean, where the ordering happens to run the other way;
   439	\texttt{arm\_running.csv} carries both conventions side by side, and we name
   440	the one each claim uses.) The weaker statement that survives is the one we use---%
   441	the stretches are structural and long.
   442	
   443	The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always
   444	ready---is what \texttt{bres} does, and the clean measurement of it says the
   445	remedy works and does not pay. \texttt{bres} cuts idle time by 9.5 minutes
   446	against its own baseline, and its busy time falls as well, by 15.5---so the
   447	arm simply finishes 25.4 minutes earlier. What it does not do is improve the
   448	criterion: its SLO falls 3.9\,pp and its distance below the static front is
   449	unchanged (\S\ref{sec:results}). Recovering idle time is not the same act as
   450	buying a better operating point, and here the one does not produce the other.
   451	We are reporting a correction in this paragraph, not just a refinement: an
   452	earlier draft had idle falling while busy rose, leaving the run slower, and
   453	that shape came from a measurement we have since found was taken under host
   454	co-tenancy (\S\ref{sec:repro}). Idle is recoverable; it is recoverable at a
   455	price that does not move the verdict.
   456	The structural remedy would be to make the engine do work during a wait, but
   457	there is no work to do: the session's next round does not exist until its tool
   458	returns.

   180	\subsection{The controllers leave memory unused}
   181	\label{sec:results:idle-memory}
   182	
   183	Before the routes, one measurement that reframes all of them. Our controller
   184	keeps every session's permit for its lifetime, so its pool occupancy is a
   185	policy output, not a constraint. It runs at a median of 49.2\% of the pool on
   186	the base configuration, and the median number of sessions queueing for
   187	admission is 66. The configuration that finishes fastest---the repeat of that same static cap
   188	of four, at 209.1 minutes---runs at 71.9\%, and its first run at 70.2\%. Occupancy
   189	and running requests track each other reliably across the arms we have (the two
   190	columns in \texttt{occupancy.csv} rank the twenty-three arms we have almost
   191	identically, Spearman $\rho=0.95$), but
   192	running count does \emph{not} order wall clock: \texttt{horizon}=2 runs 1.25
   193	requests against the base arm's 1.14 (the all-samples mean, the column
   194	\texttt{occupancy.csv} carries; \S\ref{sec:model} uses the busy-conditional
   195	one) and finishes 15 minutes slower. That gap is
   196	what the next subsection is about.
   197	
   198	The controller believes it is nearly full. Its own decision log records the
   199	projection it admits against---the largest of measured occupancy and the summed
   200	contexts of live sessions, plus a reserve for their growth---at a median of
   201	89{,}073 tokens against a budget of 96{,}360, i.e.\ 7.6\% free. Its measured
   202	occupancy at the same instants is 49{,}875 tokens: 48.2\% of that budget, or
   203	50.8\% of the pool, is idle. The
   204	projection is 1.79$\times$ the truth on this configuration. Across the
   205	sixteen controller runs that finished on this trace and logged projections the
   206	ratio runs from 1.32 to 1.90 with a median of 1.78, and from 1.32 to 4.11 with
   207	the same median once the second trace's controller arm is included
   208	(\texttt{projection.csv} records each set, its size and its median; the second
   209	trace is the 4.11). So 1.79 is this arm's value, not a constant of the design,
   210	and we name the set because the range depends on it---over every finished run in
   211	that file, including the flash-crowd and 25-session families, it is 1.01 to
   212	4.11 with a median of 1.58. (Runs still in flight are excluded from both
   213	figures: their controller logs are still growing, and one of them reads 1.95
   214	today.) Medians here are
   215	the upper of the two middle values, the convention \S\ref{sec:results:attribution}
   216	uses throughout. Two terms produce the gap: charging every
   217	live session its full context, when a parked session's KV is evictable and
   218	therefore absent from the engine's count, and the growth reserve, which alone
   219	is 26{,}762 tokens, 28\% of the budget.
   220	
   221	Two things follow, and we are careful about the second.
   222	
   223	The first is that the controllers under-use the pool, and that this is a
   224	property of their estimates rather than of the hardware: the base
   225	configuration holds nearly half the pool idle (48.2\% of its budget, 50.8\% of\
   226	the pool) with 66 sessions queued, because
   227	it believes it has 7.6\% free when it has 48.2\%. Any account of why it is slow
   228	that does not mention this is incomplete.
   229	
   230	We then asked what actually stops it, by instrumenting the admission decision.
   231	The path is a conjunction: a session is authorized only if the SLO valve is
   232	open, the round gate is not full, and its projected footprint fits what remains
   233	of the budget. Over the base run's 8{,}196 blocked cycles---cycles with a
   234	session waiting and none admitted---the valve accounts for 6.8\%, the round gate
   235	for none, and a projection already above budget for 38.4\%.
   236	
   237	The remaining 54.8\% are cycles where the controller selected no candidate at
   238	all, and here we are explicit about the limit of what we can resolve. The
   239	controller is single-flight by default: it authorizes at most one session per
   240	two-second cycle and waits for that session to begin a round before authorizing
   241	the next. Whether that gate held or whether a candidate was tried and did not
   242	fit is \emph{not distinguishable from our logs}---the controller does not record
   243	it---so we do not attribute the bucket to either. We bounded it by running that arm: the same configuration with single-flight
   244	disabled. Two things came out of it, and the second is the one that matters.
   245	
   246	The bound first. With the gate off, the residual bucket---which can then only
   247	be \emph{candidates that did not fit}, since the gate cannot be holding---falls
   248	from 54.8\% of blocked cycles to 39.7\%, against 44.0\% that are over budget
   249	outright. So the single-flight path accounts for roughly fifteen points, and
   250	the headroom estimate remains the largest single cause. (Both arms' numbers
   251	are rows of \texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, which now includes the \texttt{sf=0}
   252	arm; an earlier revision quoted 39.9 and 43.5 for the second pair, values that
   253	appeared in no data file.)
   254	We report what did \emph{not} move, because it is the check we wrote down for
   255	this probe before running it. The two aggregate indicators barely did:
   256	admitting cycles go from 2.35\% to 2.44\% and blocked cycles from 93.3\% to
   257	93.2\%, so on those two the flag looks like it changed nothing. What moves is
   258	the composition \emph{within} the blocked cycles---which is the quantity the
   259	bound above rests on---and it moves well outside the replicate spread, the
   260	repeat of the base arm reading 58.3\% remainder against the original's 54.8\%.
   261	So the probe supports the decomposition and not the claim that disabling the
   262	gate makes the controller admit appreciably more, which is what we say above
   263	it buys 28 minutes for 3.5\,pp rather than a win.
   264	
   265	The second thing is that disabling it does not make the controller win.
   266	\texttt{sf=0} finishes in 263.3 minutes at 86.3\% SLO against the base run's
   267	291.3 and 89.8\%: it buys 28 minutes for 3.5\,pp, and its signed distance from
   268	the front moves from $-1.24$ to $-0.55$---onto the tie band from below it, not
   269	above it. Its admission rate is essentially unchanged (2.44\% of cycles admit, against
   270	2.35\%). So the gate was a real cost and removing it moves the arm along the
   271	front rather than past it, which is the same trade every other route in this
   272	section makes.
   273	
   274	Across the eight \sys{} variants that left controller logs with gate state---the
   275	set is named in \texttt{gate\_attrib.csv}, and it is eight rather than nine
   276	because the ninth, \texttt{sf=0}, is the probe we report separately just above,
   277	and because \texttt{foyfix} and the two AIMD arms run a different controller and
   278	log no gate state---the projection is over budget in a median 32.2\% of blocked
   279	cycles (19.5\% to 53.6\%) and the valve in a median 32.7\% (6.8\% to 67.9\%).
   280	Both medians move with the set, which is why we name it: over every completed
   281	run in that file---forty-two of them---they are 30.6\% and 5.3\%. One
   282	convention note, because it moves
   283	these numbers: we take the median as the upper of the two middle values when
   284	the count is even (\texttt{v[len(v)//2]}, which is what
   285	\texttt{gen\_gate\_attrib.py} computes and what the file's
   286	\texttt{eight\_median} line records), not the mean of the two; on eight arms
   287	that difference is 0.4\,pp on the projection and 6.7\,pp on the valve. The round gate is
   288	off by default and binds in only two of the three arms that turn it
   289	on---\texttt{rg2} at 29.3\% and \texttt{rg3} at 18.0\% of blocked cycles,
   290	while \texttt{rg4}, the loosest of the three, stays at 0.6\% like every arm
   291	that leaves it off---so the gate
   292	binds only where it is set tightly enough to.
   293	We also record what this accounting omits, because it changes the picture: the
   294	controller has a starvation guard that bypasses the budget entirely once the
   295	oldest waiter has waited 240\,s, cooling down for 300\,s. On the base run it
   296	accounts for 24.8\% of all admissions (51 of 206), and while the projection is
   297	over budget it is the \emph{only} way in---of 3{,}700 such cycles, nine admitted,
   298	and all nine were forced. It is therefore the guard, not the budget test, that
   299	sets the floor on how fast this controller can admit.
   300	
   301	The second is that under-use does \emph{not} order the outcomes, so it is not
   302	the whole explanation either. \texttt{horizon}=1 and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache sit
   303	at the same median occupancy (57.8\% of the pool) and the same mean running
   304	count (1.52 against 1.51 on the busy-time measure of \S\ref{sec:model}), and
   305	the cap is 19 minutes faster. \texttt{hz0} runs
   306	at 64.1\%, higher than that cap, and is 54 minutes slower. Occupancy buys
   307	running requests reliably---\texttt{occupancy.csv}'s occupancy and
   308	running-count columns track
   309	each other across all twenty-three arms---but converting running requests into wall
   310	clock is not simply the per-step cost of \S\ref{sec:model}. Decomposing that
   311	19 minutes into its idle and busy components, 12.6 of them are idle time---%
   312	65\%---and only 6.8 are busy; the two runs' step costs differ by 1.2\,ms
   313	(29.2 against 28.0), about 4\%. A parked session is one the controller has
   314	authorized but not yet released, so the same policy that sets the running count
   315	also sets a large part of the idle time. Both
   316	effects are real; on this workload the unused-memory one is larger for the arms
   317	that finish worst, and the step-cost one is what separates the arms that finish
   318	close together.

   400	\subsection{Route 1: the accounting base}
   401	
   402	Our controller's projection (Eq.~\ref{eq:proj}) charges a session for its full
   403	context even while it is parked in a tool wait, when its KV is evictable and
   404	therefore absent from the engine's reported occupancy. We argued this was a
   405	double charge and removed it, admitting on engine telemetry alone.
   406	
   407	The charge is not double, and removing it does what the argument predicted on
   408	the engine side: median occupancy rises from 49.2\% to 56.2\% of the pool and
   409	the busy-hour running count from 1.35 to 1.48, and the round-1+ prefix hit
   410	rate falls only from 80.6\% to 79.7\%, so no eviction spiral occurred. The run
   411	also finishes 25.4 minutes \emph{earlier} (265.9 against 291.3).
   412	
   413	It is nonetheless not a win. The SLO falls from 89.8\% to 85.9\%---3.90\,pp---
   414	and the static front itself falls 3.81\,pp over those same 25.4 minutes, so the
   415	arm keeps its place relative to the envelope to within 0.09\,pp while moving
   416	25 minutes along it: it slides, at the front's own slope, rather than
   417	crossing. What makes that a test rather than a coincidence is what it would
   418	have taken to cross: at 265.9 minutes the front sits at 87.2\%, so the arm
   419	would have had to give up no more than 2.6\,pp of SLO instead of 3.9, and it
   420	did not.
   421	
   422	We are careful not to rest anything on the 0.09\,pp. It is not a resolution
   423	claim, and the base configuration's own four runs show why: against this same
   424	front they sit at $-1.24$, $-1.73$, $-1.49$ and $+0.18$\,pp, a 1.9\,pp spread.
   425	That is the scale this statistic moves on, and it means the charge-free arm's
   426	distance from any \emph{one} of those runs is not resolvable---measured
   427	against the second of them it is 0.40\,pp \emph{better}, against the fourth
   428	1.5\,pp worse. The slope comparison is the part that survives, and it is the
   429	part the conclusion rests on.
   430	
   431	We want to be explicit that this is a revision. Our earlier measurement of this
   432	route had the arm 4.2 minutes \emph{slower} and 6.0\,pp worse, and we read the
   433	extra concurrency and the extra step cost---$\overline{R}$ up 1.35 to 1.48,
   434	$\tau_2$ up 23.8 to 31.3\,ms---as cancelling. That measurement was one of the
   435	co-tenancy-flagged runs (\S\ref{sec:repro}), and its $\tau_2$ was the inflated
   436	term: measured alone the same arm reads 24.8\,ms, so the second factor is
   437	$+$4.2\% rather than $+$31.5\%, and busy-time throughput
   438	($\overline{R}/\tau$) rises 5.1\% where the flagged measurement had it
   439	falling 17\%. Nothing cancels. The occupancy half of the argument
   440	(49.2\% to 56.2\%) is a volume measurement and was never in doubt. We keep the
   441	route in the negative column, now on the stronger ground that the highest-leverage
   442	knob the mechanism argues for moves the arm along the front rather than over it,
   443	and we record the correction here rather than in a footnote because it is the
   444	one place in this paper where a re-run changed a conclusion's reasoning rather
   445	than its precision.

   448	SGLang's \texttt{token\_usage} subtracts evictable blocks, so a parked
   449	session's prefix is genuinely not counted, and charging for it reserves
   450	capacity that really will be needed back. The end-to-end measurement agrees

   453	\subsection{Route 2: the growth reserve}
   454	
   455	Sessions grow by a median of 2.7K new tokens per round (\texttt{input\_len}
   456	over rounds $\ge 1$ in the trace), so the controller reserves
   457	capacity for the next \texttt{horizon} rounds. We swept the horizon over
   458	$\{0,1,2,3\}$ and the reserve is not a free parameter to be minimised:
   459	Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon} shows the response is non-monotone. Removing the
   460	reserve entirely costs 12.6\,pp of SLO, and a reserve of two rounds is
   461	15 minutes \emph{slower} than three. The optimum is at one round, and there it comes closest to the
   462	static front---0.93\,pp below it, inside the tie band---and that single point is
   463	the one this paper spends the most words on, because it does not reproduce: the
   464	repeat of this configuration lands 5.6\,pp below the line
   465	(\S\ref{sec:results}). Read the sweep as a shape, not as a result at its
   466	minimum.

   479	\subsection{An independent adaptive controller}
   480	\label{sec:results:aimd}
   481	
   482	Every controller above is ours, which invites the objection that the negative
   483	result is a statement about one design rather than about adaptive admission.
   484	We therefore implemented a second, mechanistically different controller: an
   485	AIMD window over an agent-level admission set, following the control law of
   486	the congestion-based system we compare against in \S\ref{sec:related}. The
   487	window grows additively while cache occupancy sits below a low watermark, is
   488	cut multiplicatively when occupancy exceeds a high watermark \emph{and} the
   489	prefix hit rate falls below a threshold, and holds otherwise. Cuts pause the
   490	youngest sessions at their next step boundary; growth resumes paused sessions
   491	first. Every input is a runtime gauge---occupancy and hit rate from the
   492	engine, nothing from the trace---and the window acts through the same named
   493	permits as \sys{}.
   494	
   495	It does not beat the static front either, at either parameter setting
   496	(Table~\ref{tab:runs}). At the values reported in that paper's evaluation it
   497	finishes in 364.3 minutes at 81.4\% SLO, $11.2$\,pp below the envelope; the
   498	same arm with its retuned watermarks finishes in 264.6 minutes at 52.8\%,
   499	$34.3$\,pp below (\texttt{table1.csv}). The retuned setting is also one of the most
   500	volatile runs we report: its segment-to-segment SD is 13.2\,pp of SLO, with
   501	segment values swinging between 37 and 72. Two runs are more volatile
   502	(\texttt{cap4} at 15.8 and the all-throttles-off arm at 14.4), and no other arm
   503	that keeps its throttles on is as bad---which is what a control loop oscillating
   504	around a capacity cliff looks like.
   505	
   506	We report two things honestly about this comparison. First, this is our
   507	reimplementation of a published control law, not the authors' system, and a
   508	reimplementation of any controller can underperform its paper. We built it to
   509	the published law, gave it the same permit interface and the same 2\,s cycle
   510	as \sys{}, and did not tune it beyond the two documented settings; but the
   511	caveat stands. Second, both controllers stall. At its paper parameters this one pinned its
   512	window at one session, admitted nothing, and left the engine idle for over an
   513	hour. Our own controller does the same thing more briefly: in 4 of the 11
   514	\sys{} runs a scan finds windows in which the engine was idle, 85 or more
   515	sessions were waiting, the SLO valve was open and the controller issued no
   516	permit at all. Three of those runs have a single such window each---170.5, 173.2
   517	and 191.5\,s, 0.9--1.1\% of the run's wall clock---and the arm that disables
   518	every throttle has nine of them, together 12\% of its wall clock (\texttt{stall.csv} states
   519	the criterion; an earlier draft quoted 150--175\,s and 0.9\% for all four,
   520	which no version of the criterion reproduces). The remaining runs, including
   521	the best one, show no such window. Nothing is logged
   522	as an error and the run recovers on its own.
   523	
   524	We traced one of those windows to a
   525	code path that explains it: single-flight admission considered only the head of
   526	the FIFO queue, and when the head was a session too large to fit the remaining
   527	budget it was never admitted, never left the queue, and blocked every smaller
   528	session behind it. That is the code every run in this paper executed. The fix
   529	in our repository---scan the queue, still admit at most one per cycle---is
   530	\emph{not} in the artifact that produced these results, so the numbers here
   531	predate it and we do not claim them for it. We are also explicit that this is
   532	\emph{a} path, not a proven cause:
   533	the audit is a log signature, and the same signature can be produced by other
   534	closed gates. What we can say is that the signature is present in a third of
   535	our \sys{} runs and absent from all of our static caps, which have no
   536	admission decision to get stuck in.
   537	
   538	That is the honest version of a finding we first reported too strongly. An
   539	intermediate draft of this paper called it a deadlock and said the
   540	configuration that produced our best result was the one that deadlocked on its
   541	repeat. Neither holds: the windows are minutes, not hours, and they clear on their
   542	own, and the configuration that produced our best result never showed one. Nor
   543	do they move any verdict we report---0.9--1.1\% of the wall clock in each of
   544	the three runs that has a single one, and in the all-throttles-off arm 12\% of
   545	a wall clock that is already the slowest in the table. We keep them because the mechanism is real and its size
   546	is the point: on a pool that holds four median sessions, a controller's failure
   547	mode is a silent stall, not graceful degradation, and it took a repeat run to
   548	see it.

   550	\subsection{Route 4: execution granularity}
   551	\label{sec:results:gate}
   552	
   553	The first three routes all assume that what a controller can set is \emph{how
   554	many sessions are resident}. A static cap sets the same quantity, so it is
   555	unsurprising that they land in the same place. The fourth route attacks the
   556	assumption instead: because a large fraction of live sessions are parked at
   557	any instant, a cap on \emph{resident} sessions leaves the engine idle whenever
   558	all residents happen to be parked. Capping the \emph{running-request} count
   559	instead should keep the engine busy while leaving the resident count high.
   560	
   561	The permit design supports this directly: a paused session finishes its
   562	current round and blocks before its next one, and resumes when the controller
   563	re-admits it. We implemented it as a gate on the engine's own
   564	\texttt{num\_running\_reqs} and swept the gate over $\{2,4\}$. Only the
   565	gate-of-four arm ran to 998 of the trace's 999 steps; the gate-of-two arm
   566	reached 770 and is not used, so what we report below is a single comparison
   567	against the no-gate arm rather than a sweep.
   568	
   569	It does not work, and the measurement says why. Against the no-gate arm, a
   570	gate of four moved mean running requests from 1.33 to 1.30 and mean live
   571	sessions from 3.25 to 3.03: it tightened both, by 2\% and 7\%, rather than
   572	pinning the one it aims at. That is what we
   573	observe, and we state it as the observation it is rather than deriving a bound
   574	we cannot support. A live session spends most of its time parked in a tool wait
   575	(the ratio of mean running requests to mean live sessions is 0.33 to 0.64
   576	across the twenty-three arms, \texttt{occupancy.csv}'s \texttt{running\_mean}
   577	over \texttt{runs.csv}'s \texttt{conc}), so pausing live sessions removes
   578	capacity that was not running in the first place---but that ratio is an average over a run, not a
   579	bound on any instant, and the running count can in principle reach the live
   580	count. Both repairs fail: without a
   581	constraint on admission, a paused session is re-admitted on the next cycle and
   582	blocks for at most one 2\,s cycle; with one, the policy is a session cap again.
   583	The obstacle is granularity---\texttt{paused} takes effect at round
   584	boundaries, while pinning an instantaneous count requires control the
   585	permit mechanism does not have.
   586	
   587	The measured outcome matches: the gate moved running concurrency from 1.33 to
   588	1.30 and live sessions from 3.25 to 3.03. It tightened rather than pinned, and
   589	a tightening is another cap.

    97	family; the location is. We report the point estimates and not a threshold, because a $\pm10\%$

   100	measure is no steadier ($+0.45$, $-1.25$, $+0.73$). And the controller never
   101	operates where the branch turns expensive: 1.22, below 1.29 and below the main
   102	trace's 1.53 alike.

     8	stronger one, a trace whose load moves mid-run, whose baselines co-tenancy made

   112	\section{Related Work}
   113	\label{sec:related}
   114	
   115	\paragraph{Admission and scheduling for agentic serving.} Recent systems
   116	report large gains from treating an agent session, rather than a request, as
   117	the schedulable unit, and from preserving session state across tool calls:
   118	session-aware admission with CPU-restorable KV
   119	\cite{talaria,continuum}, program-level pause/resume with shortest-context-first
   120	restoration \cite{thunderagent}, and congestion-based control over an
   121	agent-level window \cite{concur}. A recurring measurement in this line is that
   122	recomputation, not admission, dominates: \cite{concur} attributes 49.1\% of
   123	end-to-end latency to recomputation, and \cite{mori} preserves state on the
   124	CPU at the cost of a reload. Our results are consistent with this literature
   125	rather than in tension with it, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} explains why: the
   126	mechanism these systems add is one our controller already has.
   127	
   128	\paragraph{Engine-side techniques.} A separate line improves the engine
   129	rather than the admission decision. Iteration-level batching and paged KV
   130	\cite{yu2022orca,kwon2023pagedattention} are the substrate every policy here runs
   131	on; chunked prefill with stall-free batching \cite{sarathi}, prefill/decode
   132	disaggregation \cite{distserve}, and cache-centric scheduling
   133	\cite{mooncake} are the refinements. Our \S\ref{sec:model} measures 2.6--8.2\,ms of each
   134	27--37\,ms step as work that is not decoding---prefill and non-decode
   135	overhead---which is exactly what this line attacks. We expect these techniques to move the frontier rather than to decide who
   136	sits above it, but we did not run the interaction and so state it as an
   137	expectation.
   138	
   139	\paragraph{State retention and preemption.} A third line keeps session state
   140	alive across the gaps we measure in \S\ref{sec:model}: keeping KV on cheaper
   141	storage across turns \cite{yu2025pensieve,gao2024cachedattention}, migrating it
   142	between instances \cite{sun2024llumnix}, and preempting at iteration granularity
   143	\cite{wu2026fastserve}. These systems exercise finer control than our 2\,s
   144	admission cycle, which is the boundary of our negative result: we falsify one
   145	control granularity, not the family. What they share with \sys{} is that the
   146	state is not discarded---which is why, as we argue below, adding admission
   147	intelligence on top buys nothing here.
   148	
   149	\paragraph{Application-level dependencies and replay.} Serving an LLM
   150	\emph{application} rather than a request means the scheduler sees dependencies
   151	between calls \cite{lin2024parrot}, and agentic traces have structure that
   152	request-level benchmarks do not capture \cite{wang2026xperf}. Our replay
   153	preserves that structure but fixes it in advance, which is exactly the
   154	limitation these works would press on.
   155	
   156	\paragraph{Prefix caching.} The prefix cache is often proposed as the signal
   157	an admission policy should use. What we measure is that the \emph{realized}
   158	reuse is a function of the admission policy and of the host tier, while the
   159	\emph{planned} reuse is a constant of the trace; an earlier draft of this
   160	paper conflated the two and got the direction wrong in both. The trace plans
   161	a round-1-and-later hit rate of 80.9\%. Without HiCache the realized
   162	round-1+ rate follows the plan only at the loosest cap and then collapses:
   163	80.8\%, 78.5\%, 70.6\%, 56.0\%, 32.8\% for caps of one through five. With
   164	HiCache it tracks the plan at every cap we ran: 80.7\%, 79.6\%, 78.7\% for
   165	caps of two, three and four. Our controller sits at 80.6\%.
   166	So the honest statement is not that admission does not move this quantity---%
   167	\texttt{cap4} alone moves it 25 points---but that the host tier is what keeps
   168	it from moving, which is the same mechanism \S\ref{sec:model} and
   169	\S\ref{sec:criterion} keep running into. A configuration without a host tier
   170	that admits four sessions does not have the cache that a host-tier one at the
   171	same concurrency does. All eight numbers in this paragraph are the
   172	\emph{observed} per-step hit rate (\texttt{server\_prefix\_hit\_rate} in the
   173	per-step logs), unweighted mean over rounds $\ge 1$; the planned rate is the
   174	trace constant 80.9\% and does not move with the cap. \S\ref{sec:model} uses
   175	a different convention---all steps, token-weighted, so that reuse and
   176	recomputation partition the prompt---under which the same collapse reads
   177	92.5\%$\to$36.4\% of the trace's bound for the device-only caps and
   178	92.4\%$\to$90.2\% for the static host-tier caps. We state both because the paper's earlier
   179	drafts mixed them. We note the boundary of this claim---we varied admission policy,
   180	not eviction policy, and SGLang exposes alternate eviction policies we did not
   181	test. A characterization of production KV-cache traffic at a large cloud
   182	provider finds that eviction-policy design is ``highly workload-dependent''
   183	and that reuse patterns differ sharply by request category
   184	\cite{wang2025kvcachewild}; that is reason to expect a single trace family to
   185	understate the space, though it is not a policy comparison and we do not
   186	present it as one.

   188	\section{Discussion}
   189	\label{sec:discussion}
   190	
   191	\paragraph{Why published gains do not reappear.} The systems cited above win
   192	by keeping session state alive across tool waits and by pausing at agent
   193	granularity rather than request granularity. \sys{} has both properties by
   194	construction: the permit file gives per-session pause and resume, and the
   195	controller never frees capacity by discarding a session---it admits fewer
   196	sessions instead. To that extent the mechanism these systems add is one our
   197	controller already has, which is one reason adding admission intelligence on
   198	top of it buys nothing here.
   199	
   200	We are careful not to overstate this, because the distinction is between
   201	\emph{choosing} to discard state and \emph{having} it evicted. Retention here
   202	is the engine's radix cache doing its ordinary job; it is not something the
   203	controller implements, and \S\ref{sec:model} measures that the cache evicts
   204	parked prefixes under pressure and that they are recomputed. What \sys{}
   205	avoids is the deliberate trade of state for capacity. A system that manages the
   206	host tier as an explicit policy---as \cite{mori} and \cite{yu2025pensieve}
   207	do---holds a lever we do not, and our negative result does not cover it.
   208	
   209	\paragraph{The threshold decides the verdict, and we report that.} Every SLO
   210	number in this paper counts a request as good if its time-to-first-token is
   211	under 10\,s. That constant sets the whole vertical axis, so we re-derived the
   212	frontier at 5\,s and 20\,s as well, from the same per-request logs. The
   213	ordering of the static caps does not change. What changes is where the
   214	controllers sit relative to them, and at the loose end one of them appears,
   215	in one of its two runs, to cross:

   484	\section{Conclusion}
   485	
   486	We asked whether an adaptive admission controller can beat a tuned static
   487	concurrency cap on a memory-bound agentic LLM workload, and found that on our
   488	system it cannot: nine variants, four routes tested, and of the six whose
   489	measurements are clean, none above the static front. (Four were measured while
   490	other jobs shared the host; we re-measured one, which moved 6.5\,pp closer
   491	without crossing, and the other three are still out.) We expected the reason to be a cancellation---admission policy controls
   492	how many requests are in flight, the per-step cost rises with that number, and
   493	the product is what the wall clock sees---and we built a two-part decomposition
   494	of the wall clock that makes the cancellation measurable. That decomposition
   495	holds, and the cost it measures is real, but it is not the whole reason and on
   496	the configuration our controllers run it is not the binding one: there the
   497	elasticity stays below one across the operating range, so added concurrency was
   498	still cheap and the engine had room. Our controllers did not take it. What we
   499	can say at the end is narrower than what we set out to say: on this hardware and
   500	this workload, the admission rules we could build and tune did not beat a static
   501	cap, the capacity estimate they act on is 1.79$\times$ the truth, and a
   502	structural idle component that no policy we built recovers at a profit remains.
   503	The regime in which adaptivity should have paid---where the same switch is worth
   504	$+51\%$ and $-42\%$---is one where the controller is not dominated by any static
   505	point we measured, and does not dominate one either. The measurement we offer is
   506	a diagnostic an engineer can take before committing to a sweep; whether it
   507	predicts the outcome on another workload is a question we tested once and could
   508	not confirm.
   509	
   510	The part we would carry elsewhere is not the negative result but the
   511	mechanism behind it. Prefix reuse is bounded by the workload---75.5\% of our
   512	prompt tokens---and whether that bound survives added concurrency is set by
   513	the memory tier: device-only, realization falls from 92.5\% to 36.4\% as the
   514	running count rises, and the shortfall reappears as recomputation; with a host
   515	tier the static caps hold at 90.2--92.4\% across the range our controllers
   516	occupy, and the worst of the nineteen host-tier runs that completed this
   517	trace---the one that disables every throttle---still realizes 72.8\%, where the
   518	nearest device-only point, the cap
   519	of four at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.65, manages 60.7\%---while
   520	the device cache misses \emph{more}. Two configurations at the same running
   521	count and the same pool occupancy realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound,
   522	which is why we describe the tier rather than the occupancy as the variable.
   523	That claim is measured on one trace family and one card, its controlled
   524	version is still running, and we do not extend it past what we measured---but
   525	it is the finding a system designer can act on, and it is the one we would
   526	test first on different hardware. Two boundaries come with it. On a second
   527	workload both of our measurements survive in direction and neither in level:
   528	that trace realizes less of its own bound at a lower occupancy, and its
   529	elasticity turns positive at a lower running count---reaching 1.0 there where
   530	the main trace reaches 1.15 at 1.53---so neither an occupancy threshold nor a
   531	concurrency threshold transfers. And the variable-load case, which is where a controller should
   532	have its best chance, is measured but not usable---its baselines were taken
   533	under host co-tenancy and we report it as open rather than as a result.

   142	        the same pool occupancy realize 79.0\% and 91.3\% of the bound, so it
   143	        is not how full the pool is but what happens when it overflows; that
   144	        pair differs in date and GPU as well, so a capacity control that
   145	        varies the pool alone on one card is queued; \S\ref{sec:results}
   146	        reports it when it lands. The per-step elasticity follows the same line:
   147	        it passes one above $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \approx 1.5$
   148	        device-only and stays below one across the controllers' whole
   149	        operating range with a host tier (\S\ref{sec:criterion}). Two
   150	        measurements that do not share a code path---client-side step timing
   151	        and engine-side token accounting---give the same answer. This is the
   152	        finding we would carry to another system first, and it is what the
   153	        title's question turns on.
   154	  \item We test four routes by which an adaptive admission controller was
   155	        expected to beat a tuned static cap on this workload, and we are
   156	        precise about what each one rests on. Two are falsified by measurement
   157	        against criteria we fixed before the runs---changing the accounting
   158	        base (\S\ref{sec:results}) and changing the growth reserve. One is a
   159	        knob rather than a hypothesis, and we report it only because it slides
   160	        the operating point along the front and adds no mechanism. The fourth,
   161	        changing the execution granularity from session count to
   162	        running-request count, is falsified by measurement \emph{and} by an
   163	        argument about why the permit mechanism cannot express the control it
   164	        would need. We do not call the last two of these falsifications of a
   165	        stated hypothesis, because they were not.
   166	  \item We give a two-part decomposition of the wall clock (\S\ref{sec:model})
   167	        that locates the cancellation: admission policy moves the
   168	        running-request count, and the per-step cost moves with it. On the
   169	        device-only configurations we can account for that rise token for
   170	        token---evicted prefixes are recomputed almost one for one. On
   171	        host-tier configurations recomputation is flat and the account has to
   172	        come from elsewhere; we report the part of that residual we were able
   173	        to attribute, and the part we were not (\S\ref{sec:model},
   174	        \S\ref{sec:repro}).
   175	  \item We reduce the question to one measurable number, the elasticity of
   176	        per-step cost with respect to the running count (\S\ref{sec:criterion}),
   177	        and measure it at $0.38$ on the model-consistent step cost and $0.61$
   178	        on the per-request one, on the configurations whose mechanism we can
   179	        account for token for token. We offer it as a diagnostic rather than a
   180	        predictor: it says where on the curve a policy operates and how much
   181	        the remaining headroom is worth, and \S\ref{sec:criterion} is explicit
   182	        about the sense in which it is circular and the sense in which it is
   183	        not.
   184	  \item We characterize when adaptivity \emph{could} have paid, and show that
   185	        our workload is not that case: the same throttle configuration that is
   186	        worth $+51\%$ at 25 sessions costs $-42\%$ at 200 (\S\ref{sec:load}).
   187	        Put differently, no single scalar cap is optimal across both, which is
   188	        the regime adaptive control exists for. On the low-load tier, where
   189	        that argument bites hardest, the controller is \emph{not} worse---%
   190	        same-generation measurements put it on the front, $+0.9$\,pp above the
   191	        line and inside the noise. On the high-load tier it is dominated. That
   192	        asymmetry, not a blanket failure, is what this paper measures.
   193	  \item We make the measurements reproducible: the front and each controller's
   194	        signed distance are produced by one script from the raw per-step logs,
   195	        the tables and the sub-thousand-step runs by two others, all named in
   196	        \S\ref{sec:repro}. We report the six claims we had to retract along the
   197	        way, with the checks that caught them.
   198	\end{itemize}

     4	\subsection{Workload}
     5	
     6	We replay 200 coding-agent sessions drawn from the {\sc TraceLab} corpus
     7	\cite{zhu2026tracelab}. Each session is a
     8	sequence of rounds; a round presents a prompt of median 23{,}187 tokens
     9	(p90 51{,}064; maximum 89{,}436, which alone is 88\% of the KV pool), and
    10	between rounds the session waits on a tool before returning with a longer
    11	context. Arrivals are Poisson at $\lambda=0.04\,\text{s}^{-1}$ over an
    12	80-minute window, giving 999 steps in total. Two sessions of maximum size
    13	cannot be co-resident: their combined contexts (89{,}436 and 87{,}821 tokens)
    14	exceed the pool by 75\%.
    15	
    16	The tool wait is the load's defining property and the one most easily
    17	misread. Its \emph{median} is 0.35\,s, which invites the conclusion that
    18	sessions never leave the engine. Its \emph{mean} is 16.85\,s measured and 15.82\,s
    19	demanded, and the maximum is 300\,s; 21.8\% of the
    20	tool waits the trace specifies exceed ten seconds---18.0\% of the 799 round
    21	transitions, 14.4\% of its 999 steps---and 18.6\% of the measured gaps do. We verified that our replay
    22	harness honors the trace faithfully: across 799 round transitions the measured
    23	gap is 16.85\,s mean against a demanded 15.82\,s. On a typical transition the
    24	harness adds nothing---the median of the per-transition measured/demanded
    25	ratios is 1.001---while the ratio of the two means is 1.065, the difference
    26	sitting in the tail. The corpus's own characterization makes the same point
    27	qualitatively---it reports
    28	``diverse and heavily-tailed tool calls'' and calls these gaps
    29	``human-paced''---but gives no numeric mean \cite{zhu2026tracelab}, so the
    30	16.85\,s above is our measurement over the released trace, not a figure we
    31	quote from that paper.

    33	\subsection{Engine and the binding constraint}
    34	
    35	We use SGLang v0.5.10 \cite{zheng2024sglang} with Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct
    36	\cite{qwen2coder} extended to 96K context,
    37	on one RTX 3090 (24\,GB). KV is paged \cite{kwon2023pagedattention}; at \texttt{mem-fraction-static} 0.85
    38	the pool is 101{,}432 tokens. Hierarchical caching (HiCache), with a host tier of twice the device pool, is
    39	enabled on the configurations marked \emph{+HC} and on every \sys{} run; the
    40	five plain static caps run without it. We keep both because the host tier turns
    41	out to change the mechanism (\S\ref{sec:model}). Every run is guarded: if the pool does not
    42	come out at $101{,}432 \pm 20$---which happens when another tenant holds
    43	memory on the card---the run is aborted rather than recorded, because SGLang
    44	sizes the pool from free memory at startup and a shrunken pool silently
    45	produces incomparable numbers.
    46	
    47	The relevant consequence is that the pool holds only about four median
    48	sessions. Concurrency is therefore capacity-bound, not scheduler-bound, and
    49	this is the premise on which everything below rests.
    50	
    51	\subsection{The controller under test}
    52	
    53	\sys{} is an admission controller that reads engine telemetry and issues
    54	\emph{named} permits rather than a scalar cap: a session is admitted by
    55	appearing in a permit file, and the runner refuses to start a round for a
    56	session that is not listed. This design choice matters for the comparison,
    57	because it is what allows per-session actions at all---including the pause
    58	primitive we exploit in \S\ref{sec:results:gate}. It also means that a session,
    59	once admitted, holds its permit for its entire lifetime, so the controller
    60	never frees capacity by discarding a session's state---it admits fewer sessions
    61	instead. That is a statement about the \emph{policy}, not about the memory: the
    62	engine's radix cache still evicts parked prefixes under pressure, and we
    63	measure that it does and that they are recomputed (\S\ref{sec:model}). We
    64	return to this distinction in \S\ref{sec:discussion}, because it bounds how far
    65	our negative result generalises.
    66	
    67	Each control cycle (2\,s) the controller computes
    68	\begin{equation}
    69	  \Pi \;=\; \max(\rho, \textstyle\sum_i c_i) \;+\; \textstyle\sum_i g_i ,
    70	  \label{eq:proj}
    71	\end{equation}
    72	where $\rho$ is the engine's reported occupancy, $c_i$ the context length of
    73	live session $i$, and $g_i$ its forecast growth over the next
    74	\texttt{horizon} rounds. A session is admitted when $\Pi + n \le B$, with $n$
    75	the candidate's estimated need and $B$ the budget. The three terms are
    76	respectively the measured physical occupancy, a reservation for sessions
    77	currently parked in a tool wait (whose KV is evictable and therefore absent
    78	from $\rho$), and a reservation for future growth.
    79	
    80	\subsection{Baselines and the verdict criterion}
    81	
    82	Baselines are static caps: at most $N$ sessions resident, $N \in \{1,\dots,5\}$,
    83	with and without HiCache. We compare on two axes: end-to-end wall clock
    84	(time from the first submission to the last completion) and SLO attainment
    85	(fraction of requests whose time-to-first-token is under 10\,s).
    86	
    87	The verdict criterion deserves care, because ``faster than cap-$N$'' is not
    88	the question. The question is whether a policy reaches a point \emph{above}
    89	the static front. A point that lies merely between two static caps is not a
    90	win: mixing cap-$N$ and cap-$M$ by time slice approximates any point on the
    91	line between them. We flag this segment argument as an argument, not a
    92	measurement---we did not run the time-sliced policies, and carrying queue state
    93	and cache contents across a switch could bend the line. It is the criterion we
    94	apply, and it is the stricter of the two we could have used---stricter than
    95	comparing each controller against the discrete static points alone, because the
    96	line sits above them. We state the direction plainly rather than claim it as a
    97	caution: this choice makes our negative result \emph{easier} to reach, not
    98	harder. Our best arm is $0.55$\,pp below the line and would be $+4.4$\,pp
    99	against the nearest static anchor below it in wall clock (\texttt{cap3}+HiCache's
   100	repeat, 81.9\% at 230.3 minutes; $+5.2$ would be the comparison against that
   101	anchor's first run at 81.1\% and 236.4 minutes, which is the one it displaced).
   102	The arm we repeated most tightly is a different one---\texttt{horizon}=1, at
   103	$0.93$\,pp below the line and $+3.7$\,pp against the same first run---and we
   104	name both because ``best'' depends on which of the two the reader means. So we take the
   105	upper-left
   106	envelope of the static points and report each controller's signed distance
   107	above or below it. We classify a signed distance of more than $+1.0$\,pp as
   108	above the front, less than $-1.0$\,pp as below it, and the band between the
   109	two as \emph{on} it---a tie, not a win. The band is a convention we fixed
   110	before running anything, and we report it here because every verdict in this
   111	paper depends on it. Our later replicate measurements (\S\ref{sec:results})
   112	bracket it rather than justify it: the SLO spread of configurations we ran
   113	more than once ranges from 0.5\,pp to 5.1\,pp, so for some configurations
   114	the band is generous and for others it is far too narrow. We flag this as a
   115	weakness of the criterion, not a defence of it. One further property of the
   116	envelope deserves flagging, because
   117	three of the nine controller variants finish slower than 295 minutes. Its
   118	right-hand segment runs from \texttt{cap2}+HiCache at 295 minutes to
   119	\texttt{cap1} at 527, and \texttt{cap1} is the only anchor there that runs
   120	\emph{without} HiCache. Those three---\texttt{hz2},
   121	\texttt{breshz1} and \texttt{foyfix}---are therefore judged against an
   122	interpolation between one host-tier point and one device-only point. If the
   123	envelope is drawn from the host-tier anchors alone, that segment does not exist
   124	and those three cannot be adjudicated at all; drawn from device-only anchors
   125	alone, they sit further below. We report the conservative reading and note that
   126	the honest statement about those three is \emph{below the front as we defined
   127	it}, not \emph{below every static configuration}. A fourth arm,
   128	\texttt{bres}, sat in the same position in an earlier version of this
   129	paper; its re-measured value is 265.9 minutes, which puts it back inside the
   130	device-only range, so the class has three members and not four. Two of the
   131	three
   132	(\texttt{hz2}, \texttt{breshz1}) were also measured under host
   133	co-tenancy and are marked $^\ddagger$ in Table~\ref{tab:runs}; whether they
   134	are slower than 295 minutes at all is one of the things the re-runs will
   135	settle. The front and every
   136	controller's signed distance are computed by one script from the raw per-step
   137	logs; the table generator computes the distance for the four runs that did not
   138	complete all 999 steps---both are named in \S\ref{sec:repro}---and that
   139	script's own first version was wrong: it extrapolated past the end of the
   140	envelope and declared the slowest static cap a winner. We fixed it and report
   141	the episode because it is the failure mode this criterion is most prone to.

    84	\subsection{What determines busy time}
    85	
    86	During busy time the engine executes steps; each step produces one token per
    87	running request. Hence
    88	\begin{equation}
    89	  W_{\text{busy}} \;\approx\; \frac{G}{\overline{R}}\,\tau ,
    90	  \label{eq:busy}
    91	\end{equation}
    92	with $G$ the total generated tokens (fixed by the trace at 706{,}010) and
    93	$\tau$ the per-step cost. Across our runs $\tau$ is not a fit parameter we
    94	tuned; we obtain it two independent ways, and reporting both is what shows
    95	they are measuring different things rather than confirming each other.
    96	
    97	\paragraph{Method 1 (derived).} $\tau_1 = W_{\text{busy}} \cdot \overline{R} /
    98	G$. Two choices in that expression are easy to get wrong and both matter. The
    99	time term must be the \emph{busy} component, not the wall clock---using the
   100	wall clock charges idle time to the steps. And $\overline{R}$ must be the mean
   101	running count \emph{during busy time}, not the mean over the whole run: the
   102	whole-run mean includes the idle samples, where the count is zero, so it equals
   103	$\overline{R}_{\text{busy}} \times$ (busy fraction). Using it makes $\tau_1$
   104	too low by exactly the busy fraction---so the estimate is short by the
   105	\emph{idle} fraction, 36.3\% for \texttt{cap1}, whose busy share is 63.7\%. We report both columns in the data, and use the
   106	busy-conditional mean throughout; the whole-run mean is what an earlier
   107	revision of this paper used, and it made the two methods agree far better than
   108	they do.
   109	
   110	\paragraph{Method 2 (independent).} For each step record we compute the
   111	per-request decode interval,
   112	$(\text{total\_duration} - \text{first\_token}) / (\text{output\_len} - 1)$,
   113	and take the median over steps with $\text{output\_len} \ge 32$.
   114	
   115	The two do not agree exactly, and the disagreement is informative rather than
   116	embarrassing. $\tau_1$ exceeds $\tau_2$ on every run we accept, by 7.7--39.7\%
   117	(\texttt{decomp.csv}, column \texttt{excess\_pct}), which in
   118	absolute terms is 2.6--8.2\,ms of a 27--37\,ms step. (The one run we exclude
   119	from the reuse accounting, the \texttt{horizon}=1 repeat, reads 66.3\% here
   120	against its original's 18.5\%. The anomaly sits in the engine's token counters
   121	and in everything derived from them, so that is the scope of the exclusion:
   122	this run's $\tau$ columns, its reuse and recomputation are unusable, while its
   123	wall clock and SLO---which come from the client---are ones we use, and it
   124	appears in Table~\ref{tab:runs} and in the repeat spread of
   125	\S\ref{sec:results} on those two columns alone.) The gap is
   126	\emph{consistent with} engine steps that are prefill and
   127	therefore emit no output token---we can bound it, not identify it, because
   128	subtracting a median from a mean does not isolate a component. All we can form
   129	from these two is their ratio: the excess $\tau_1/\tau_2 - 1$ runs
   130	17.7--39.7\% across the five device-only configurations. Two things follow, and
   131	we separate them. First, it is a ratio of \emph{times}, not of steps, and we
   132	have no measurement of the fraction of step \emph{counts} that are prefill---
   133	chunked prefill makes those two different numbers. Second, even as a time
   134	ratio it is the gap between two measurements, not a decomposition of one: it
   135	is consistent with prefill and non-decode work filling it, but we cannot rule
   136	out that some of the difference is the two estimators disagreeing for other
   137	reasons. So the two are not
   138	two estimates of one number; they are measurements of two different things,
   139	and we do not size a component with their difference. Fig.~\ref{fig:steptime} shows both, run by run. Method 2 remains the one that does not depend on the
   140	quantity it is used to explain, and it is the one we quote when the question
   141	is the cost of a decode step. The engine's own latency counters
   142	(\texttt{itl\_p50}, \texttt{e2e\_p50}, \texttt{ttft\_p50}) are exported as zero
   143	by SGLang v0.5.10 in our configuration, so no directly measured step time
   144	exists in our data; instrumenting this is future work.

   161	\subsection{The per-step cost is not constant in concurrency}
   162	
   163	Equation~\ref{eq:busy} would be uninteresting if $\tau$ were a constant. It is
   164	not. Across the thirteen runs we fitted---the five device-only caps, the three
   165	host-tier caps that span the same range, and the five controller arms we report
   166	individually---$\tau_1$ ranges from 27.0 to 36.7\,ms and $\tau_2$
   167	from 20.6 to 33.1\,ms, and both rise with the running count: fitting
   168	Eq.~\ref{eq:elasticity} to the five device-only configurations gives
   169	$\varepsilon = 0.38$ for $\tau_1$ and $0.61$ for $\tau_2$
   170	($R^2 = 0.89$ for the latter, Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}).
   171	
   172	The relation is a trend, not a law. Two host-tier configurations with similar
   173	running counts behave differently: \texttt{cap4\_hc} runs 1.73 requests at
   174	29.8\,ms while \texttt{F:hz0} runs 1.66 at 35.7\,ms. Running count sets a
   175	floor and a trend, not a value---and on the host-tier family, as we show below,
   176	it does not set the value at all.
   177	
   178	This is the crux of the paper, and we state it at the strength the arithmetic
   179	supports. Hold $\tau_1$ at its value for \texttt{cap1}, 28.8\,ms, and busy
   180	time falls as $1/\overline{R}$: raising the running count from 1.00 to 1.84
   181	should have cut busy time by a factor of 1.84, from 340 minutes to 184. The
   182	measured curve flattens and lands at 234. The rise in $\tau$ takes back 50 of
   183	the 156 minutes that the extra concurrency would otherwise have bought---\emph{a

   411	\subsection{Why idle time exists, and why recovering it does not pay}
   412	
   413	The length of an individual idle stretch is a property of the workload, not of
   414	the controller. A session in a
   415	tool wait holds its permit and its KV but issues no request; with only about
   416	two live sessions, the probability that all of them are simultaneously waiting
   417	is substantial. The direct evidence is the duration of the stretches
   418	themselves: they are of the same order as the tool waits, and an order of
   419	magnitude longer than the 2\,s control cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:idle}). No
   420	controller can shorten a gap that the tool set.
   421	
   422	How much idle a \emph{run} accumulates is a different matter, and there the
   423	controller is not off the hook. Idle accumulates when every live session
   424	happens to be parked at once, and how many sessions are live is precisely what
   425	an admission policy sets. Two configurations with the same mean running count
   426	make this visible: \texttt{horizon}=1 (1.52) and \texttt{cap3}+HiCache (1.51)
   427	idle 36.7 and 24.1 minutes respectively---a 52\% difference at matched
   428	concurrency---and 12.6 of the 19.4-minute wall-clock gap between them is that
   429	idle difference rather than step cost. So the gap durations are the workload's,
   430	and the number of gaps is partly the policy's.
   431	
   432	We note one claim we had to drop here. An earlier draft reported that idle
   433	fraction falls monotonically with mean concurrency. With the full data it does
   434	not: on the busy-conditional measure this section uses throughout,
   435	\texttt{F:hz0} at $\overline{R}_{\text{busy}}$ 1.67 idles 15.2\% while
   436	\texttt{cap4} at 1.65 idles 10.4\%---more concurrency and \emph{more} idle,
   437	the reverse of the reported trend. (The two arms read 1.41 and 1.48 on the
   438	all-samples mean, where the ordering happens to run the other way;
   439	\texttt{arm\_running.csv} carries both conventions side by side, and we name
   440	the one each claim uses.) The weaker statement that survives is the one we use---%
   441	the stretches are structural and long.
   442	
   443	The obvious remedy---keep more sessions live so that someone is always
   444	ready---is what \texttt{bres} does, and the clean measurement of it says the
   445	remedy works and does not pay. \texttt{bres} cuts idle time by 9.5 minutes
   446	against its own baseline, and its busy time falls as well, by 15.5---so the
   447	arm simply finishes 25.4 minutes earlier. What it does not do is improve the
   448	criterion: its SLO falls 3.9\,pp and its distance below the static front is
   449	unchanged (\S\ref{sec:results}). Recovering idle time is not the same act as
   450	buying a better operating point, and here the one does not produce the other.
   451	We are reporting a correction in this paragraph, not just a refinement: an
   452	earlier draft had idle falling while busy rose, leaving the run slower, and
   453	that shape came from a measurement we have since found was taken under host
   454	co-tenancy (\S\ref{sec:repro}). Idle is recoverable; it is recoverable at a
   455	price that does not move the verdict.
   456	The structural remedy would be to make the engine do work during a wait, but
   457	there is no work to do: the session's next round does not exist until its tool
   458	returns.
